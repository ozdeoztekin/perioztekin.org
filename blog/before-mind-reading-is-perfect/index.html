Before Mind Reading Is Perfect: What BCIs Can Actually Do

I started with the question everyone asks first: can a machine read a mind? The evidence gives a more useful answer than yes or no. Current systems can decode selected meanings, movements, gestures, and speech intentions under specific conditions, but they still depend on training, task structure, signal quality, and often cooperation. That technical reality matters because it keeps the conversation honest.

Why I wrote this

The question is not magic. It is measurement.

Brain-computer interfaces are easy to overstate. One version sounds miraculous: a person who cannot speak or move uses a neural device to communicate again. Another version sounds terrifying: a machine silently extracts private thoughts. The research I read is more specific than either story.

The useful technical question is what kind of signal is being measured, what the system is trained to decode, how much calibration it needs, and whether the person is cooperating with the task. Those details decide whether a result is a medical tool, a lab demonstration, a consumer interface, or an overclaimed mind-reading story.

Semantic decoding

The famous fMRI result recovered meaning, not exact thoughts.

The study that first changed my thinking was Tang et al. (2023). The researchers used fMRI responses and a language model to reconstruct the meaning of stories people heard, imagined, or watched silently. The decoder often recovered the gist, but it did not reproduce every word perfectly, and it was trained separately for each participant using many hours of that person’s brain responses (Tang et al., 2023).

The limits are not footnotes. The system worked best when the participant cooperated, it did not transfer cleanly across people, and participants could interfere with decoding by deliberately thinking about something else. Later work on semantic decoding across participants and stimulus types makes the research direction more powerful, but still does not turn it into universal mind reading (Tang & Huth, 2025).

Clinical BCIs

The strongest near-term case is assistive communication and control.

Clinical BCI work is moving because the goal is concrete: restore communication, cursor control, or device interaction for people with severe motor or speech impairment. Neuralink has registered PRIME and VOICE studies, Synchron is testing an implanted endovascular BCI in the INTENT trial, and Paradromics has registered a Connexus BCI study for communication restoration (ClinicalTrials.gov, PRIME; ClinicalTrials.gov, VOICE; ClinicalTrials.gov, INTENT; ClinicalTrials.gov, Connexus).

Recent peer-reviewed work shows why this matters. Long-term independent use of an intracortical BCI has supported speech and cursor control at home, and a separate system synthesized voice from attempted speech with very low delay (Card et al., 2026; Wairagkar et al., 2025). The technology is not reading random private thoughts. It is decoding trained, task-relevant intention for a patient-centered purpose.

Non-invasive paths

Outside the implant world, signals get easier to collect but harder to interpret.

Non-invasive systems are attractive because they avoid surgery, but the signal problem is harder. A 2026 Nature Neuroscience study decoded typed sentences from non-invasive brain activity, with MEG performing far better than EEG; the result is impressive, but it also shows that sensor quality and experimental setup still matter (Lévy et al., 2026).

There is also a different path: peripheral interfaces. A Reality Labs paper described a wrist-based sEMG interface that reads neuromotor signals for gestures and handwriting, and Meta has publicly described a Neural Band that uses wrist signals to control AI glasses (Kaifosh et al., 2025; Meta, 2025). Apple Vision Pro, meanwhile, treats eye and hand input as central to interaction and says pre-interaction gaze is processed privately on device (Apple, n.d.).

Technical limits

The constraints are part of the evidence.

Across these studies, the same constraints keep returning: training data, calibration, participant cooperation, task design, sensor quality, and the difference between decoding a structured intention and discovering an unstructured private thought. A speech BCI can be extraordinary because it helps a person express intended speech. That does not make it a general-purpose thought scanner.

Those constraints are not reasons to ignore the field. They are reasons to describe it precisely. The real technical story is not that mind reading has arrived. It is that machines are getting better at translating selected neural, muscular, and behavioral signals into usable outputs when the task is well-defined.

What I take from it

BCIs are becoming practical before they become perfect.

That is the main lesson I took from the technical sources. The most important systems are not trying to read every thought. They are trying to restore speech, movement, control, or communication. At the same time, the spread of neural and neuromotor interfaces makes it harder to pretend that cognitive data is only a future problem.

This post is the technical side of the question. The policy side is separate: once a system can infer attention, emotion, intention, vulnerability, or neurological condition, what should the law do with that inference? I take that up in The Mental-State Inference Loophole.
