Policy note · AP Seminar · Mental privacy

The Mental-State Inference Loophole

My AP Seminar question is whether the United States should codify statutory protection for mental privacy that reaches inferences drawn from non-neural data. My answer is yes, but carefully: the legal trigger should be the mental-state inference, not only the sensor. A rule that protects brain signals while ignoring eye movement, typing cadence, facial expression, heart rate, app behavior, or other cognitive biometrics leaves the most scalable privacy problem outside the frame.

AP Seminar question

The legal problem is inference, not just electrodes.

The research question I am working with is: to what extent should the United States codify statutory protection for mental privacy that reaches inferences drawn from non-neural data? The reason the question matters is that consumer technologies can infer mental states from signals that do not look like brain data at all: eye movement, typing cadence, facial expression, heart rate, sleep, app behavior, location, and attention patterns.

Magee, Ienca, and Farahany describe this broader category as cognitive biometrics: data that can be processed to infer cognitive, affective, or conative states (Magee et al., 2024). That framing changed the policy question for me. If a company can infer stress, attention, vulnerability, or intention from non-neural data, then a neural-data-only statute can protect the doorway while leaving the side entrance open.

The evidence problem

Imperfect decoding is not a reason to wait.

The technical evidence is not perfect mind reading, and that matters. Tang et al. used fMRI and a language model to reconstruct the meaning of language people heard, imagined, or watched silently. The system required participant-specific training and cooperation, and it often recovered gist rather than exact words (Tang et al., 2023). Chandler also warns that mental-state inference is a chain of interpretation, not a transparent window into the mind (Chandler, 2025).

Those limits do not eliminate the legal problem. They define it. An inaccurate inference can still harm someone if a school, employer, insurer, platform, or agency treats it as objective. An accurate inference can also be sensitive if it exposes a private mental state the person never chose to disclose. That is why I do not think the law should wait for perfect decoding.

State-law gap

The first state statutes are important, but they are too sensor-centered.

Colorado and California show that U.S. privacy law can be updated for neurotechnology. Colorado’s HB24-1058 brought neural data into its consumer privacy framework, and California’s SB 1223 added neural data to sensitive personal information (Colorado General Assembly, 2024; California Legislature, 2024). These are real gains.

The problem is that the next generation of mental privacy risk may come from non-neural inputs. Montana’s neurotechnology definition excludes several bodily measures, including pupil dilation, motor activity, and breathing rate, while Connecticut’s proposal centers data generated by the central or peripheral nervous system (Montana Code; Connecticut General Assembly, 2025). A statute can therefore look modern while still missing cognitive inferences produced from non-neural data.

What to change

The statute should follow the inference.

My current policy answer has three parts. First, Colorado-style language should be modified so protection is not limited by an identification-purpose frame when the real harm is mental-state inference. Second, the United States should create a federal definition keyed to the inference drawn, not only to the sensor used. Third, non-neural carve-outs should be eliminated where they allow companies to infer intimate mental states from eyes, muscles, breathing, behavior, or other bodily signals while avoiding neuroprivacy rules.

That does not mean every fitness metric or typing pattern should be treated like a medical brain scan. The legal standard should be risk-sensitive. It should ask whether the data, alone or combined with other data, is used to infer cognition, emotion, attention, intention, vulnerability, neurological condition, or psychological state, especially in high-stakes contexts such as education, employment, insurance, healthcare, advertising, policing, or access to essential services.

Federalism

A national floor would solve the patchwork problem.

State experimentation is useful, but mental-state data does not stay inside state borders. A patchwork of different definitions can leave people protected in one jurisdiction and exposed in another. The introduced MIND Act is useful because it recognizes the need to study neural data and related biometric, physiological, and behavioral data that may reveal cognitive, emotional, psychological, or neurological states (U.S. Congress, 2025). But a study bill is not the same as a binding privacy floor.

That is why I favor federal statutory protection rather than relying only on state-by-state reform. A federal rule could set minimum protections while allowing states to go further. It would also give companies, schools, medical providers, and courts a clearer definition of mental-state inference than a patchwork of sensor-specific statutes.

Rights design

Statutory protection is more practical than a sweeping new constitutional right.

Some scholars argue for constitutional protection of mental privacy and freedom of thought; Tomain, for example, develops a constitutional route for the digital age (Tomain, 2025). Istace’s rights framework pushed me toward a more cautious conclusion: new rights can be symbolically powerful, but derived rights and statutory translation may be clearer and more enforceable (Istace, 2025).

In the United States, a statute also handles a major practical problem: many mental-privacy risks come from private actors. A constitutional claim can run into state-action limits, while a statute can directly regulate companies, platforms, employers, schools, insurers, and data brokers. For this project, I am not arguing for a broad new fundamental rights category. I am arguing for limited statutory protection that gives existing privacy, autonomy, and freedom-of-thought principles a workable rule.

Agency limits

HIPAA, FDA, and FTC authority do not fully cover the conduct.

Existing federal regimes cover pieces of the problem, but not the whole pattern. HIPAA is strongest in covered healthcare relationships, not in ordinary consumer technology (HHS, n.d.). FDA authority is tied to medical-device regulation and includes software exclusions under the 21st Century Cures Act framework, which means general wellness or non-medical products can fall outside the core medical-device pathway (21 U.S.C. § 360j(o)). The FTC can police unfair or deceptive practices, but after-the-fact enforcement is not the same as a clear statutory rule for mental-state inference (FTC, n.d.).

The regulatory target should therefore be conduct, not hardware. The question should not only be whether a device is implanted, medical, or explicitly neural. It should be whether the actor is collecting or using data to make sensitive mental-state inferences, and whether the person has meaningful notice, consent, limits on use, security, access, deletion, and protection from high-stakes misuse.

International signals

Other legal systems already point beyond raw neural data.

International examples are not templates that the United States can copy wholesale, but they help show what the conceptual move looks like. Chile’s neurorights law protects brain activity and information derived from it, which is important because it recognizes that the protected interest includes derived information, not only raw signals (Chile Ley 21.383). UNESCO’s Recommendation on the Ethics of Neurotechnology also treats neurotechnology governance as a question about neural data, indirect data, dignity, autonomy, and mental privacy (UNESCO, 2025).

The European Union’s AI Act is also relevant because it restricts certain emotion-recognition uses in workplace and education settings (European Union, 2024). That does not solve the U.S. problem, but it supports the idea that mental-state inference can be regulated by use context, not only by the physical sensor.

My current answer

Protect mental privacy without freezing useful technology.

My answer is not that every cognitive signal should be locked down. It is that U.S. law should codify a limited statutory protection for high-risk mental-state inferences from both neural and non-neural data. The rule should include opt-in consent, purpose limits, data minimization, security duties, access and deletion rights, limits on sale and targeted advertising, and stronger restrictions in schools, workplaces, insurance, healthcare, and law enforcement.

The goal is not to scare people away from assistive neurotechnology. It is to make trust possible. A person who needs a BCI to communicate should not have to wonder whether the same ecosystem is quietly building a profile of their private mental life. And a person using ordinary consumer technology should not lose mental privacy simply because the inference came from eyes, hands, heart rate, behavior, or attention rather than from a brain electrode.
