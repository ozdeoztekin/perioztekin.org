About me

Why neuroscience

I couldn’t help wondering not just what happens, but why the body and mind bother. Why does a neuron fire at all? Why does a brain, damaged and disoriented, sometimes find its way back?

Chemistry gave me a molecular language: electrons shifting, bonds breaking, energy moving through a system looking for equilibrium. Biology showed me how living systems are built from that same restlessness. Physics gave me the forces underneath both. Every thread I pulled kept pointing me back to the same place. The brain.

Last summer the question stopped being abstract. At Stanford’s CNI-X I worked on A.R.A.I.A., an app concept for people with aphasia. The project framed the problem as a disconnect between what someone wants to say and what they can communicate: intelligence and understanding may remain, while speaking and expression become difficult. That made the design question feel urgent: how can technology support frequent, structured practice without pretending to replace clinicians?

What I keep coming back to is this: the brain’s failing and its coming back are the same plasticity, just running in different directions. I want to study both.

Capstone project

A.R.A.I.A.
aphasia recovery AI

An AI-supported app concept for people rebuilding communication after stroke-related aphasia. The prototype focused on speech and conversation exercises, AI-recommended lessons, speech-recognition feedback, progress tracking, and therapist/caregiver support. It began as my six-person team capstone at Stanford Medicine’s CNI-X 2025, and it’s the project I most want to take further.
