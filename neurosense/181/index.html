The Sunk Cost Exit

sunk cost bias

The hardest plan to quit is the one you already suffered for.

The Science

  • Sunk cost bias pulls decisions toward whatever already consumed time, money, or energy.
  • Past effort can look like evidence that continuing is the responsible choice.
  • Clear decisions need to separate past cost from future value.

The Protocol

  • Set aside the time already spent and ask what you would choose from here.
  • Write the next cost of continuing in plain terms.
  • Decide from the next hour, week, or outcome instead of the old receipt.

See it in real life

Example

The turntable that jammed twice

Four actors are standing on the turntable when it stops. It groans a quarter turn and sticks, with the kitchen still half facing the seats. At Monday’s tech rehearsal it happens twice, and the scene change takes almost three minutes when the director wants it under thirty seconds.

Avery designed that turntable, a round platform that spins the set, and built it over six Saturdays with $60 of her own money. The musical opens on Friday. Her plan is late nights all week and a $45 bearing shipped overnight. She’s too upset to decide well tonight, so she makes one rule, that nothing new goes into it until tomorrow, and the bearing stays in her cart.

Six Saturdays feel like a reason to keep going, because quitting would waste them. That’s sunk cost bias, where whatever already took your time, money and energy pulls the decision toward more of the same, and past effort starts to feel like a duty to finish. But the Saturdays are spent either way. What’s still open is what the next four days buy.

At lunch on Tuesday she writes out the receipt, everything it has cost, which is six Saturdays, $60 and one splinter, on the back of a cue sheet, and she folds it under. Then she asks what she would build if she were starting fresh today. She writes the next cost in plain words, three more late nights, $45 and the chance it still sticks on Friday. She decides based on Friday, when the kitchen has to turn into the street in under thirty seconds, and two rolling platforms can do that, built on the turntable’s own casters.

At Wednesday’s dress rehearsal the change takes 24 seconds on the stage manager’s stopwatch, and on opening night, in blue light, it takes 22. Nobody in the audience knows there was supposed to be a turntable.

  2. What changes itShe folds the receipt under, writes the next cost and decides from Friday.

Try it yourself · 10 minutes

The ticket question

You’ll need four to six people, like family, friends or teammates on a bus, and something to write on. Each person hears only one version.

  1. Split your people into two groups. Read version A to one group and version B to the other, one person at a time if you can.
  2. Ask each person whether they’d choose the show or the friend’s plan, and mark the answer under their version.
  3. Count how many picked the show in each version.
  4. Then read both versions to yourself and notice which one tugs harder toward the show.
Version AYou paid $40 of your own money for a ticket to a show on Saturday night. That afternoon, a friend invites you to something free that you’d enjoy more, at the same time. The ticket can’t be refunded, sold or given away. Which do you go to?
Version BYou won a free ticket to a show on Saturday night. That afternoon, a friend invites you to something free that you’d enjoy more, at the same time. The ticket can’t be sold or given away. Which do you go to?

It’s the same evening with the same two choices, and the only difference is money that’s already gone.

What you’ll probably notice. In both versions the evening ahead is identical, so if only the future counted, the answers would come out the same. If version A gets more votes for the show, that’s the $40 pulling on a decision about the future, the same pull Avery felt from six Saturdays. A handful of people can go either way, but a lot of people feel the tug when they read version A themselves.

Step by step

Make the from-here call

Use it on something you keep going with mostly because of what it already cost you.

  1. Fold the receipt under. Write down what you’ve already put in, like hours, money and effort, and then fold the paper so you can’t see it. Ask whether, starting today and knowing what you know now, you would choose this.
  2. Write the next cost in plain words. Skip “it’ll be hard” and write something like “three more late nights, $45, and it might still not work.”
  3. Decide based on what comes next. Name what you’re really deciding for, like the next hour, the next week or the show on Friday, and pick the option that serves it best.
  4. Wait a day if you’re upset, and add nothing. If emotions are running hot, put the final call off until tomorrow, and in the meantime, put in no new money, hours or sign-ups.
  5. Keep what it taught you. Write one thing the old plan taught you, like a skill, a part or a person to ask, and carry it into the next one.

How to tell it’s working. Each time you use it, write down the decision, and a week later add one line about how that week went. After a few, look back, and if your from-here choices usually left you with more time or less dread than the old plan was heading for, it’s working.

When it gets hard. The receipt sneaks back in every time you think about how much you already put in. When it does, write that thought on the folded-under half and ask the from-here question again.
