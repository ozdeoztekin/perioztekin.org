Why You Both Think You're Right

The Temporoparietal Junction

An argument gets stuck because each of you is sure your own view is just obvious. That stuck feeling is not stubbornness. It is a skill that has gone quiet.

The Science

  • Saxe and Kanwisher (2003), NeuroImage: in brain scans, the temporoparietal junction responded most when adults read about what a character believed or wanted.
  • Seeing their side takes effort; skip it, and your own view can feel like the entire truth.
  • So the fight stops being about the problem and starts being about who is right.
  • Feeling stuck and sure is a skill gone quiet, not stubbornness.

The Protocol

  • Run a quick point-of-view check before you react.
  • Ask one real question: what does this look like from their side?
  • Say it in three parts: what I know, what I am assuming, what they might be seeing.
  • Notice the signal, name the mechanism, and change one input before autopilot.

See it in real life

Example

Two tickets and a seventieth birthday

The two tickets have been in Lucas’s phone since June, ninety dollars of summer-job money for Saturday night. It’s the band he and Reuben have played on every bus ride since eighth grade, and when he bought them he texted Reuben “got us tickets” and nothing else.

At lunch on Thursday, Reuben says he can’t go, because his grandma’s seventieth birthday dinner moved to Saturday. Lucas hears him bailing, and Reuben hears Lucas making a scene about a family party. They leave lunch in opposite directions, and that night Lucas types “forget it, I’ll take someone who actually shows up” with his thumb over send.

Near the back of the brain, an area called the temporoparietal junction keeps track of the fact that other people know and want different things than you do. That takes effort. Without that effort, your own view can feel like the whole truth, and a fight stops being about Saturday and turns into a contest over who’s right.

He deletes the text and asks himself one real question, which is what this looks like from Reuben’s side. Then he calls and says it in three parts. “What I know is you can’t come, and I paid for both tickets. What I’m assuming is you think that’s no big deal. What you might be seeing is me making a scene about your grandma.” Reuben goes quiet. “Wait, you paid? I thought your cousin got them free.”

Back in May, Lucas had said his cousin at the venue might get extras, and when that fell through he never mentioned it. One sentence in his story changes, from “he’s bailing on me” to “he thought it cost me nothing.” Reuben finds a buyer at face value within the hour, and on Saturday, between songs, Lucas gets a video of Reuben’s grandma blowing out her candles, with the message “tell them to play the slow one.”

  2. What changes itHe asks himself one real question, then says it in three parts on a call.
  3. What happens nextReuben thought the tickets were free, and he finds a buyer within the hour.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

The forehead E

You’ll need a finger, a pen and a scrap of paper. Do the first step before you read the rest.

  1. With one finger, quickly trace a capital E on your forehead without planning it.
  2. Now work out which way it faced, and whether it would read correctly to you from the inside or to someone standing in front of you.
  3. Think of a disagreement that’s still bugging you, and write the one sentence you’d use to tell the story, like “She ignored what I said.”
  4. Under it, write three short lines, one for what you know from what you saw or heard, one for what you’re assuming and one for what they might be seeing.
  5. Read your first sentence again, and rewrite any part of it that no longer fits.

What you’ll probably notice. If your E reads correctly to you, it’s backward for everyone facing you, and if it reads correctly to them, you had to flip it in your head first. Either way, seeing it from the other side took a separate step that you had to choose. The three lines are that same step, slowed down, and if even one word of your first sentence changed, you put their view next to yours.

Step by step

Check the other map first

Save it for the next time you feel completely sure the other person is wrong.

  1. Catch the sure feeling. When your next line is already loaded and you’re certain they’re being impossible on purpose, treat that certainty as the signal and stop before you send it or say it.
  2. Ask one real question. Ask yourself what this looks like from their side, as a question you don’t know the answer to, not as a setup for your next point.
  3. Say it in three parts. Say what you know from what you saw or heard, what you’re assuming and what they might be seeing. Say it out loud, so they can fix the part you got wrong.
  4. Put their map next to yours. You don’t have to agree with them. You’re making sure you’re both arguing about the same problem, with the same facts on the table.
  5. Check whether a sentence changed. Afterward, tell yourself the story in one line. If at least one sentence is different from before, the check did its job, even if nothing is settled yet.

How to tell it’s working. For the next three disagreements, write your one-line version of the story before the check and again after it. If the line after keeps coming out different, and the two of you get back to the problem itself sooner, the check is worth keeping.

When it gets hard. In the moment, the three parts can sound like a script, and the other person may roll their eyes. Keep the words plain and short, and if you’re too angry to mean the question, say you need ten minutes and come back to it.
