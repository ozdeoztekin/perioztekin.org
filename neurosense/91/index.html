Why You Both Think You're Right

The Temporoparietal Junction

An argument gets stuck because each of you is sure your own view is just obvious. That stuck feeling is not stubbornness. It is a skill that has gone quiet.

The Science

  • Saxe and Kanwisher (2003), NeuroImage: the temporoparietal junction is a specialized hub for theory of mind, tracking that others know and want different things.
  • When that hub goes quiet, the brain treats your own view as the entire truth.
  • So the fight stops being about the problem and starts being about who is right.
  • Feeling stuck and sure is a skill gone quiet, not stubbornness.

The Protocol

  • Run a quick point-of-view check before you react.
  • Ask one real question: what does this look like from their side?
  • Say it in three parts: what I know, what I am assuming, what they might be seeing.
  • Notice the signal, name the mechanism, and change one input before autopilot.

See it in real life

Example

Two tickets and a seventieth birthday

The two tickets have been in Lucas’s phone since June, ninety dollars of summer-job money for Saturday night. It’s the band he and Andre have played on every bus ride since eighth grade. He texted Andre “got us tickets,” nothing else.

At lunch Thursday, Andre says he can’t go: his grandma’s 70th birthday dinner moved to Saturday. Lucas hears bailing; Andre hears Lucas making a scene about a family party. They leave lunch in opposite directions, and that night Lucas types “forget it, I’ll take someone who actually shows up,” his thumb over send.

Near the back of the brain, the temporoparietal junction tracks the fact that other people know and want different things. That takes effort. When it goes quiet, your own view feels like the entire truth, and the fight stops being about Saturday and turns into who’s right.

He deletes the text and asks himself one real question: what does this look like from Andre’s side? Then he calls and says it in three parts. “What I know is you can’t come, and I paid for both tickets. What I’m assuming is you think that’s no big deal. What you might be seeing is me making a scene about your grandma.” Andre goes quiet. “Wait. You paid? I thought your cousin got them free.”

In May he’d said his cousin at the venue might get extras. They fell through, and he never mentioned it. One sentence in his story changes: “he’s bailing on me” becomes “he thought it cost me nothing.” Andre finds a buyer at face value within the hour, and on Saturday, between songs, Lucas gets a video of Andre’s grandma blowing out candles: “tell them to play the slow one.”

  2. What changes itOne question to himself, then three parts out loud on a call
  3. What happens nextAndre thought the tickets were free, then finds a buyer within the hour

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

The forehead E

You need a finger, a pen and a scrap of paper. Do step 1 before you read the rest.

  1. With one finger, quickly trace a capital E on your forehead. Don’t plan it.
  2. Now work out which way it faced. Would it read correctly to you, from the inside, or to someone standing in front of you?
  3. Think of a disagreement that’s still bugging you. Write the one sentence you’d use to tell the story, like “She ignored what I said.”
  4. Under it, write three short lines: what I know (only what I saw or heard), what I’m assuming, and what they might be seeing.
  5. Read your first sentence again, and rewrite any part of it that no longer fits.

What you’ll probably notice. If your E reads right to you, it’s backward for everyone facing you, and if it reads right to them, you had to flip it in your head first. Either way, seeing it from the other side took a separate step that you had to choose. The three lines are that same step, slowed down. If even one word of your first sentence changed, you put their map next to yours.

Step by step

Check the other map first

Use it the next time you feel completely sure the other person is wrong.

  1. Catch the sure feeling. When your next line is already loaded and you’re certain they’re being impossible on purpose, treat that certainty as the signal. Stop before you send it or say it.
  2. Ask one real question. What does this look like from their side? Ask it as a question you don’t know the answer to, not as a setup for your next point.
  3. Say it in three parts. What I know (only what you saw or heard), what I’m assuming, and what you might be seeing. Say it out loud, so they can fix the part you got wrong.
  4. Put their map next to yours. You don’t have to agree with them. You’re making sure you’re both arguing about the same problem, with the same facts on the table.
  5. Check whether a sentence changed. Afterward, tell yourself the story in one line. If at least one sentence is different from before, the check did its job, even if nothing is settled yet.

How to tell it’s working. For the next three disagreements, write your one-line version of the story before the check and again after it. If the after line keeps coming out different, and the two of you get back to the actual problem sooner, it’s working.

When it gets hard. In the moment, the three parts can sound like a script, and the other person may roll their eyes. Keep the words plain and short. If you’re too angry to mean the question, say you need ten minutes, and come back to it.
