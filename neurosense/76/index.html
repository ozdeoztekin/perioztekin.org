Why Smell Can Pull Up a Memory

Olfaction, Smell & Memory

One smell can bring back a whole scene before you are ready. That is because smell is tied closely to emotion and memory systems.

The Science

  • Herz (Brain Sciences, 2016): other senses pass through the thalamus before reaching the cortex, but smell is processed first in the amygdala and hippocampal area, the brain's emotion and memory hub.
  • Herz et al. (Neuropsychologia, 2004): in a small brain-imaging study of five women, the smell of a perfume tied to a personal memory activated the amygdala and hippocampal regions more than a photo of the same perfume, and felt more emotional.
  • That anatomy is why a hallway, hoodie, soap, or snack can feel like a time machine.
  • The memory may feel vivid even if it is incomplete.

The Protocol

  • Use scent as a cue, not a magic key: pick one safe, mild smell for one specific state.
  • Use it while studying, calming down, or starting a routine.
  • Repeat it enough times that your brain learns the association.
  • Then use the same scent later as a reminder cue.

See it in real life

Example

Back on the block, one year later

The smell gets to Rocco before the pool does. He pushes through the lobby doors for the league championship, the warm chlorine air hits his face, and suddenly it’s last year and he’s on the block: the long whistle, his twitch before the horn, the official’s raised hand, the walk past the bleachers. Disqualified for a false start, in this same pool.

His stomach drops before he’s even out of his parka. Strangely, a lot of the memory is missing. He can’t remember which lane he had or who won the heat. The horn and that raised hand are perfect, though.

Smell takes a shortcut. Odor signals travel more directly into the brain’s olfactory and limbic regions than sights or sounds do, and those regions sit right next to the amygdala and hippocampus, which handle feeling and memory. So a smell can arrive with the feeling already attached and pull up a whole scene before he’s ready, vivid and patchy at once. The same wiring can be put to work on purpose.

First he steps back out to the lobby for a minute, where the air smells like floor wax. The feeling is information, not an order. Then the part he’s practiced. For three weeks, before every set of starts at practice, he has peeled a clementine, held the peel to his nose and run the same routine: four arm swings, goggles on, two slow breaths, eyes on the black line. Behind the blocks today, he opens a sandwich bag of peel from this morning and runs it again.

On the block he stays still through “take your mark” and goes on the horn. A clean start. He finishes fourth in his heat, and the official’s hand stays down.

  1. Where it stallsChlorine air, and he’s back at last year’s false start
  2. What changes itA minute in the lobby, then the peel and his practiced routine
  3. What happens nextStill through “take your mark,” a clean start, the hand stays down

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

The smell drawer

You need three or four mild smells from around the house, like cinnamon, an orange, a bar of soap, a crayon or sunscreen, plus paper and a pen.

  1. Close your eyes and have someone hand you one smell, or pick one without looking. Breathe in gently.
  2. Write down the first memory or place that comes to mind within about ten seconds, even if it’s only a feeling.
  3. Do the same with the others.
  4. For each memory, write what you know for sure: the year, who was there, what happened just before.
  5. Put away any smell that brings up something painful. Never sniff cleaning products, markers or sprays.

What you’ll probably notice. Something usually arrives within seconds, often a feeling first and details after. When you try to pin down the facts, gaps show up: the memory felt vivid, but it’s patchy. That’s The Science above in miniature. Smell has a short route to the regions that tag memories with feeling, so the feeling can land whole even when the details are thin.

Step by step

Teach a smell one job

Pick the moment first, then the smell.

  1. Pick one mild, safe smell. A citrus peel, a mint tea bag, a plain lip balm, a cedar pencil. Something gentle that doesn’t give you a headache, and not one already tied to an old memory.
  2. Give it one job. Choose one state: starting practice, starting homework, calming down before you perform. That smell is only for that.
  3. Pair them every time. Smell it right before the routine, then do the routine the same way. Do it several times a week for two or three weeks, so your brain can learn the link.
  4. Bring it to the real moment. Carry it in a small bag. Before the race, the test or the performance, smell it and run the routine you paired it with.
  5. Step away from painful smells. If a smell pulls up something that hurts, step out for a minute and breathe somewhere else. The feeling is information, not an order.

How to tell it’s working. Time how long it takes you to settle into the routine at the start of the first week and again in the third. If it’s getting quicker, or the smell starts to bring the routine to mind by itself, the cue is working.

When it gets hard. If the smell shows up everywhere, it stops meaning one thing. Keep it for its one job, sealed in a bag or jar between uses.

Good to know. If certain smells keep bringing back something painful, or the memories feel overwhelming, talk with a parent, a counselor or a doctor. Skip any scent you’re allergic or sensitive to.
