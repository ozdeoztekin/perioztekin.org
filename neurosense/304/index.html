Tired but Unable to Sleep Most Nights? What to Do Next

Orexin, Insomnia & Getting Help

Waking is actively supported by brain networks, so tiredness alone does not show what keeps you awake.

The Science

  • One contributor is orexin, released by neurons in the hypothalamus; it reaches several wake-supporting neuron groups, including histamine neurons.
  • Orexin binds receptors on these neurons and moves their voltage toward firing, so they send more wake-supporting signals.
  • Learned arousal can also keep you awake: bed becomes linked with worry and trying hard to sleep, so getting into bed can trigger more alertness.

The Protocol

  • Tell a parent or trusted adult that the problem keeps returning and affects your day, and ask for help arranging an assessment.
  • Bring a simple record of bedtime, estimated time asleep, waking, night awakenings, caffeine and daytime effects.
  • Ask a clinician whether age-adapted cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia fits; do not cut sleep or pick medication yourself.

See it in real life

Example

Fourteen nights in a small notebook

At Saturday’s orienteering practice in the park, Maelle lined up the wrong end of the compass needle and led her scout patrol the opposite way, a slip she never used to make. With the next meet three weeks off, she keeps missing things she knows.

Since early October she has gone to bed worn out most nights and lain awake an hour or more. She has slept through her first alarm twice and snapped at Mila over nothing. Going to bed earlier only gave her longer to worry, so she figures she’s doing sleep wrong.

A brain doesn’t fall asleep merely because it runs low on energy, since waking is actively held up. Neurons in the hypothalamus release orexin, a messenger that reaches wake-supporting neurons such as histamine neurons, and in rat brain tissue it pushed them toward firing. Feeling exhausted can’t show Maelle her orexin level, and other processes can keep someone up, like weeks of worrying and trying hard in bed, which can tie the bed to alertness. Working out which applies to her takes an assessment, not more effort.

On Sunday she tells her dad she can’t sleep most nights, that it has been five weeks and that school and scouting are slipping, and asks for help seeing a doctor. He books the first opening, two weeks away. Each morning until then she notes in a small notebook roughly when she went to bed, fell asleep and woke, any night waking and a line about the day before. The caffeine column stays empty.

With Maelle’s dad there, the doctor reads all fourteen nights. By Maelle’s estimates, ten include an hour or more awake, beside notes about compass slips and a nap in the car. When she asks whether CBT-I adapted for teenagers might fit, the doctor says it could, explains that it’s a series of sessions with a trained therapist and refers her to one at the clinic. They leave with the first visit booked.

  2. What changes itShe tells her dad and keeps a two-week record for the doctor.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

Three nights, two numbers

Do this in the daytime with a pencil and the back of an envelope. It looks back at nights that already happened, so nothing about tonight changes.

  1. Make a small table with a row for each of the last three nights you remember well.
  2. In the first column, rate how tired you were when you got into bed, from 0 for not tired at all to 10 for completely worn out.
  3. In the second column, write your best guess at how long falling asleep took. Memory is enough, so leave any app or tracker out of it.
  4. Circle your most tired night and check whether it was also your quickest.
  5. Beside each night, add a word or two about what was on your mind when the light went out.

What you’ll probably notice. If your most tired night was also your quickest, tiredness and sleep lined up on those nights. If it wasn’t, you’ve caught the two coming apart, the way they do for Maelle. Waking is actively held up by brain systems, among them one that uses orexin, a messenger that pushes wake-supporting neurons such as histamine neurons toward firing, and a bed that has become linked with worry can switch alertness on as well. A table like this can’t say which of these is at work. If tired but wakeful nights keep coming back, that question belongs with an adult and an assessment.

Step by step

Take the pattern to a doctor

For trouble falling or staying asleep that keeps coming back and is making your days harder.

  1. Take a repeating pattern seriously. If you’re tired but struggle to sleep most nights, and getting up, concentrating or your mood is suffering, that deserves more than trying harder on your own.
  2. Tell a parent, caregiver or trusted adult. Say that it keeps coming back and how it’s affecting your days. A sentence like “It’s been most nights for a month, and I’m slipping in class” gives them enough to act on.
  3. Keep a simple record. Each morning, jot down roughly when you went to bed, when you think you fell asleep, when you woke up and any waking in the night. Add any caffeine, and in the evening a few words on how the day went. Rough estimates are fine.
  4. Ask for an assessment, and bring the record. Ask the adult to help you book a visit with a doctor or another clinician. Different processes can keep a person awake, and two weeks of notes show the pattern better than one bad night can.
  5. Ask whether CBT-I could fit. At the visit, ask about cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia, adapted for your age. It’s a structured treatment done over several sessions with a trained clinician, so don’t try to copy it by cutting your sleep or choosing any medicine yourself.

How to tell it’s working. Once you have a week or two of notes, read them with the adult you told and count the nights that took a long time to fall asleep and the days that felt the effects. You’re looking for the parts that repeat, so you can describe them clearly. Keeping the record isn’t a treatment in itself.

When it gets hard. Telling someone can feel like making a fuss over nothing, so it gets put off for another week. If saying it out loud is hard, hand the adult your notebook or a written note instead.

Good to know. This plan gets you to care and doesn’t treat anything. Never cut your sleep or start a medicine or sleep aid on your own, and if daytime sleepiness makes something like riding a bike unsafe, or worry or low mood feels heavy most days, tell an adult right away rather than waiting for the appointment.
