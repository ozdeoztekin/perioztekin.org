The Math Behind Your Mind.

The Nernst Potential

The Nernst potential is the exact voltage where electrical and chemical forces on a single ion cancel out. Your neurons live perpetually just shy of that balance.

The Science

  • Nernst (Zeitschrift für Physikalische Chemie, 1889): the Nernst equation predicts the equilibrium potential for any single ion species across a semi-permeable membrane. At that voltage, the electrical driving force on the ion exactly cancels the chemical (concentration) driving force, and net flow stops.
  • For typical neuronal concentrations, the Nernst potential is approximately +60 mV for Na+, -90 mV for K+, and -65 mV for Cl-. The resting membrane potential of a neuron sits between these values, weighted by the relative permeabilities of each ion species.
  • Goldman (Journal of General Physiology, 1943): the Goldman-Hodgkin-Katz equation generalizes the Nernst equation to multiple ions, predicting actual resting potential as a permeability-weighted average. This formalism is the quantitative bridge between ion biophysics and cellular electrophysiology.
  • Three closely related terms: the Nernst potential is what the equation gives for one ion; the equilibrium potential is the voltage at which that ion has no net flow, the same value; and the reversal potential is the measured voltage at which a channel's current changes direction, which matches the equilibrium potential only when the channel passes a single kind of ion.

The Protocol

  • Name the two pushes for any ion: concentration pushes one way and charge pulls the other; the Nernst potential is the voltage where they cancel.
  • Draw the number line: plus 60 for sodium, minus 65 for chloride, minus 90 for potassium, and shade the stretch where a resting neuron sits.
  • Remember the weighting: a neuron rests closest to the ions that cross its membrane most easily.

See it in real life

Example

Both sides pulling, nothing moving

The sign on Tomas’s table says “Potassium leaks out of your nerve cells.” He volunteers Saturdays at the science museum, at the “Your Body Is Electric” table, and most kids walk past the sign. This week a girl in a dinosaur raincoat reads it aloud and looks up at him. “Then why doesn’t it all leak out?”

He says something about a pump putting it back. She asks why the leaking stops, and he says “Great question” and hands her a sticker. It bothers him all week.

The answer is a tug-of-war. Every ion feels two pushes. Potassium is more concentrated inside the cell, so the difference pushes it out. But each one that leaves takes a plus charge with it, so the inside grows more negative and pulls potassium back. At about minus 90 millivolts the two cancel, and the net flow stops, though both pushes are still there. That balance point is potassium’s Nernst potential, also called its equilibrium potential. Sodium’s is about plus 60 and chloride’s about minus 65, and a resting neuron sits somewhere between them, pulled toward the ions that cross its membrane most easily.

A week later he comes back with a jump rope. When a kid stops at the sign, Tomas hands over one end: the kid is the crowd pushing potassium out, and he’s the negative charge pulling it back. They pull against each other, Tomas matching the kid’s strength, until the middle of the rope holds still. “Still pulling?” “Yes.” “Still moving?” “No.” That, he tells them, is minus 90.

By closing he has done the rope 23 times. On the last one, a boy turns to his dad before Tomas can say anything. “It stops because it’s a tie.”

  1. Where it stalls“Then why doesn’t it all leak out?” and a sticker instead of an answer
  2. What changes itA jump rope: both sides pulling, the middle holding still
  3. What happens nextTwenty-three rope pulls, and a boy explains it to his dad

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

The hanging bag

A balance point you can measure. You need a rubber band or hair tie, a sandwich bag, a paper clip, four spoons, a ruler and a drawer knob or hook.

  1. Loop the rubber band over the knob and hook the empty bag onto it with the paper clip. Measure from the floor to the bottom of the bag.
  2. Put two spoons in the bag and let it drop, bob and settle. Measure again.
  3. Add the other two spoons and measure where it settles now.
  4. While the bag hangs still, list everything that is still pulling on it.

What you’ll probably notice. The bag stops moving, but nothing has stopped pulling: the spoons still pull down, and the stretched band pulls up just as hard. That’s an equilibrium, two forces that cancel. For potassium, the spoons are the concentration pushing it out and the band is the growing negative charge pulling it back, and at about minus 90 millivolts the two cancel. Add spoons and the band has to stretch farther before the pulls match, the way a bigger push needs a bigger voltage to balance it.

Step by step

Find each ion’s balance point

About fifteen minutes today, two minutes tomorrow.

  1. Name the two pushes. For any ion, write the concentration push on one side and the electrical pull on the other. The balance point is the voltage where they cancel.
  2. Draw the number line. Mark plus 60 for sodium, minus 65 for chloride and minus 90 for potassium. Shade the stretch between them: that’s where a resting neuron sits.
  3. Add the weighting. Under the line, write one sentence: a neuron rests closest to the ions that cross its membrane most easily.
  4. Learn the three names. Nernst potential: the equation’s answer for one ion. Equilibrium potential: the same idea in measurements. Reversal potential: the measured voltage where the current through a channel changes direction.
  5. Do the towel tug with someone. Each of you holds one end of a towel and pulls gently until it holds still. Say which of you is the concentration and which is the charge, and what is still happening when nothing moves.

How to tell it’s working. Tomorrow, with no notes, draw the number line with all three ions and shade where a resting neuron sits. Then explain, using the words push and pull, why potassium stops leaking at minus 90.

When it gets hard. If the signs flip in your head, start with potassium: it leaks out, so the inside has to turn negative to hold it back. That’s why its number is a minus.
