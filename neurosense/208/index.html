Why Rubbing a Bump Makes It Hurt Less

Gate Control Theory of Pain

Rubbing a bump feels like soothing the skin, but it can change how your spinal cord handles pain signals.

The Science

  • A bang activates nociceptors, sensors for potentially damaging pressure. Their signals get shaped in the spinal cord, then become pain in the brain.
  • Touch has its own fibers, switched on by light stroking, and in the spinal cord they connect with the pain fibers.
  • In gate control theory, rubbing switches touch fibers on, triggering a chemical messenger that quiets pain fibers, so fewer signals reach the brain.

The Protocol

  • For a minor bump on intact skin, rub the spot gently or run water over it.
  • Take any easing as a quieter signal, not as proof the bump is fine; relief is not guaranteed.
  • Get pain checked if it is new, intense, spreading, or from a real injury.

See it in real life

Example

The dresser on the second landing

The rental truck has to be back by six, so Renata and her cousin Teodoro are hauling her dresser up the stairs of the new apartment as fast as they can. At the second landing it swings, and the corner cracks into her knee.

She sits down hard on the step, eyes watering, with her hand already rubbing the spot. “Rub it, you’re fine,” Teodoro says from his end of the dresser. Rubbing does seem to help, and what she wants to know is whether she can keep carrying.

The bang set off nociceptors, sensors that respond to pressure that could do damage. Their signals travel along nerve fibers to the spinal cord, get shaped there and head up to the brain, where they become pain. Touch has its own fibers, switched on by light stroking, and in the spinal cord they connect with the pain fibers. In gate control theory, rubbing switches the touch fibers on, which triggers a chemical messenger that quiets the pain fibers, so fewer pain signals reach the brain. That turns the pain down without doing anything for the knee itself.

So she keeps the strokes light and slow, and after a minute or so the sharp throb settles into a dull ache. A quieter signal says nothing yet about the knee itself, so she doesn’t treat it as an all-clear. The skin isn’t broken, it was a bang rather than a twist, and over the next few minutes the ache stays in one spot and keeps fading. She sends Teodoro for her uncle to take the dresser and carries lamps and boxes for the next hour.

The dresser makes it to her room, and the truck is back in the lot at 5:40. The next morning there’s a purple mark the size of a quarter on her knee, and by the weekend it has turned yellow.

  1. Where it stallsHalfway up the stairs on moving day, a dresser corner cracks her knee.
  2. What changes itShe rubs it lightly, then watches whether the ache stays put and fades.
  3. What happens nextShe carries boxes instead, the truck is back on time, and the bruise fades.

Try it yourself · 2 minutes

Bubbles through a pinched straw

You’ll need a glass half full of water and a bendy straw. It’s a model, not a body, but it shows what rubbing does and what it leaves alone.

  1. Breathe out slowly and steadily through the straw for a few seconds and watch the stream of bubbles. The bubbles stand for the signals from a bump on their way to the brain.
  2. Breathe out the same way again, and halfway through, pinch the middle of the straw partly closed with your other hand. Your pinch is the touch from rubbing, turning the signal down at the spinal cord.
  3. Let go, and watch the stream come back.
  4. Notice that your breath was the same in every round.

What you’ll probably notice. The bubbles thin out while you pinch, even though you’re breathing out as hard as before, and some still get through. Gate control theory has the same shape. Rubbing switches on touch fibers, and in the spinal cord a chemical messenger quiets the pain fibers, so fewer pain signals reach the brain, turning pain down rather than off. Your breath, like the bump, never changed, so an ache that eases still deserves a quick check.

Step by step

After an ordinary bump

This is for minor bumps on unbroken skin, like a knee against a desk or a shin on a bed frame.

  1. Check what kind of bump it is. Look at the skin and think about how it happened. A bang on unbroken skin is what this plan is for, and a cut, a twist or a fall needs more than rubbing.
  2. Rub it lightly or run water over it. Use slow, light strokes over the spot, or hold it under cool running water for a minute or so. Light stroking is what switches on the touch fibers, so there’s no need to press hard.
  3. Read the easing as a quieter signal. If the ache drops, the pain signal got turned down on its way to your brain. That doesn’t tell you yet how the knee or elbow underneath is doing.
  4. Keep an eye on it afterward. Over the next hour, notice whether the ache stays in one spot and keeps fading, or starts to spread or swell. If it spreads, swells or gets worse, stop and tell a parent.
  5. Know when to get it checked. Pain that’s new, intense or spreading, or that comes from a real injury, needs a parent’s attention and a doctor or school nurse, however much rubbing eases it.

How to tell it’s working. Rate the ache from 0 to 10 before rubbing, after a minute and again an hour later. A drop after a minute is the signal turning down, and a number that climbs later is the one to take seriously.

When it gets hard. Squeezing hard can feel like doing more, but light, slow strokes are what the touch fibers respond to, so ease off the pressure.

Good to know. Rubbing can turn pain signals down, and it doesn’t treat an injury. If pain is new, intense or spreading, if the skin is broken, or if it came from a fall or a twist, tell a parent and get it checked by a doctor or school nurse.
