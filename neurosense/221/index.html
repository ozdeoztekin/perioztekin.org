Why Your Phone Makes Car Rides Feel Worse

Motion Sickness & Sensory Conflict

In a moving car, a still screen tells your eyes one story while your inner ear reports another.

The Science

  • Your inner ear's vestibular system senses turns and speed-ups; your brain checks that against your eyes, body position and whether you meant to move.
  • Agreeing reports earn your brain's trust. Conflicting ones confuse it, and that mismatch is generally thought to trigger motion sickness.
  • A phone creates that conflict: your eyes see a still screen while your inner ear reports turns. Moving scenery shows your eyes the same journey.

The Protocol

  • As a passenger, put the screen down when looking at it starts to turn your stomach.
  • Watch the scenery go by, ideally out the front, so your eyes take in the motion your inner ear senses.
  • Start your next ride looking out the window rather than down, and see how you feel.

See it in real life

Example

Halfway through a hand on the hill road

The last time Cormac rode to a comic convention, he spent the first hour on a bench outside the hall with his head between his knees, while his cousin Orla set up their table alone. He had drawn on his tablet the whole way there.

This year he has his own zine to sell, and the doors open at ten. He’s in the passenger seat sketching a sample for their sign when the highway gives way to the hill road, and halfway through a character’s hand his stomach turns over. It puzzles him a little, because when he drives on his learner’s permit he never feels sick at all.

In each inner ear, the vestibular system senses every curve and every burst of speed, and his brain checks that report against his eyes, his body’s sense of position and whether he meant to move. When the reports agree, the brain trusts them. The tablet makes them disagree, since his eyes are on a still drawing while his inner ear reports one curve after another, and that kind of mismatch is generally thought to trigger motion sickness. Behind the wheel his own movements match the motion, which may be why driving feels fine.

He leaves the hand unfinished. He zips the tablet into his backpack and watches the road through the windshield, the yellow line bending ahead and the trees sliding past as the car leans into each curve. Now his eyes are taking in the same trip his inner ear is feeling, and he keeps them there until Orla parks.

By then the queasy feeling has faded, and he carries the box of zines in himself. When the doors open he’s behind the table, and by closing he has drawn fourteen quick sketches for people who stopped by. On the ride home he starts out watching the road, with the tablet zipped in his bag.

Try it yourself · one day

Call the curves

Save this for your next ride as a passenger, in a car or on a bus. You don’t need anything, and the driver stays out of it.

  1. Sit facing forward, close your eyes and keep them closed for a minute or two.
  2. Each time you feel a turn, a speed-up or a slowdown, say which one out loud, like “left,” “faster” or “braking.” If someone is sitting next to you, they can tell you how many you got right.
  3. Open your eyes and watch the road through the front windshield for the next few turns, and notice whether what you feel and what you see now arrive together.
  4. If you start to feel queasy at any point, open your eyes and keep them on the road ahead.

What you’ll probably notice. With your eyes closed you can still feel most turns and most touches of the brakes, which is your inner ear’s vestibular system at work. When you watch the road ahead, your eyes report the same turns at the same moment, so the two reports agree. A phone in your lap would tell your eyes that nothing is moving while your inner ear keeps reporting the curves, and that mismatch is the one generally thought to bring on motion sickness.

Step by step

Ride with your eyes on the road

Try it on your next few rides as a passenger, in a car, a bus or a team van.

  1. Put the screen down at the first sign. As soon as looking at a phone, a tablet or a book starts to turn your stomach, put it away, even halfway through a message. The still screen is what puts your eyes at odds with your inner ear.
  2. Watch the scenery go by. Look out the front windshield if you can see it, or out a side window if you can’t, and let your eyes follow the road and everything passing. That way your eyes take in the same motion your inner ear is sensing.
  3. Pick a seat with a view ahead. When you get to choose, sit where you can see out the front, like the passenger seat or the front of a bus.
  4. Start the next ride looking out. Don’t wait until you feel sick. Begin the ride with the screen in your bag and your eyes on the scenery, and see how you feel when you get there.
  5. Rate each ride afterward. Write down how long the ride was, what you mostly looked at and how your stomach felt from 0 to 10.

How to tell it’s working. After a few rides, compare the ones where you looked down with the ones where you watched the road. If the road-watching rides tend to end with lower numbers, keep the screen in your bag. It often helps, though not on every ride or for everyone.

When it gets hard. A long ride without a screen can feel endless. Save the screen for stops, when the car is standing still, and spend the moving stretches talking, listening to music or watching for landmarks.

Good to know. If you feel sick on almost every ride, or feel dizzy or sick even when you aren’t traveling, tell a parent and get it checked by a doctor.
