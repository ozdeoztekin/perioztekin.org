Give the Movement a Time to Arrive

Metronome Pacing & Movement Timing

A movement that falls apart at speed may be missing a timing target. A steady beat can give it one.

The Science

  • A regular beat gives you a timing reference outside the movement, so instead of guessing each interval, you can prepare for a recurring event.
  • Hearing is tied to movement preparation: regular sounds let the brain predict what comes next, and premotor activity helps prepare the next move.
  • The cerebellum contributes to rhythmic timing and the basal ganglia help regulate movement; with other systems, they help turn a beat into action.

The Protocol

  • Pick a harmless pattern you already know, like alternating two fingers, and match each contact to one click at a manageable pace.
  • Once it is stable, set the beat slightly faster, and lower the pace if the pattern collapses or you cannot keep up.
  • Then try the same sequence without the click to see how it holds up on its own.

See it in real life

Example

The fast song at the sing-along

Every Thursday after school, Amalia plays the upright piano for the sing-along at the senior center. The favorite is the fast song, with an oom-pah left hand, a low note on the beat and a chord after it, and Mabel starts the clapping by the second verse.

At a comfortable pace Amalia’s left hand is fine. When the clapping speeds up, the low notes come early, the chords crowd into them, and by the last chorus the song is a scramble. Last week Magnus stopped singing and laughed, “Are we racing somebody?”

She knows the pattern. What she lacks at speed is a target for when each note should arrive. A regular beat gives a timing reference outside the movement, so instead of guessing every gap, she can get ready for something that keeps coming. Regular sounds let the brain predict what comes next, premotor activity helps prepare the next move before it happens, and the cerebellum and basal ganglia, with other systems, help turn a beat into action. A metronome gives each low note a click to land on, and she can hear whether it comes early or late.

Back home she sets the metronome where the pattern stays even and puts one low note on every click. Once it holds, she nudges it a few clicks faster, and when the chords smear, she drops back to the last clean pace. By Tuesday it holds at the speed Mabel likes. On Wednesday she turns the click off partway through a verse and back on a few bars later. The first time she’s ahead of it, so she plays with it a while longer, and on the next try she’s still with it when it comes back.

On Thursday there’s no metronome on the piano, only the clapping. The low notes stay on the beat through all three verses, and Magnus sings the last chorus without stopping.

  1. Where it stallsWhen the clapping speeds up, her left hand turns the song into a scramble.
  2. What changes itA metronome gives every low note a click to land on.
  3. What happens nextWith no click on Thursday, the song holds through all three verses.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

Two fingers, one click

Use a table and any metronome, such as a free metronome app or website. Nothing here needs more speed than you can manage.

  1. Set the metronome slow, around 60 clicks a minute. Rest your hand on the table and tap your index and middle fingers in turn, one tap on each click.
  2. When ten taps in a row land on the clicks, raise the pace by a few clicks.
  3. Keep raising it a little at a time. When taps start landing early or late, or the two fingers bunch up, go back to the last pace that stayed even.
  4. At that pace, turn the sound off for ten taps, then turn it back on and listen to hear if your taps still land with the click.

What you’ll probably notice. With the click on, you can hear each tap land early, late or right on it, because the beat tells you when the next tap should arrive and gives you something to get ready for. With the sound off, there’s nothing outside the movement to check against, so any drift only shows up when the click comes back. Keeping the pattern even with the beat and keeping it even without the beat are two separate results, and the last step checks the second one.

Step by step

Give each note a click

For a passage or a movement pattern you already partly know that falls apart when you speed up.

  1. Pick a pattern you partly know. Choose something safe that you can already play or do slowly, like a left-hand accompaniment, a scale or a rhythm on a drum pad.
  2. Choose what lands on the click. Pick one part of the movement to put on each click, like the low note of each beat or the first finger of each group.
  3. Start where it stays even. Set the metronome to a pace where the pattern is steady, and play until it holds.
  4. Go a few clicks faster. Once it’s stable, raise the pace slightly. If the pattern collapses or you can’t keep up, go back down to the last pace that worked.
  5. Test it without the click. Turn the sound off partway through, keep going and turn it back on to see if you’re still together. How it goes with the click and how it holds up without it are different results.

How to tell it’s working. Each session, write down two things, the fastest pace that stayed even with the click and whether the passage held when the click went off. Watch the second one, since that’s what you’ll have when there’s no metronome in the room.

When it gets hard. It’s tempting to chase a higher number on the metronome. If the notes stop sounding like the pattern, extra speed isn’t helping, so drop back a few clicks.
