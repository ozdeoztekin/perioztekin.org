The Choice You Never Actually Made

Defaults & Decision Architecture

A default decides what happens if you do nothing, and people tend to stay with what is already in place.

The Science

  • An opt-in form makes joining the extra step and an opt-out form makes leaving the extra step; the options match, but the default differs.
  • People tend to stick with the status quo; one possible reason is that change brings uncertainty, which can feel uncomfortable.
  • Decision architecture arranges a choice so a predictable bias can work for people; enrolling employees automatically can raise retirement sign-ups.

The Protocol

  • Choose one app whose alerts keep interrupting your work.
  • Turn off its nonessential notifications once, in its settings, instead of resolving to ignore each alert.
  • Then leave the setting alone, so doing nothing now keeps the quieter default.

See it in real life

Example

Banners over page 61

“Your crops are ready,” says the banner that slides down over page 61. Anouk’s summer reading for English is a 340-page novel, due the first day of school, and she’s reading it on her phone because the library’s e-book was free and the paper copies had a waiting list. The banners come from a farming game her cousins got her hooked on, and they show up every few minutes.

Every night she promises herself she’ll ignore them. Mostly she does, but every so often she taps one, and twenty minutes later she’s back on the page, hunting for her place. On Tuesday she read eleven pages.

The game came with every alert switched on, and that setting is a default, meaning whatever happens if you do nothing. With alerts on from the start, quiet becomes the extra step, the way an opt-out form includes you unless you choose to withdraw. People tend to stick with what’s already in place, and one possible reason is that change brings uncertainty, which can feel uncomfortable. Anouk knows that part, because she worries about missing the game’s weekend harvest festival. Her nightly promise meant a fresh decision at every banner, and whenever she didn’t make it, the default won.

On Wednesday she opens the game’s settings and not the game. She switches off its notifications, the crops, the energy refills and the gifts from neighbors, which takes about a minute. Then she closes the settings and leaves them alone, so doing nothing now means a quiet page. She’ll visit the farm on purpose, after she reads.

That night she reads from page 61 to page 112 without a single banner. By Sunday she’s past page 200, and she finishes the last chapter on the porch with five days to spare.

  1. Where it stallsGame banners keep sliding over her book, and she reads eleven pages.
  2. What changes itShe turns off the game’s alerts once and leaves the setting alone.
  3. What happens nextShe reads past page 100 that night and finishes with days to spare.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

The settings you never chose

Use your phone, or any tablet or computer you use every day. This round is only for counting, so don’t change anything yet.

  1. Open the notification settings and go down the list of apps one at a time.
  2. For each app, ask whether you ever chose its alert setting yourself or whether it’s still the way it came, and tap the counter for every one you never touched.
  3. Find the app that interrupted you most today and look at what its alerts are set to.
  4. Write that app’s name down somewhere you’ll see it tomorrow, and close the settings.

The timer needs JavaScript. A phone stopwatch works just as well.

What you’ll probably notice. If your count is high, most of what interrupts you was decided by whoever made the apps, and it stays that way for as long as you do nothing. That’s what a default is. The quieter option was there the whole time, a switch away, but leaving things as they are takes no step at all, and people tend to stick with what’s already in place.

Step by step

One switch, then leave it

Start with the app whose alerts pull you away most from work that takes focus.

  1. Choose one app. Pick the one whose alerts interrupt you most during homework, practice or anything else that needs your attention, and start with that one only.
  2. Switch off its nonessential alerts once. Open the app’s settings or your phone’s notification settings and turn off what you don’t need, like rewards, reminders to come back and suggestions. Keep only alerts you’d be sorry to miss.
  3. Leave the setting alone. Don’t switch it back on for a special event or a busy week, so that doing nothing keeps the quieter default.
  4. Visit the app on purpose. Open it yourself when your work is done. That way you decide when to check, and the app doesn’t.
  5. Compare a week later. Count something that matters, like pages read or problems finished, and compare it with a similar evening before the switch. If it helped, pick a second app.

How to tell it’s working. Before you flip the switch, count how many alerts that app sends during one evening of work and how much you get done. A week later, count your work again on a similar evening and compare the two.

When it gets hard. For the first few days you may worry about missing something in the app. Give it a set time after your work, so checking has a place and the switch can stay off.
