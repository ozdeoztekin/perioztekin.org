What If Autism Isn't a Deficit?

Intense World Theory

The Intense World Theory reframes autism: not a deficit of feeling, but a brain whose gain knob is stuck on MAX. Same signals, louder response.

The Science

  • Markram & Markram (Frontiers in Human Neuroscience, 2010): the Intense World Theory, built largely on a rat model, proposes that autism involves local brain circuits that are hyper-reactive and hyper-plastic, so the same stimulus produces a larger neural response.
  • Casanova et al. (Neurology, 2002): in a small post-mortem study, the brains of people on the autism spectrum had narrower, more numerous cortical minicolumns; the authors suggested this could change local connectivity.
  • Robertson & Baron-Cohen (Nature Reviews Neuroscience, 2017): sensory processing differs in autism, including sharper detail perception on some tasks, differences in adaptation and atypical multisensory integration; for many people on the spectrum, everyday input feels more intense.
  • A shutdown in a loud classroom is often overload, not disengagement: when input outpaces processing, disconnecting from the source is a protective response.

The Protocol

  • Name the three loudest 'noises' when overwhelmed (auditory, visual, emotional).
  • Take five quiet minutes: noise-canceling headphones, dim light or a step outside.
  • If someone near you shuts down, give them space; it usually means too much input, not too little feeling.

See it in real life

Example

Five minutes by the shoe counter

The pinsetter on lane 14 is sticking, and Daniel is the only one who has noticed. It’s Devin’s birthday, cosmic bowling at nine, and Daniel picked out the gift two weeks ago. He’s on the autism spectrum, and he catches a lot that other people tune out.

By 9:40 that’s the problem. The speaker above their lane is thumping, the black lights make everyone’s shoes glow, and three of Devin’s cousins are asking him questions at once. He stops answering. When Devin calls him up for his turn, he keeps staring at the ball return.

One idea about autism, the Intense World theory, is that the brain’s local circuits react more strongly, so the same input lands louder. Many people on the spectrum notice quieter sounds than others do, and the noise takes longer to fade into the background. When more comes in than the brain can process, disconnecting is how it protects itself. A shutdown is overload, not losing interest.

Daniel has a plan for this, and it starts with naming things. In his head: the speaker, the lights, the questions. The speaker is the loudest. He holds up five fingers to Devin, their signal, walks out to the bench by the shoe counter, puts on his noise-canceling headphones and closes his eyes. He doesn’t talk or try to figure anything out. Devin keeps the cousins at the lane and skips his turn.

About six minutes later Daniel walks back in with the headphones still on. He bowls the last three frames, picks up a spare in the ninth, and is there when the cake comes out, in time to hand Devin the gift.

  2. What changes itNames the three loudest, then five minutes with headphones on
  3. What happens nextBack for the last three frames, the cake and the gift

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

Turn one thing down

This works anywhere: your room, a kitchen, a bus. You need a pen, paper and a timer, plus headphones or earplugs if you have them.

  1. Sit still for one minute and write down everything you can hear, see and feel on your skin. Keep going after the obvious ones.
  2. Circle the three loudest: one sound, one sight, and one thing on your mind, like a conversation or a worry.
  3. Turn the strongest one down for two minutes: headphones or earplugs, eyes closed, a dimmer light or a quieter room.
  4. Then check the other two. Are they easier to ignore than before?

What you’ll probably notice. One minute of sitting still can fill more lines than you expected, because the brain usually turns a lot of input down without being asked. Lowering the biggest source tends to make the rest easier to handle. For many people on the autism spectrum, that turning down happens more slowly, so a room adds up faster, which is why the plan starts with the loudest input: it’s the easiest one to change.

Step by step

When the room gets too loud

Steps 1 to 4 are for whoever is overloaded; step 5 is for friends.

  1. Name the three loudest. One sound, one sight, one person or feeling. Naming them shows you which one to cut first.
  2. Turn the strongest one down. Noise-canceling headphones, a dim corner, eyes closed or a step outside, for five minutes.
  3. Skip talking and fixing. No explaining and no problem-solving while you’re out. Let it settle first.
  4. Go back when you’re ready. You decide when, and it can take more than five minutes. Keeping the headphones on when you go back is fine.
  5. If you’re the friend, give space. A shutdown means too much is coming in. Keep others from crowding, hold the questions, and save their turn.

How to tell it’s working. Each time you use it, notice how long it takes before you can rejoin, and whether the other two inputs feel easier once the loudest is down. Over a few weeks, see if you start catching it earlier, before talking gets hard.

When it gets hard. Stepping away can feel rude in the moment, so set it up beforehand. Agree on a signal with one friend, like five fingers, so nobody has to explain anything when it happens.

Good to know. Autism looks different from person to person, and this is one theory about it. If overload happens a lot or gets in the way of school or friendships, a doctor, a school counselor or another professional who knows you can help build a plan that fits.
