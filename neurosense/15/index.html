Your Brain Has No Volume Knob

The Action Potential

Passive ion gradients can't explain how a neuron sends a signal. The action potential is the brain's solution: a millisecond, all-or-nothing electrical explosion.

The Science

  • Hodgkin & Huxley (Journal of Physiology, 1952): when membrane voltage at the axon hillock crosses the threshold (about -55 mV), voltage-gated Na+ channels open en masse. Na+ floods inward down its electrochemical gradient, driving the membrane to about +30 mV in roughly 1 millisecond.
  • Voltage-gated K+ channels then open with a delay, letting K+ flow out and bring the voltage back down, briefly dipping below rest. For a moment afterward, the sodium channels that just opened cannot open again; this refractory period stops the signal from running backward.
  • The Hodgkin-Huxley mathematical model reproduces the action potential's shape, threshold, refractory period and conduction speed using sodium and potassium conductances that change with voltage and time, plus a small leak. Hodgkin and Huxley shared the 1963 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for this work.
  • The action potential is all-or-none: subthreshold inputs decay passively; once threshold is crossed, the response amplitude is fixed. Information is encoded in firing rate and timing patterns, not amplitude.

The Protocol

  • Draw the spike from memory: rest at minus 70, threshold at minus 55, a sharp rise to plus 30, then a dip below rest before it settles.
  • Label the two doors: sodium in on the way up, potassium out on the way down.
  • Learn the rule that trips people up: a stronger input makes more spikes, not bigger ones.

See it in real life

Example

Why the hard serves sting more

Jaden spent the whole volleyball practice receiving serves, and his forearms can still tell him which ones were hard. Now he’s on the couch with his psychology notes, and one question on the review sheet seems to answer itself. When a stimulus gets stronger, what happens to the neuron’s action potential?

He writes “it gets bigger,” because that’s exactly how the serves felt. Lots of people give that answer, and it’s wrong.

An action potential, the electrical signal a neuron sends, works more like a trigger than a dimmer. A neuron rests at about minus 70 millivolts, and incoming signals nudge that number up. If they don’t push it past about minus 55, the nudge fades away and nothing gets sent. If they do, sodium channels open all at once, sodium rushes in, and the voltage shoots up to about plus 30 in roughly a thousandth of a second before potassium flowing out brings it back down. The spike comes out the same size every time. What a hard serve changes is how many spikes his nerve fibers send and how close together they come, so the sting is in the rate, not the size.

So instead of memorizing the sentence, he builds the idea. He folds twenty index cards into little tents and lines them up on the coffee table. When he flicks the first one too softly, nothing happens. When he pushes it just hard enough, the whole line goes down, and when he hits it much harder, the line goes down exactly the same way. Then he sketches the spike from memory twice, marking where sodium comes in and where potassium goes out.

The next morning the question is on the test, worded a little differently. He circles “a higher firing rate” and moves on to the next one.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

The card-tent model

Dominoes work for this, but index cards or playing cards folded into tents are just as good. It’s a model, not a real axon, but it gets the big rules right.

  1. Fold 15 to 20 cards in half and stand them in a line, each one a little closer to the next than its own height.
  2. Flick the first card so softly that it wobbles but stays up.
  3. Stand it back up and push it just hard enough to fall, then watch how the last card falls.
  4. Reset the line, hit the first card much harder, and watch the last card again.
  5. Now try to knock the line over a second time without standing the cards back up.

The timer needs JavaScript. A phone stopwatch works just as well.

What you’ll probably notice. The soft flick sends nothing, like an input that never reaches threshold. Once you’re past that point, the last card falls the same way whether your push was barely enough or far more than enough, and that’s what all or none means. The fallen line can’t go again until you reset it, which is the model’s version of the refractory period. The only way to say “stronger” is to knock lines down more often, and that’s what the slider shows, with more spikes but never bigger ones.

Step by step

Make it stick before the test

It takes about fifteen minutes, split across two days.

  1. Draw the spike from memory. Draw a flat line at minus 70, a dashed line at minus 55, a sharp rise to plus 30, and then a drop that dips below rest before it settles. Check it against your notes afterward, not while you draw.
  2. Label the two doors. Write “sodium in” on the way up and “potassium out” on the way down. Those two arrows explain the whole shape.
  3. Teach the card model. Show a friend or a sibling the soft flick, the just-enough push and the hard push in under a minute, and tell them what each one stands for.
  4. Write the trap question. Make up a multiple-choice question with “the spike gets bigger” as one of the wrong answers, and write one sentence on why it’s wrong.
  5. Redraw it tomorrow. Before you open anything, draw and label the spike again. Whatever you leave out is what to review.

How to tell it’s working. The next day, with no notes, you can draw and label the spike in under two minutes and explain why a stronger stimulus means more spikes instead of bigger ones.

When it gets hard. If the numbers blur together, hold on to just three. It rests at minus 70, fires at minus 55 and peaks at plus 30, and everything else hangs off those.
