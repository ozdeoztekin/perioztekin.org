The 2 A.M. Question That Quiets Overthinking

Temporal Distancing & Rumination

Overthinking zooms in on what upsets you. Asking how you might feel tomorrow morning can zoom you back out.

The Science

  • Overthinking zooms in on whatever is upsetting you, in fine detail. With strong emotion in the mix, that narrow view can turn troubling.
  • A tentative account: rumination tends to engage networks that replay the past and track your present state; future-building ones may be less involved.
  • Temporal distancing means asking how you will feel tomorrow, next week or next year, which can remind you that the feeling may change.

The Protocol

  • When a worry starts looping at night, ask yourself: how might I feel about this tomorrow morning?
  • Then widen the time frame: ask how you will feel about it next week, and next year.
  • Let the answers remind you that the feeling may change, without telling yourself the problem does not matter.

See it in real life

Example

The click of Salma’s reading lamp

Zainab fell asleep content. She shares a room with her little sister, Salma, who reads in bed, and at eleven Zainab told her to turn off the lamp because nobody cares about her dumb horse books. Salma clicked it off without a word, and the room went dark.

At 2:10 Zainab is wide awake, and everything is going wrong fast. She keeps hearing that click and the silence after it. Salma will remember this forever, she thinks, and Zainab must be the kind of sister who says things like that.

Overthinking zooms in on whatever is upsetting you, in fine detail, and with strong emotion in the mix, that close-up can turn troubling. One tentative account of this kind of loop, called rumination, is that networks for replaying the past and tracking how you feel right now tend to be more active, while parts of memory that build new scenes of the future may be less involved. Zainab’s loop fits that, all click and silence and how awful she feels, with nothing ahead.

She tries sending her mind forward instead. “How might I feel about this tomorrow morning?” Embarrassed, probably, but Salma will be at the breakfast table, and Zainab can say sorry before the bus. If she apologizes, next week this might be one bad night, not the story of them. A year from now she won’t remember the exact words, but she’ll still care how she talks to her sister. The feeling may change, and the problem still matters. She whispers the plan, sorry first and then a question about the book, and the loop gets quieter.

At breakfast she says it before she pours her cereal. “I’m sorry about the horse book thing. That was mean.” Salma shrugs, and a minute later she’s explaining the whole plot. That night the lamp stays on until Salma finishes her chapter, and Zainab reads her own book by its light.

  1. Where it stallsAt two in the morning, she keeps replaying what she said to her sister.
  2. What changes itShe asks how she might feel about it tomorrow, next week and next year.
  3. What happens nextShe says sorry at breakfast, and that night the lamp stays on.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

Tomorrow, next week, next year

Do this in the daytime, with something small that’s been bugging you today, like an awkward message or a mistake at practice, not the hardest thing in your life. Use the sliders below, and jot your answers anywhere.

  1. Write the thing in one sentence, and use the first slider to rate how loud it feels right now.
  2. Ask yourself, “How might I feel about this tomorrow morning?” Write one honest line.
  3. Ask the same about next week, then next year, one line each.
  4. Write one part of it that still matters and one small step you could take.
  5. Rate it again on the second slider.

The timer needs JavaScript. A phone stopwatch works just as well.

What you’ll probably notice. The number may drop a little, even though nothing about the problem has changed. Asking about tomorrow, next week and next year moves your attention from how bad it feels right now toward the chance that your view will shift with time. The line about what still matters belongs in the exercise, since a feeling that may change and a problem that still matters can both be true.

Step by step

Send the loop forward

Practice on small things during the day, so the question is ready when a loop starts at night.

  1. Notice the replay. When the same moment keeps playing in fine detail, take that as your cue. You don’t have to force it to stop.
  2. Ask about tomorrow morning. Ask yourself, “How might I feel about this tomorrow morning?” and answer it, even if the answer is “still embarrassed.”
  3. Widen it to next week and next year. Ask the same question about next week, then next year. Picturing those can zoom you back out from the close-up.
  4. Keep what still matters. Let the answers remind you that the feeling may change, without telling yourself the problem doesn’t matter. If it matters, decide what you’ll do about it.
  5. Leave the step for daylight. Give the problem one step for the morning, like an apology at breakfast or a message after school, and do it then.

How to tell it’s working. For a week, rate each morning how loud the night’s loop got, from 0 to 10, and note whether you used the question. Compare the nights you did with the nights you didn’t, and check whether the morning steps got done.

When it gets hard. Next week or next year can sometimes bring up new worries. If that happens, go back to tomorrow morning, which is closer and easier to picture, and leave the rest for daylight.

Good to know. This is for ordinary worries and isn’t a treatment for sleep problems. If worries keep you awake most nights, or a low mood sticks around for weeks, let a parent know and ask about seeing a doctor.
