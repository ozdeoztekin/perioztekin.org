Morning Light Can Move Jet Lag the Wrong Way

Jet Lag & Light Timing

Light can shift your body clock earlier or later depending on your internal timing, not the local clock.

The Science

  • Jet lag is a mismatch between your circadian rhythm and the new day-night schedule: your phone resets instantly, but your body clock does not.
  • Light-sensitive retina cells signal the brain's central clock, the suprachiasmatic nucleus or SCN, helping reset it depending on internal phase.
  • One phase marker is your daily low in body temperature: light before it tends to shift timing later, and light after it tends to shift it earlier.

The Protocol

  • Starting two days before an eastward trip, try waking thirty to forty-five minutes earlier and getting bright daylight on waking.
  • Allow an earlier sleep opportunity too, avoid deliberate sleep loss, and never stare directly at the sun.
  • After arrival, do not simply follow local sunrise or copy another traveler's light hours; trip direction and size matter.

See it in real life

Example

Forty minutes earlier, two days before Vienna

Sunniva slept ten hours on her first night in Vienna last summer. The youth orchestra had landed that morning, and the next day her roommate, Lise, who gets up at 5:30 even at home, took her for a walk along the river in the early sun to reset their clocks. That night Sunniva lay awake until after four, and she slept through two alarms before the first rehearsal.

This year’s tour flies out of Chicago on Thursday evening, and the first concert is on Sunday.

Jet lag is a mismatch between the body clock and the new day and night, since a phone changes time zones the moment the plane lands and internal timing doesn’t. Light-sensitive cells in the retina signal the brain’s central clock, the suprachiasmatic nucleus, or SCN, and which way light moves that clock depends on internal timing, not the local hour. Light before the daily low in body temperature tends to shift timing later, and light after it tends to shift it earlier. Seven in the morning in Vienna is midnight in Chicago, so that walk may have landed in Sunniva’s internal night, on the side that pushes the clock later, and Lise, an early riser, was starting from different timing.

Starting on Tuesday, two days before the flight, Sunniva sets her alarm forty minutes earlier and eats breakfast on the back steps in the bright morning, looking at the yard and never at the sun itself. She turns her light out forty minutes earlier too, so the earlier mornings don’t cost her sleep. It’s a head start, not instant adjustment, and in Vienna, when Lise heads out for her early walk, Sunniva doesn’t copy her hours.

On the second night she still wakes around three and reads for a while, but she’s asleep again before four and up with her first alarm. At Sunday’s dress rehearsal she’s tuning her viola before her stand partner arrives.

  1. Where it stallsAfter copying her roommate’s early walk, she lay awake until four.
  2. What changes itTwo days before flying east, she wakes earlier into bright daylight.
  3. What happens nextShe still wakes at three, but she’s up with her first alarm.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

Two clocks, one morning

Paper and a pen are all it takes. Pick a trip east, real or one you’d like to take, and look up how many hours ahead the destination is.

  1. Draw two clock faces side by side, label one Home and the other There, and shade your usual sleep hours on the Home clock.
  2. On the There clock, mark seven in the morning, about when you’d head out into daylight on your first morning.
  3. Work out what time that is at home and mark it on the Home clock.
  4. Check whether the mark lands inside your shaded sleep hours.

What you’ll probably notice. After a big jump east, a morning there often falls deep in your night at home, and for the first days your body clock can still be running on home time. Light that reaches the brain’s clock before your daily low in body temperature tends to push timing later, so that bright morning can work against you. Your sleep hours only hint at where that low falls and don’t measure it, which is one reason the plan starts the shift at home, where morning light arrives when your body expects morning.

Step by step

A head start before flying east

For a trip east across several time zones, like a tour, a tournament or a visit, starting while you’re still at home.

  1. Start two days before you fly. Begin while you’re still on your familiar home schedule, so part of the shift happens before the big change instead of all of it after.
  2. Wake thirty to forty-five minutes earlier. Set your alarm that much earlier on both mornings.
  3. Get bright daylight right away. Step outside or sit by a bright window as soon as you’re up. Look at the sky and your surroundings, and never straight at the sun.
  4. Go to bed earlier too. Give yourself an earlier chance to sleep so the early alarm doesn’t cut your night short. The aim is to move your timing, not to lose sleep.
  5. After landing, don’t copy anyone’s light. Local sunrise or another traveler’s light hours can push your clock the wrong way, since the trip’s direction and size and your own timing decide which way light moves it.

How to tell it’s working. Write down roughly when you fall asleep and wake on the two days before the trip and the first few days after. If you’ve flown east before, compare those nights with that trip, keeping in mind that a head start isn’t instant adjustment.

When it gets hard. The earlier bedtime is the hard part, since you may not feel sleepy yet. Dim the lights and lie down at the earlier time anyway, and don’t make up for it by staying up later the next night.

Good to know. There’s no melatonin, supplement, caffeine, fasting or special lamp anywhere in this, and nothing in it should cost you sleep. If you have a sleep problem or a health condition, talk it over with your family and a doctor before changing your schedule.
