Fuel the Long Session Before the Tank Runs Low

Carbohydrate Fueling & Gut Absorption

In a long session, water may not solve the fuel problem; carbohydrate helps only once it reaches your blood.

The Science

  • Carbohydrate is stored as glycogen in muscle and liver, and in prolonged demanding exercise those stores can fall enough to limit your work.
  • Eaten carbohydrate helps only if it reaches the circulation, which means moving through the gut and crossing the intestinal wall first.
  • Glucose and fructose cross by different transport routes, so when a large intake strains one, using both can make more carbohydrate available.

The Protocol

  • If you are a trained adult in a demanding session beyond about two hours, start around 60 grams of carbohydrate an hour, or lower.
  • Divide it across the session, such as 20 grams roughly every 20 minutes, and build up only as your stomach tolerates it.
  • Rehearse your known foods or drinks, amount and timing in training, and do not treat this as a rule for every short workout.

See it in real life

Example

Dropped on the water tower climb

Two Saturdays running, Yosef has been dropped on the water tower climb, the last hill of his cycling club’s long ride. He’s nineteen and has trained with the club and its coach, Kasper, for two seasons, with a hundred-mile charity ride coming in September.

He never skimps on water, but he doesn’t eat until his legs go hollow, well into the third hour. Last week he tried fixing that with a big slice of cake at the café, and his stomach sloshed and cramped all the way up the climb while the group rode away.

Water isn’t fuel, though. On a long, hard ride his body draws on carbohydrate stored as glycogen in his muscles and liver, and those stores can fall far enough to limit what he can hold. Eaten carbohydrate helps only once it crosses the intestinal wall into his blood, so a big serving eaten late can still be on its way when the climb starts. Glucose and fructose cross by different routes, so when a large amount strains one, a mix can let more through, but making a drink sweeter doesn’t guarantee it gets used any faster.

Kasper’s answer is fuel that starts early and comes in pieces his gut can handle. The target is about sixty grams of carbohydrate an hour, and since Yosef’s stomach isn’t used to eating on the bike, he starts below that and works up over three Saturdays. Using the labels, he cuts the fig bars and rice cakes he already likes into pieces with about twenty grams of carbohydrate each and sets his bike computer to beep every twenty minutes from the start.

On the third Saturday he eats a piece at every beep and drinks his water too. When the group tops the water tower climb, he’s still with them on the last wheel, and his stomach is quiet. That night he writes down what he ate, when, and how it sat.

  1. Where it stallsHe drinks plenty but eats late, and he gets dropped on the last hill.
  2. What changes itHe eats about twenty grams of carbohydrate every twenty minutes, rehearsed over three rides.
  3. What happens nextHe reaches the top of the climb still with the group.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

Two hands, two routes

This is a kitchen-table model, and nothing gets eaten. You need a bowl, an empty cup, 40 dried beans, 20 pieces of dry pasta and the stopwatch below.

  1. Put 40 beans in the bowl. For round 1, move them into the cup one at a time with your right hand only, as quickly as you can, while your left hand rests in your lap.
  2. Empty the cup, then put 20 beans and 20 pieces of pasta in the bowl.
  3. For round 2, use both hands together, each carrying one piece at a time, with the right hand taking only beans and the left only pasta.
  4. Compare the two times. Then think back to round 1 and ask yourself whether a bigger pile of beans would have made that one hand any faster.

The timer needs JavaScript. A phone stopwatch works just as well.

What you’ll probably notice. Each hand stands for one route across the gut wall, and each route carries only its own kind of sugar, glucose on one and fructose on the other. Once a hand is moving as fast as it can, a bigger pile doesn’t speed it up, and in the gut a sweeter drink doesn’t guarantee faster delivery either. With two routes working at once, more gets across each minute. The model leaves out a lot, but it shows why the amount and the kind of fuel both matter in a long session.

Step by step

Rehearse the fuel before the day

The idea is to practice the whole plan on training days, so nothing about it is new when the day that matters comes.

  1. Save it for long, hard sessions. If you’re a trained adult with a demanding session beyond about two hours coming up, plan the fuel for that session. Ordinary short workouts don’t need a fueling plan.
  2. Start around 60 grams an hour, or lower. That’s one starting target for carbohydrate in long sessions. If your stomach isn’t used to food while you work, begin below it and build up only as your stomach tolerates more.
  3. Split it into small, regular portions. For sixty grams an hour, that’s about twenty grams roughly every twenty minutes, starting early in the session. Small planned portions are easier to handle than one big serving you’ve never tried.
  4. Rehearse with foods you know. Use foods or drinks you already like, read the labels to size the portions, and try the exact amounts and timing in training first. At higher amounts, a mix with both glucose and fructose can use both routes.
  5. Write down how it sat. After each long session, note what you ate, when, and how your stomach felt, and change one thing at a time.

How to tell it’s working. Over a few long sessions, note how the last hour felt and whether your stomach stayed comfortable at each amount. One good ride is a hint, so look for a pattern across several before you settle on a plan.

When it gets hard. The most common mistake is trying something new on the day that matters, like a new food, a bigger amount or a sweeter drink. If your stomach complains, return to the last amount that sat well and build up more slowly in training.

Good to know. This is for trained adults in long, demanding sessions, not for anyone under 18 or for ordinary workouts, and it isn’t a reason to buy any product. If you have diabetes or another condition that affects what you eat, follow the plan you have with your doctor or dietitian.
