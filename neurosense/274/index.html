Let the Help Disappear One Step at a Time

Worked Examples & Fading Support

Following a solution and producing one are different jobs; fading worked steps hands the decisions to you.

The Science

  • A worked example shows the problem, steps and result; early on, that can make an unfamiliar procedure understandable without discovering the route.
  • If every step stays visible, the material keeps making your choices; removing all of it at once can leave too much unsupported problem solving.
  • Fading creates a gradual transfer: some correct steps remain while you generate the missing part, which limits how much you must control at once.

The Protocol

  • Study an accurate worked example of the kind of problem you are learning, then try a similar one with the final step left blank.
  • Check your step against the correct solution; if it is wrong, inspect the operation that produced it before removing more help.
  • As each part becomes workable, remove another worked step on the next similar problem, then attempt a whole one without them.

See it in real life

Example

Fifty moves with a rook to spare

At Tuesday’s chess club, Adela had a king and a rook against Lazar’s lone king, one of the most basic wins in chess. She chased his king around the middle of the board, checking it from every side, and after fifty moves without a capture Lazar claimed a draw, which the rules allow.

What stings is that she knows this ending. She has played through the two pages on it in the club’s endgame book several times at home, and every move made sense. With nobody supplying the next one, she didn’t know where to start.

Following a solution and producing one are different jobs. A worked example shows the problem, the steps and the result, which makes an unfamiliar procedure understandable without having to find the route. If every step stays visible, though, the book keeps making the choices, and dropping all the help in one go, the way a real game does, can leave too much to work out at once. Fading the help hands the decisions over gradually, with some correct steps left in place while you supply the missing part.

On Wednesday she sets up the book’s next position, covers its last move with a sticky note and plays everything before it. She finds the checkmate and slides the note down to check. On the next position she leaves herself two moves and gets the first wrong, checking before her own king is close enough, so his king walks out of the corner. She works out why before taking away more help. Over the next few nights the note climbs a move at a time until it covers the whole line, and then she starts from positions of her own.

A week later Lazar plays the ending against her from a new position, and sixteen moves in, his king is trapped on the edge and she says checkmate. King, bishop and knight against a king is a different ending, and she hasn’t touched it yet.

  1. Where it stallsWith a rook to spare, she can’t finish, and the game is drawn.
  2. What changes itShe covers the book’s last moves and takes over one more each night.
  3. What happens nextFrom a new position, she gives checkmate in sixteen moves.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

Let the steps fade

Grab a pencil and some paper. The first problem is fully worked, and each one after it leaves more of the steps to you.

  1. Read the worked example and say why each step happens.
  2. In the second problem the earlier steps are done for you. Supply the last one yourself, then check it against the answers.
  3. If it’s wrong, find the operation that went wrong before you go on.
  4. In the third problem supply the last two steps, and in the fourth, do the whole thing on your own.
Worked4x + 5 = x + 23 → take x from both sides → 3x + 5 = 23 → take 5 from both sides → 3x = 18 → divide both sides by 3 → x = 6
Last step yours5x + 4 = 2x + 16 → take 2x from both sides → 3x + 4 = 16 → take 4 from both sides → 3x = 12 → ?
Two steps yours6x + 1 = 2x + 21 → take 2x from both sides → 4x + 1 = 21 → ? → ?
All yours7x + 3 = 3x + 15
Answersx = 4 · x = 5 · x = 3

Keep the answers covered until you’ve tried all three.

What you’ll probably notice. The second problem probably felt manageable, because only one decision was yours, dividing both sides by three. By the last one you made every decision yourself, and you got there one step at a time, with a correct example still in view. That’s what fading does, giving the help a planned exit. Solving the last one shows you can handle problems like it, not every new kind of equation.

Step by step

Fade the help one step at a time

For any new procedure that comes with worked examples, like a kind of math problem, a chemistry calculation or a chess technique.

  1. Study a correct worked example. Go through an accurate worked example of the exact kind of problem you’re learning, step by step, and make sure you can say why each step happens.
  2. Leave only the last step blank. On a similar problem, keep the setup and the earlier steps, cover the final step and do that part yourself.
  3. Check it, and look into any mistake. Compare your step with the correct solution. If it’s wrong, find the operation that produced the error before you take away more help.
  4. Uncover one more step each time. When that part is workable, leave the last two steps blank on the next similar problem, then three, so you take over more of the route.
  5. Finish with a whole problem. Try a complete similar problem with no worked steps. Getting it right shows you can do this kind on your own, not every new variation.

How to tell it’s working. Before you start, and again after a few days of fading, count how many whole problems of this kind you can solve correctly with no worked steps. If that number rises, you’re producing solutions rather than following them.

When it gets hard. It’s tempting to take all the help away after one good try. If a step falls apart when you remove more, put that step’s help back for a problem or two, then try again.
