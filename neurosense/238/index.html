Why Random Notes Can Disrupt a New Piano Sequence

Sound Feedback & Sequence Learning

For a new piano sequence, a steady tone can work as well as real notes, but random notes can hurt learning.

The Science

  • When learning a fixed piano-key sequence, the notes you hear and the finger movements you make are two different streams of information.
  • Early on, a steady doorbell-like tone can support learning as well as correct notes, while random notes can make playing and learning much worse.
  • In one explanation, motor neurons in your cortex help control movements you are still learning, and attending to your fingers helps engage them.

The Protocol

  • Pick a short, fixed key sequence and repeat it with your attention on the order of your fingers.
  • Keep the sound predictable while the sequence is new, rather than letting each press make a random note.
  • Once the sequence is in your hands, bring the sound back in and put the movements and the notes together.

See it in real life

Example

A doorbell job for Ziad

Rosalie’s band is playing the street fair on Saturday, their keyboard player broke her wrist, and Farah said yes before she thought it through. The song opens with a short riff, the same fixed run of keys over and over, and for the first eight bars it’s the only sound.

All week she has practiced at the digital piano, and all week her little brother, Ziad, has climbed onto the bench to help. Every time she presses a key, he presses one too, somewhere up high and different each time, and he calls them sparkles. By Thursday the riff still falls apart in the same spot.

When you’re learning a fixed sequence of keys, the notes you hear and the movements you make are two different streams of information. Early on, the same doorbell-like tone on every press can support learning as well as the right notes, while random notes on each press can make playing and learning much worse. One explanation is that early learning of a sequence is mostly about producing the finger movements. Motor neurons in the cortex help control movements you’re still learning, and paying attention to your fingers helps engage them. Ziad’s sparkles are random notes on every press.

Farah doesn’t want to kick him off the bench, so she gives him a new job. He’s the doorbell now, and every time she presses a key, he presses the same high C. She slows the riff down, says the finger numbers out loud and keeps her eyes on her hand, and plays it over and over until she gets five clean runs in a row.

On Friday she thanks the doorbell, sends him to bed and plays the riff with only its own notes, listening this time, until the sound and her fingers fit together. On Saturday, under the tent, she plays the opening alone, and the drummer comes in right on time.

  1. Where it stallsHer brother adds a random high note every time she presses a key.
  2. What changes itHe becomes a steady doorbell, and she plays with her eyes on her fingers.
  3. What happens nextShe plays the opening riff alone at the street fair, right in time.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

Steady ding, random clang

You’ll need a partner, a metal spoon, a mug, two other things that sound different when tapped, like a pot lid and a book, and the stopwatch below.

  1. Rest your hand on a table and number your fingers from index to pinky, 1 to 4. You’ll tap the two sequences in the box.
  2. For round 1, use sequence A. Each time you tap, your partner taps the mug lightly once, the same sound every time. Use the stopwatch to see how many seconds you need to tap the whole sequence correctly five times in a row.
  3. For round 2, use sequence B, and this time your partner taps a different object after each of your taps, in no pattern. Use the stopwatch the same way.
  4. Compare the two times. Then swap jobs, and have your partner do the random round first.
Sequence A4 1 3 2 4 1, with the same sound on every tap
Sequence B2 4 1 3 1 2, with a random sound on every tap

Finger 1 is your index finger, and finger 4 is your pinky.

The timer needs JavaScript. A phone stopwatch works just as well.

What you’ll probably notice. The random round tends to be messier, with more restarts and lost places, even though both sequences are six taps long. The steady sound gives your ears nothing new to chase, so your attention can stay on the order of your fingers. It’s a rough tabletop version of the piano finding, where one repeated tone worked as well as the right notes early on and random notes made learning much worse.

Step by step

Fingers first, then the sound

Reach for it when a new passage keeps falling apart in the same spot.

  1. Pick a short, fixed sequence. Choose a few notes that always come in the same order, like the opening of a riff or a tricky run, short enough to repeat many times.
  2. Put your attention on finger order. Play it slowly and say the finger numbers as you go, with your eyes on your hand.
  3. Keep the sound predictable. Hearing the right notes is fine, and so is one steady tone. Leave out anything that adds a different note every time you press.
  4. Repeat until it’s in your hands. Aim for five clean runs in a row at a slow tempo before you speed it up.
  5. Bring the sound back in. Once the sequence is in your hands, listen to the notes again and shape them, then put the passage back into the piece.

How to tell it’s working. Each time you sit down to practice, play the sequence ten times first and count the clean runs. Write the number down and see whether it climbs over a few days.

When it gets hard. Playing with your attention on your fingers can feel unmusical, and it’s tempting to start shaping the sound right away. Give your fingers a few days first, since the music comes back in the last step.
