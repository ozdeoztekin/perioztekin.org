Your Brain Cheats Physics to Be Fast

Saltatory Conduction

Action potentials don't crawl down an axon. They leap from gap to gap, and that physical leap is where the brain's reaction speed lives.

The Science

  • Huxley & Stämpfli (Journal of Physiology, 1949): action potentials in myelinated axons regenerate only at the Nodes of Ranvier, the small unmyelinated gaps between myelin segments. Between nodes, the voltage propagates passively through the insulated segment.
  • The nodes hold a very high density of voltage-gated Na+ channels (clusters of roughly 1,000 to 2,000 per square micron). Each regeneration step at a node restores the action potential to full amplitude before it travels passively to the next node.
  • Rasband & Peles (Cold Spring Harbor Perspectives in Biology, 2016): Na+ channels cluster at nodes, K+ channels at juxtaparanodes, and cell-adhesion molecules at the paranodes hold the structure together. When this organization is disrupted, nerve conduction slows.
  • Hartline & Colman (Current Biology, 2007): myelin speeds conduction roughly tenfold, and getting the same gain by thickening the axon instead would take an axon about 100 times wider. Saltatory conduction lets fast signals travel along thin axons that save space and energy.

The Protocol

  • Eat foods with omega-3 fats, such as walnuts, flaxseeds and fatty fish; myelin is mostly fat.
  • Use the same focused practice as Episode 13; myelination and saltatory speed are linked.
  • Skip alcohol; heavy drinking is linked to damage in white matter, the brain's myelinated wiring.

See it in real life

Example

Five strips of tape down the hallway

Olivia buzzed in on a myelin question twice at Wednesday’s practice and got it wrong both times. First she said the signal travels through the myelin, and then she called myelin a better wire. There are four people on her team, biology is supposed to be her part, and the regional science quiz is on Saturday.

That night she reads up on it. Myelin doesn’t carry the signal at all. It wraps the axon, the long fiber a neuron sends its signals down, in sections with small gaps between them. The gaps are called nodes of Ranvier, and they’re packed with sodium channels. The action potential, the neuron’s electrical signal, gets rebuilt to full strength only at those gaps, and along the wrapped sections in between, the voltage spreads on its own. So instead of being rebuilt at every point along the fiber, the signal leaps from gap to gap, which is called saltatory conduction. Without myelin, an axon would have to be about 100 times wider to send signals as fast, so leaping is how fast reflexes fit inside thin nerve fibers that don’t cost much energy.

Then she sticks five strips of blue painter’s tape down the upstairs hallway, a stride apart, while her little brother watches from the stairs. Walking heel to toe, with a foot down at every point like a signal in an axon with no myelin, takes her 22 steps. Stepping only on the tape takes five.

On an index card she draws an axon with five gaps, writes “rebuilt here” over each one, and explains it to her brother until he can say it back to her.

On Saturday the moderator has only read “In a myelinated axon, the action potential is regenerated” when Olivia hits her buzzer. “At the nodes, the gaps in the myelin,” she says. The bonus question asks why that makes the signal faster, and she answers that one too.

  2. What changes itShe walks the hall heel to toe, then steps only on five strips of tape.
  3. What happens nextOn Saturday she buzzes in before the question is finished.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

Heel to toe, then the tape

This is a walking model of where the signal gets rebuilt. You’ll need a hallway or a clear stretch of floor, five pieces of tape and the stopwatch below.

  1. Stick the five pieces of tape down the floor in a line, about one big stride apart.
  2. Walk the whole line heel to toe, with each step touching the one before, and count your steps while you time it.
  3. Walk it again stepping only on the tape. Walk instead of jumping, and count and time that too.
  4. Compare the two counts and the two times.

The timer needs JavaScript. A phone stopwatch works just as well.

What you’ll probably notice. Heel to toe, you put your foot down over and over, but on the tape you land only five times and cover the same floor much faster. An axon without myelin has to rebuild the action potential at every point along the way, like the heel-to-toe walk. A myelinated axon rebuilds it only at the gaps, where the sodium channels are packed in, and the voltage spreads through the insulated stretch in between on its own, so the signal arrives with far fewer stops. The one thing the model gets wrong is effort. Your strides take work, but the voltage crosses the insulated stretch without any.

Step by step

Learn where the signal leaps

It takes about fifteen minutes, spread over two days.

  1. Draw the axon with its gaps. Draw a long line with myelin segments like sausages and small gaps between them, and label the gaps “nodes.”
  2. Mark where it’s rebuilt. Over each gap, where the sodium channels are packed, write “rebuilt here, full size.” Over each insulated stretch, write “spreads on its own.”
  3. Walk it once. Do the heel-to-toe walk and then the tape, and say “rebuild” out loud every time your foot lands on a strip.
  4. Know why it matters. Without the leaping, an axon would have to be about 100 times wider to be as fast. Leaping is how fast reflexes can run through thin fibers.
  5. Explain it in three sentences. Tell a friend or someone at home what myelin does, where the signal is rebuilt and why that’s faster. Tomorrow, redraw the axon without looking.

How to tell it’s working. Tomorrow, with no notes, draw the axon, mark where the action potential is rebuilt and explain in three sentences why leaping is faster. If the person you explained it to can say it back, you’ve got it.

When it gets hard. If “saltatory” won’t stick, remember that it comes from the Latin word for leaping, and think of the tape strips, since the signal lands only on the gaps.
