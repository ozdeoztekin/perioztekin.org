What Slowing Brain Aging Really Means

Brain Aging & Alzheimer's Prevention

People say they want to slow down brain aging, as if it were one single thing. It is not. There is no one dial. There are several, running at once.

The Science

  • Jack et al. (2013, Lancet Neurol): Alzheimer's involves a cascade of changes, including amyloid and tau buildup and neuronal injury, that begins years before symptoms.
  • Iturria-Medina et al. (2016, Nat Commun): vascular dysfunction and reduced blood flow are among the earliest detectable changes in the aging, at-risk brain.
  • Livingston et al. (2020, Lancet Commission): a large share of dementia risk is linked to modifiable factors like blood pressure, physical activity, hearing, and social engagement.

The Protocol

  • Prioritize consistent, sufficient sleep, when the brain clears waste.
  • Exercise regularly to support blood flow and vascular health.
  • Keep blood pressure and blood sugar in a healthy range.
  • Stay socially connected and mentally challenged.

See it in real life

Example

Day 52 of the puzzle streak

The streak counter on Arjun’s memory-puzzle app says 52. He started it the week his grandfather in Pune was diagnosed with Alzheimer’s, and he hasn’t missed a day: forty-five minutes after school, alone in his room, door shut. If puzzles can keep his own brain safe, he isn’t taking chances.

To get home faster, he takes the bus now instead of walking with Dev, the way they did all last year. Dev asked three times. Then he stopped asking.

Brain aging isn’t one process. Sticky proteins can build up, cells and their connections can wear down, low-grade inflammation can simmer, and blood flow can drop. In one person one of these leads; in another, a different one does. A large share of dementia risk is linked to things people can change, like blood pressure, physical activity, hearing and social connection. The puzzles work one dial, alone. The walk he dropped worked two: moving, which feeds blood flow, and being with someone.

He starts with an index card and five dials: sleep, moving, blood pressure and sugar, people, learning something new. Then he marks what his week actually touches. One mark. He texts Dev to ask about walking tomorrow and gets back a thumbs-up and “finally.” He keeps the puzzles, but only ten minutes, after dinner.

Fifteen school days later, the card on his wall has thirteen walks marked, and the streak ended at 61. On Friday Dev asks if he wants to come to his cousin’s pickup game on Saturday, and Arjun adds it to the card before he answers.

  1. Where it stallsA 52-day puzzle streak, alone in his room, and Dev stopped asking
  2. What changes itFive dials on an index card, and the walk home with Dev
  3. What happens nextThirteen walks in fifteen school days, puzzles down to ten minutes

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

The dial sort

You need a pen and a piece of paper, and an honest look at your own week.

  1. Draw five columns and label them: Sleep, Moving, Blood pressure and sugar, People, Something new.
  2. Go down the list here. For each habit, tick every column it touches.
  3. Add three things you actually did this week, and tick their columns too.
  4. Circle the column with the fewest ticks in your own week.
1A memory-puzzle app, alone
2Walking to school with a friend
3The same bedtime most nights
4A beginners’ class in something you’ve never tried
5A yearly checkup with a doctor
6A pickup game with friends

There’s no answer key. Go by what the video says each dial needs: sleep, movement for blood flow, a doctor for blood pressure and sugar, people, and new challenges.

What you’ll probably notice. A single brain trick tends to land in one column. Ordinary habits shared with other people often land in two or three. That’s the video’s point: brain aging is a stack of processes, so the habits that help are the ones that touch several systems at once. The column you circled is a good place to start.

Step by step

Turn several dials at once

Treat these as habits for life.

  1. Protect steady sleep. About the same bedtime most nights, and enough hours. Sleep is when the brain clears waste.
  2. Move most days. Something that raises your heart rate: walking fast, biking, a game, dancing. Moving feeds blood flow to the brain.
  3. Know your numbers with a doctor. At your next checkup, ask about your blood pressure and blood sugar. For parents and grandparents, those numbers are part of regular care.
  4. Pick habits that come with people. Walk with a friend, join a class or a team, call someone on a schedule. One habit can cover moving and people at once.
  5. Keep learning something new. Something that stretches you a little, so your brain keeps meeting a challenge instead of repeating one.

How to tell it’s working. At the end of each week, redo the dial sort with your own week. If the column you circled has more ticks after a month, it’s working. Don’t judge it by how sharp you feel on any one day.

When it gets hard. Worry makes one dramatic fix feel safer than five ordinary habits. When you catch yourself hunting for the one thing, look at your card and pick the next habit that touches two columns.

Good to know. None of this is a guarantee, and it can’t tell you anything about a diagnosis. For your own or a family member’s risk, talk with a doctor.
