Write the Loop Out

Expressive Writing

A private page can do something a busy mind cannot. It can give a looping thought one place to land. The point is not perfect sentences.

The Science

  • Expressive writing means writing about a stressful or difficult experience for fifteen to thirty minutes.
  • It seems to work best with both facts and feelings, and the standard method uses three to five sessions, often four.
  • It can feel worse at first, and people whose writing slowly made sense of the event tended to benefit most.

The Protocol

  • Write privately for fifteen to thirty minutes without editing.
  • Include facts, feelings then and now, and any links that come to mind.
  • Repeat across four sessions, and decompress afterward.

See it in real life

Example

The night before Lucía left

Lucía’s bedroom door is the last thing Gabriela sees before the hallway light goes off, and that’s when the loop starts. The night before her sister left for college, Lucía packed the speaker they had always shared, and Gabriela said, “Take it. Nobody here will miss you.” Lucía said “okay” and shut her door.

That was three weeks ago, and their texts since then have been short and polite. Every night the scene replays, with the speaker, the word okay, the closed door and the quick hug in the driveway, and most nights Gabriela is still awake after one.

A loop like that keeps circling when it has nowhere to land, and expressive writing gives it one. You write for fifteen to thirty minutes, privately and without editing, about the facts, how you felt then and now, and the connections your mind keeps making, over about four sessions. Going straight at the feeling on purpose can be uncomfortable, but people whose writing slowly makes sense of what happened tend to feel better later on.

She takes out a spiral notebook, sets a 20-minute timer and writes without fixing anything. The second night she cries through most of it and finds things she didn’t know were there, like how quiet the house is now, how their parents argue more without Lucía to make them laugh and how scared she was of being the only one left at dinner. After each session she walks the dog around the block.

By the fourth night the pages are about what she wants to say to Lucía, and she’s asleep before midnight. On Saturday she calls Lucía and reads her nothing from the notebook. “Sorry about the speaker night,” she says, and after a pause Lucía says, “Yeah. That one stung.” Then Lucía asks about the dog, and they talk for forty minutes.

  1. Where it stallsThe same scene replays every night, and she’s still awake after one.
  2. What changes itShe writes privately for twenty minutes, four nights, then walks the dog.
  3. What happens nextShe’s asleep before midnight, and then she calls Lucía and they talk for forty minutes.

Try it yourself · one evening

One private page

You’ll need paper or a notebook nobody else reads, fifteen minutes alone and the timer below. For a first try, pick something that keeps replaying but isn’t the hardest thing you’ve been through.

  1. At the top of the page, rate how loud the loop is right now, from 0 to 10.
  2. Start the timer and write without stopping, fixing or rereading about what happened, how you felt then, how you feel now and anything it keeps connecting to.
  3. If you run dry, write the last sentence again until something new comes.
  4. When the timer ends, close the notebook and take five calm minutes, like a walk, a shower, a glass of water or a song.
  5. Rate the loop again, and notice tonight whether it comes back the same way.

The timer needs JavaScript. A phone stopwatch works just as well.

What you’ll probably notice. Some people feel lighter right away, and plenty feel a bit stirred up, because they went straight at the feeling on purpose, and both are normal. The bigger change tends to show up across sessions, when the loop starts to feel like a story you’ve already told instead of a scene you’re stuck in. The plan below has four sessions for that reason.

Step by step

Four private sessions

Pick one thing that keeps replaying, and stay with it for all four.

  1. Find fifteen to thirty private minutes. Pick the same time each day if you can, somewhere nobody reads over your shoulder.
  2. Write without editing. Forget about spelling, grammar and audience. Nobody has to read it, and you don’t have to reread it.
  3. Put in facts, feelings and links. Write what happened, how you felt then, how you feel now and the connections your mind keeps making.
  4. Do four sessions. Write four days in a row, or four times within a week, since the repeats are part of how it works.
  5. Decompress after each one. Take a walk or a shower, have a snack or play some music before you go back to anything else.

How to tell it’s working. Before each session, rate how loud the loop is from 0 to 10 and how often it showed up the day before. Across the four sessions, see whether those numbers drop, or whether it starts to feel more like a story you can look at.

When it gets hard. Some sessions feel worse than others, especially early on, because you’re touching real emotion on purpose. If it gets to be too much, stop early, decompress and write about a smaller corner of it next time.

Good to know. If writing stirs up strong distress, or the topic is something really big, like a loss or something that hurt you, don’t do it alone. Stop and talk with a parent, a school counselor or another adult you trust.
