Memories Are Made While You Sit Still

Hippocampal Consolidation

The hippocampus converts short-term experience into long-term memory. Remove it, and you can never form a new declarative memory again.

The Science

  • Scoville & Milner (Journal of Neurology, Neurosurgery and Psychiatry, 1957): the case of patient H.M. (Henry Molaison) established the hippocampus as essential for declarative memory formation. After bilateral medial-temporal-lobe resection for epilepsy, H.M. could no longer form new memories of facts or events, though his intelligence and procedural learning were intact.
  • Squire & Wixted (Annual Review of Neuroscience, 2011): the hippocampus binds the components of an experience (what, where, when) and acts as an index pointing to distributed cortical sites. Over weeks to months, these traces become independent of the hippocampus through systems consolidation.
  • Tambini, Ketz & Davachi (Neuron, 2010): in awake people, hippocampal-cortical correlations during rest right after learning predicted later memory for the studied material, a sign that memory processing continues in the minutes after learning.
  • Wilson & McNaughton (Science, 1994): in rats, groups of hippocampal neurons that fired together during a task fired together again during later sleep, a reactivation thought to help consolidation.

The Protocol

  • 5 minutes of silence after every study block. Phone AWAY, not just face-down.
  • Don't switch straight to a high-input task; give your brain a quiet gap.
  • Sleep after studying helps consolidation, and an all-nighter skips it.

See it in real life

Example

Gold and Gold Flip

Gold and Gold Flip look almost the same on the play sheet. In Gold, Nolan comes off the screen to the left; in Gold Flip he fakes left and cuts right. He has been the starting point guard for two weeks, and before Friday’s rivalry game the coach has put in twelve new plays, which Nolan has to call out and run.

At Tuesday’s practice he called Gold Flip and ran Gold, twice, and both times the whistle went and four teammates stood in the wrong spots looking at him. He has been studying the sheet every night: 20 minutes on his phone, then straight into videos until he falls asleep.

His routine skips a step. The hippocampus ties the pieces of a new memory together, the what, the where and the when, and keeps an index of where they’re stored. In the minutes after learning, it stays busy with the new material, and in awake people, that activity during rest predicts what they remember later. Sleep reactivates recent memories again. A feed right after studying fills that window with new things to take in.

On Wednesday he studies the sheet at the kitchen table for 20 minutes, then puts his phone in the hall closet, where he can’t see it, and sits on the back steps for five minutes doing nothing. After that he showers and has the light off by 10:30, with no videos. Thursday, the same.

At Thursday’s walk-through the coach shouts the twelve plays in random order, and Nolan calls and runs every one without a whistle. On Friday, with 40 seconds left, he holds up a fist for Gold Flip, and all five of them are exactly where they should be.

  1. Where it stallsCalls Gold Flip, runs Gold, twice, with four teammates watching
  2. What changes itTwenty minutes on the sheet, phone in the closet, five quiet minutes
  3. What happens nextAll twelve plays clean, and Gold Flip right on Friday

Try it yourself · 10 minutes

The scouting report

A small version of the five quiet minutes, with a check tomorrow morning. You need paper, a pen and your phone’s alarm.

  1. Read the scouting report once, slowly, for about a minute. Then cover it.
  2. Set a five-minute alarm on your phone, leave the phone in another room, and sit somewhere with nothing to do. Looking out a window is fine.
  3. When the alarm goes off, write down every fact you remember, with the who, the where and the when.
  4. Tomorrow morning, before you look at anything, write them down again and count what stayed.
Number 4Left-handed. Passes to the corner after every screen.
Number 11Only shoots from the right corner.
Number 22Stays under the basket. Slow getting back on defense.
Second halfFull-court press after every made free throw.
TimeoutsTheir coach calls one after any 6-point run.
Last minuteThey always inbound to number 4.

Six facts. Read them once, slowly, then cover them.

What you’ll probably notice. You’ll probably get most of the report back after the quiet minutes. Whatever is still there tomorrow went through both windows in The Science above: processing in the minutes right after you read it, and again in sleep. Each fact ties a who to a where or a when, which is exactly the kind of binding the hippocampus does.

Step by step

Let it settle before you scroll

Use it after every study block this week.

  1. Study in one block. One subject, one sheet or one chapter, with your phone out of reach.
  2. Put the phone in another room. Not face down on the desk. Somewhere you’d have to get up and walk to.
  3. Take five minutes of nothing. Sit, look out a window or walk slowly without headphones. Set a timer so you’re not checking the clock. Ten minutes is fine if you have them.
  4. Ease into something low-key. Next, pick something with little new input, like a shower, the dishes or a walk. Save the feed and the games for later.
  5. Sleep on it. Deep sleep runs the replay a second time, so an all-nighter before the big day skips that round.

How to tell it’s working. The next morning, before rereading, write down everything you remember from the block. Do it after a few days with the quiet five minutes and a few days when you went straight to your phone, then compare the counts.

When it gets hard. The first few times, five minutes of nothing feels long, and your hand will go to your pocket. That’s why the phone goes in another room.

Good to know. Forgetting some of what you study is normal. If you notice you’re forgetting recent conversations or getting lost in places you know well, tell a parent and see a doctor.
