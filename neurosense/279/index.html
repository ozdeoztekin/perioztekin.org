Slow Down to Gather Evidence, Not Just to Wait

Evidence Accumulation & Speed Versus Accuracy

Staring longer at the same blur may not help; finding the fact that separates your options can.

The Science

  • Your brain weighs evidence about competing options against stored information from earlier encounters, estimating what each is likely to return.
  • The evidence is noisy: details can be obscured, and biological processing varies, so in a poor sample noise can outweigh the useful signal.
  • More relevant information can shift the balance between options; staring longer at a blur that might be a three or an eight may not.

The Protocol

  • Before an important choice, decide which matters more here: speed or accuracy.
  • When accuracy matters, name the uncertainty that could actually change your choice: what fact separates the options?
  • Use the time available to check that fact: inspect the detail, confirm the instruction or get the missing information.

See it in real life

Example

The line across the corner

Joon’s phone died for good on Monday, and he has $160 saved from walking dogs. Two sellers nearby have the same model. One wants $160, and the other wants $120, but her photo shows a thin bright line across one corner of the screen, and she’ll sell to whoever can pick it up tonight.

He has zoomed in on that corner for twenty minutes. Some looks it’s a crack, others a window’s reflection, and the closer he zooms, the more it breaks into gray squares. His thumb keeps drifting toward “I’ll take it,” mostly so he can stop looking.

His brain isn’t reading an answer off the photo. It weighs what he can see against what it has stored from earlier encounters, like last spring’s game controller, whose “small scuff” was a crack, and estimates what each option is likely to get him. That evidence is noisy. A photo can blur the one detail that matters, and the brain’s own processing varies too, so a choice built on a poor sample can go wrong. Staring longer at the same blur gives him nothing new to weigh.

Before answering, he asks what matters more tonight, speed or getting it right. Being slow might cost him this phone, but phones like it get listed most weeks. Being wrong would cost $120 and leave him with a cracked screen. The fact that separates his options is whether the line is in the glass, and a reflection moves when the angle changes while a crack stays put. He asks the seller for one more photo of the screen showing plain white, taken from the side.

It arrives eleven minutes later, and the line runs straight across the white, exactly where it was. He thanks her and messages the other seller, and on Saturday he and his dad pick up the $160 phone. When Joon tilts it under the porch light with a blank white page open, the glass is clean.

  1. Where it stallsHe stares at the same blurry photo, ready to buy and be done.
  2. What changes itHe asks for one new photo that would separate a crack from glare.
  3. What happens nextThe line stays put, so he buys the phone with clean glass.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

Three or eight

Tear two small squares from a sheet of paper, grab a pen and some tape, and find a room or hallway long enough to stand well back. The sliders below record how sure you feel.

  1. Write a 3 on one square and an 8 on the other, in your ordinary handwriting and about the size of a fingernail. Fold both, mix them up behind your back and tape one, unfolded, to a wall or a door without looking at it.
  2. Without looking at it, walk well back, far enough away that you can’t tell which number it is, and only then turn around.
  3. Stay where you are and keep staring at it for thirty seconds, then rate your certainty with the first slider.
  4. Now name the one detail that separates the two. An 8 is closed on its left side and a 3 is open there. Move closer a step at a time, watching only the left side, and stop as soon as you can tell.
  5. Rate yourself again with the second slider, then check the paper.

The timer needs JavaScript. A phone stopwatch works just as well.

What you’ll probably notice. Thirty seconds of staring from where you stood often changes little, because you keep looking at the same poor sample. A few steps aimed at the left side change what you know, since that detail is what separates a 3 from an 8. Your answer got better when the evidence got better, and standing there longer didn’t give you any.

Step by step

Check the fact that decides it

For the next choice where a mistake would cost you money, a trip or a lot of time.

  1. Decide what matters more here. Ask whether speed or accuracy matters more for this choice. Compare what a wrong choice would cost with what a slow one would cost, since a deadline can be real and still be the smaller risk.
  2. Name the fact that would change your mind. Look for the one uncertainty that, if it went the other way, would change what you pick, and make that the thing you check.
  3. Get a better look at that one thing. Find something that shows it differently, like a closer view, another angle, the written instructions or a question to the person who knows. Rereading what you already have may not add anything.
  4. Fit the check into the time you have. Work within the deadline you have, and stop once the fact is clear. No set amount of waiting makes a choice right, and collecting forever doesn’t help either.
  5. Note whether the check changed anything. Write down what you were about to choose, what the check showed and what you chose in the end.

How to tell it’s working. After a few choices, look back at your notes and count how often the check changed your first pick. If it never does, you may be checking things that don’t separate your options.

When it gets hard. It’s easy to check things that wouldn’t change your choice and call it being careful. Before each check, ask whether a different answer would make you choose differently, and skip it if it wouldn’t.
