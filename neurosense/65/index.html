Why Alertness Has a Dial

The Locus Coeruleus

You need to wake up for practice, class, a shift, or a hard task, but stress makes focus shaky. The same arousal that wakes you up can tip into alarm if the dial goes too high.

The Science

  • Aston-Jones and Cohen (2005), Annual Review of Neuroscience: a theory built mainly on monkey studies proposes that the locus coeruleus uses norepinephrine to tune how strongly the brain responds, which shapes performance.
  • Sara (2009), Nature Reviews Neuroscience: evidence mostly from animal studies links brief bursts of locus coeruleus activity to sharper attention, and too much steady firing to scattered attention.
  • Berridge and Waterhouse (2003), Brain Research Reviews: norepinephrine helps set waking states, and its effects on attention depend on how much is released, so the useful zone is alert, not panicked.
  • Alertness is a dial, not an on-off switch.

The Protocol

  • Aim for a small state shift, not a shock.
  • Stand up, change posture, and add thirty seconds of brisk movement.
  • Get bright light in your eyes, never by staring at the sun.
  • Notice the signal, name the mechanism, and change one input before autopilot.

See it in real life

Example

The chickadee in net two

The nets open at 5:45, as the light comes up over the field behind the nature center, and every thirty minutes someone walks the line to bring the birds in. Cyrus wants to be cleared to take birds out of the nets on his own before fall migration, and Ruth, who runs the banding station, has been watching his hands.

On Monday he was half asleep on the first run and walked right past a chickadee hanging in net two, and Ruth found it on her way back. On Tuesday he tried a jolt instead and jogged the whole trail before the first check. His hands shook so much on a goldfinch’s tangled foot that Ruth had to take over.

Alertness works more like a dial than a switch. A small cluster of cells in the brainstem called the locus coeruleus helps set it, using a chemical messenger called norepinephrine. Short bursts of firing seem to sharpen attention. Too little leaves everything foggy and slow, and too much steady firing turns jittery and scattered. The useful zone is in between, alert but not panicked. On Monday his dial was too low, and on Tuesday he cranked it past the middle.

On Wednesday he aims for a small shift. He gets up off the bench, rolls his shoulders back and walks the gravel loop briskly for thirty seconds. Then he spends a minute facing the brightest part of the sky over the field, never the sun itself, and splashes cool water from his bottle on his face. After that he gives himself one job, which is the next net, one bird at a time, feet first.

On the 6:15 run he spots a song sparrow low in net three. He frees its feet and then each wing and has it in a bag in under a minute while Ruth only watches. At the banding table she takes out his training card and initials the first line.

  2. What changes itHe stands, walks briskly for thirty seconds and faces the bright sky.

Try it yourself · 2 minutes

Foggy, jittery or in between

This is a two-minute check on where your dial is right now, with one small move to shift it. You’ll need the timer below.

  1. Read the three rows in the box and pick the one that sounds most like you right now. Give your dial a number from 1, which is foggy, to 10, which is jittery.
  2. If you’re on the foggy side, stand up, roll your shoulders back and walk briskly or march in place until the timer rings. Then look toward a window or a bright lamp for a few breaths, but never at the sun.
  3. If you’re on the jittery side, skip the movement. Sit with your feet flat and let your breathing slow down until the timer rings.
  4. If you’re already in the middle, start the timer anyway and use it to begin one task.
  5. Give your dial a number again.
FoggyHeavy eyelids, slumping, rereading the same line, slow to answer
Useful zoneAwake, steady hands, attention stays where you put it
JitteryTapping, shallow breaths, racing thoughts, hands not quite steady

The timer needs JavaScript. A phone stopwatch works just as well.

What you’ll probably notice. Once the signs have names, most people can place themselves on the dial in a few seconds, and a small move can shift the number. The direction matters, since movement and light nudge you up from foggy, which makes them the wrong move when you’re already jittery. That’s the dial the locus coeruleus helps set with norepinephrine, where too little feels foggy, too much steady firing feels scattered, and the useful zone is alert but not panicked.

Step by step

Turn the dial, don’t flip it

Run through it before practice, class or a hard task.

  1. Read your dial first. Notice if you’re foggy and slow or jittery and scattered, because the fix depends on which end you’re at.
  2. Stand up and change your posture. Get off the bed, the couch or the floor, and put your shoulders back with your feet under you.
  3. Add thirty seconds of brisk movement. Walk fast down the hall, take the stairs at a walking pace or march in place. Aim for a small shift, not a sprint.
  4. Get bright light in your eyes. Look toward a window, the outdoors or a bright lamp, and never stare at the sun. Cool water on your face is an optional extra.
  5. Aim it at one task. Name the one thing for the next ten minutes and start it while the shift is fresh.
  6. If you’re jittery, downshift instead. Skip the movement and the light. Sit down, breathe slowly and let it settle before you start.

How to tell it’s working. Every day this week, rate your dial from 1 for foggy to 10 for jittery before and after the shift, and write down whether the task started on time. You’re aiming for the middle of the scale and a start that doesn’t slip.

When it gets hard. The easy mistake is overshooting with a sprint or a blast of cold water, and your hands end up shaky. If that happens, stop, sit for a minute and let it come down before you start.
