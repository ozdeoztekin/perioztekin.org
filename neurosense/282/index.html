Make the Reminder Arrive Before the Routine Wins

Prospective Memory & External Reminders

Knowing a plan is not the same as having it come back when you can act. A well-placed reminder can help.

The Science

  • A plan makes two demands: keeping it available, and bringing it back when it can change what you do. That second part is prospective memory.
  • A goal's neural activity competes with activity drawn to sights, sounds and other thoughts; prefrontal control can bias that contest toward the goal.
  • Routine behavior can continue while attention is elsewhere: you can leave home knowing a borrowed book must go back and still leave it on the table.

The Protocol

  • Write the action itself into the reminder, such as "take the borrowed book", rather than only "remember".
  • Set it for a moment when you can act, such as before you leave home, not after you have already gone.
  • If you cannot act when it appears, move it to the next real opportunity rather than trying to remember the dismissed one.

See it in real life

Example

Sabine’s dress on the back of the door

Sabine’s green dress has hung on Flora’s bedroom door since the winter formal, and Sabine needs it back for her cousin’s wedding on Saturday. Sabine lives three houses up from the bus stop, so all Flora has to do is drop it off on the way.

On Wednesday Flora remembered it at breakfast and again on the bus, with the dress still on the door. On Thursday the same thing happened, even with a note on her mirror that said SABINE in purple marker. She looked right at it while doing her hair and wondered what Sabine had to do with anything.

Remembering a plan asks two things, keeping it available and bringing it back when it can change what you do, and the second is called prospective memory. A goal’s activity in the brain competes with everything else pulling at attention, and the prefrontal cortex, behind the forehead, can tip that contest toward the goal, which matters most when the thing isn’t in sight. Meanwhile a familiar routine runs on its own, carrying her out the door and down to the bus while her mind is on a chemistry quiz.

A note with only a name left her working out what she meant. That night she sets a phone reminder that names the action, “Take Sabine’s dress off the door,” for 7:20, when she’s dressed and still home, not for lunch, when she remembered best and could do nothing about it. On Friday it goes off while she’s braiding her little sister’s hair, so instead of swiping it away and promising to remember, she pushes it eight minutes on, to when she’ll be at the door.

At the door the phone buzzes again. She takes the dress down, zips it into its garment bag and hangs it on Sabine’s doorknob on her way to the bus. On Sunday, Sabine sends a photo from the wedding of herself in the dress, her littlest cousin on her hip.

  1. Where it stallsTwice she walks past Sabine’s house with the borrowed dress still at home.
  2. What changes itHer reminder names the action and goes off before she leaves.
  3. What happens nextThe dress is on Sabine’s doorknob before the bus comes.

Try it yourself · one day

Two errands, one reminder

Try this on an ordinary day when you’ll be out of the house for a while. A phone or a watch that can set a reminder is all you need.

  1. This morning, choose two small things to do later that aren’t part of your routine. One is to put a spoon on your desk as soon as you get home, and the other is to set a glass of water by your bed before dinner.
  2. For the spoon, write nothing down and plan to remember.
  3. For the glass, set a reminder that names the action, like “glass of water by my bed,” timed for a moment when you’ll be home and free to do it.
  4. If the reminder goes off when you can’t act, move it to your next real chance rather than dismissing it.
  5. Before bed, check your desk and the spot by your bed.

What you’ll probably notice. Plenty of people find the desk empty, even though they knew about the spoon all morning. Knowing a plan and having it come back at the right moment are separate jobs, and coming home, dropping your bag and heading for the kitchen is a routine that can carry you right past it. The reminder doesn’t make your memory any better. It brings the plan back at a moment when you can act on it.

Step by step

Remind yourself while you can act

For one-off errands that don’t fit your routine, like returning something, handing in a form or stopping at a store on the way home.

  1. Write the action itself. Name exactly what to do, like “take the library book off my desk,” rather than a word or a name you’ll have to puzzle out later.
  2. Time it for when you can act. Pick a moment when you’re in the right place to do it, such as before you leave home, not after you’re already gone.
  3. Make sure the cue reaches you. Use a phone reminder, an alarm or a note on the door you’ll walk out of, so it catches you at that moment.
  4. Move it if you can’t act yet. If it goes off at a bad moment, push it to your next real chance rather than telling yourself you’ll remember.
  5. Fix the one that slipped. When an errand gets missed anyway, look at whether the wording or the timing let you down, and change that one next time.

How to tell it’s working. For two weeks, tally the one-off errands you set up this way and the ones you planned to remember, and see how many of each got done. Reminders can still be missed, so you’re looking for fewer slips, not none.

When it gets hard. The most common slip is swiping a reminder away with a plan to do it in a minute. If you can’t act right then, move it on the spot, while the phone is still in your hand.
