Why Helpful Feedback Can Feel Like an Attack

Wise Feedback & Threat Appraisal

A high bar may feel more manageable when feedback also brings sincere belief and real support.

The Science

  • Harsh criticism can feel like a threat even though the blow is social, depending partly on an appraisal: does this demand outrun my resources?
  • If a demand feels bigger than what you can handle, your body can brace for damage, keeping more blood near your core and releasing cortisol.
  • Resources can be confidence, someone to turn to, training or time. Wise feedback sets a high standard and supplies sincere belief and support.

The Protocol

  • Name what needs changing, such as a paragraph that needs a clearer reason, and the high standard you hold the work to.
  • Say that you believe the person can reach that standard, and only say it if you mean it.
  • Offer specific help, such as working through one example together, and then actually give it.

See it in real life

Example

Twenty-three notes on Bettina’s case

Ilse wrote twenty-three comments in red on Bettina’s first debate case, most of them around the second contention, which claims free buses would cut downtown traffic without saying why. At Tuesday’s practice, in front of the team, Ilse handed it back and said it wasn’t ready for regionals.

Bettina skipped Wednesday’s practice and messaged the coach that maybe she should sit this tournament out, leaving the team a debater short. Ilse meant every comment, and the contention does need work, but she never meant for a freshman to quit.

Harsh criticism isn’t only information. How it lands depends partly on whether the demand seems to outrun your resources, and resources can be confidence, someone to turn to, training or time. Bettina has four days, no training in building an argument and nobody she feels she can ask. When a demand looks that much bigger than what you can handle, the body can shift into a threat response, bracing for damage by keeping more blood near the core and releasing cortisol, even though the blow is social. Wise feedback keeps the bar high and supplies resources, a sincere belief that the person can meet it and help to get there.

On Thursday Ilse finds Bettina at her locker and tries again. “Your second contention needs a clearer reason. At regionals every claim gets pushed in cross-examination, so each one has to hold up. Your first contention is the clearest on our team, so I know you can build this one. Can we work through a reason together at lunch?” She means it, and at lunch they build the first reason one sentence at a time.

Bettina writes the reasons for her other contentions herself that night. On Saturday, in round two, the other team’s first question in cross-examination goes straight at her second contention. Bettina answers it in two sentences, and Ilse writes “held” beside it in her notes.

  1. Where it stallsAfter twenty-three red comments, the freshman talks about sitting out the tournament.
  2. What changes itIlse names the fix, the bar, her belief in Bettina and the help.
  3. What happens nextBettina rewrites two contentions herself and holds up under cross-examination.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

Two notes on one draft

Have a pen and some paper handy, along with the two sliders below.

  1. Write three sentences arguing for something you care about, like a later curfew, a skate park in your town or the best movie of the year.
  2. Read note A as if a teammate had written it about your sentences. Notice what your shoulders and jaw do, and use the first slider to rate how much you want to rewrite.
  3. Read note B the same way, and rate it on the second slider.
  4. Compare the two numbers, and underline what note B has that note A doesn’t.
Note AThis doesn’t work. Your reason is weak, and the whole thing is vague.
Note BYour second sentence needs a clearer reason. I hold arguments to the standard of convincing someone who disagrees, and I think you can get there. Want to work out one reason together?

Both notes point at the same problem.

The timer needs JavaScript. A phone stopwatch works just as well.

What you’ll probably notice. Note A often brings a small brace, like a tight jaw or an urge to explain yourself, and the rewrite starts to look like a bigger job than it is. Note B names the same problem and keeps the same bar, and it adds two resources, a belief that you can meet the bar and an offer of help. When a demand seems to outrun what you have to meet it, the body can tip toward a threat response, and resources like these may make the same demand feel more manageable. Neither note is a magic phrase, and the help in note B only counts if someone follows through on it.

Step by step

Four parts, then follow through

Save it for the next time you give someone notes on their work, like a teammate’s case, a friend’s drawing or a sibling’s essay.

  1. Name one thing that needs changing. Pick the change that matters most and say it specifically, like “this paragraph needs a clearer reason,” where you might have said “this is vague.”
  2. Say the standard out loud. Tell them the bar you hold the work to and why it’s there, like “at the tournament, every claim gets pushed.” Keep the bar where it is.
  3. Say you believe they can reach it. Only say it if you mean it, and point to something they’ve already done that tells you so.
  4. Offer one specific piece of help. Make it concrete and give it a time, like “I’ll work through the first reason with you at lunch tomorrow.”
  5. Then give the help. Show up when you said you would. Help that never arrives says more than anything in the note.

How to tell it’s working. For two weeks, keep track of the notes you give and how many show up in the next draft. Compare that with how often your notes got used before you added the belief and the help.

When it gets hard. If you don’t believe yet that they can reach the bar, don’t say you do. Offer more help or more time, since there’s no magic phrase and people can usually tell when a line is empty.
