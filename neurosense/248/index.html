Are You Judging an Idea Before You’ve Made It?

Divergent & Convergent Thinking

Dreaming up ideas and picking one are closer to opposites, and exploring means holding judgment back.

The Science

  • Divergent thinking spreads out, as in listing everything a pen could be in three minutes; a pen propping a door open is a new direction.
  • Convergent thinking narrows down: given wing, water and engine, you land on one real thing that combines them, a plane that lands on water.
  • In one proposed model, areas at the front of the brain help multiply options by loosening context, while executive control narrows them.

The Protocol

  • Spend five to ten minutes letting thoughts surface without judging them.
  • Then spend five to ten minutes holding attention on one point, like your breath, coming back whenever it drifts.
  • On a creative task, list options first and save judging for later, rather than rejecting ideas as they appear.

See it in real life

Example

Eraser crumbs and the Night Bus poster

Night Bus has only ever played for their parents, and their first real show is a week from Saturday on the patio of the taco place on Grand Street. Liesel promised them a poster by Wednesday, and after an hour at her desk she has two thumbnails and a small hill of eraser crumbs.

Every idea dies halfway. A bus is too obvious, a moon is what every band uses, and headlights would be great if she could draw cars. She’s judging each idea at the same moment she’s trying to have it.

Coming up with ideas and choosing one feel like a single job, but they’re closer to opposites. Divergent thinking spreads out, like listing everything a pen could be, where propping a door open counts as a new direction. Convergent thinking narrows down, the way wing, water and engine lead to a plane that lands on water. In one proposed model, areas behind the forehead help multiply options by loosening context, while executive control narrows them. Exploring means holding judgment back, and Liesel has been doing both at once.

So she splits them up. First she practices switching, with eight minutes on her bed letting thoughts come and go without judging them, then eight minutes on her breath, coming back each time it wanders. Then she puts the eraser in a drawer, sets a timer for fifteen minutes and draws every idea that shows up, bad ones included.

When the timer goes off she has 31 thumbnails, and she leaves them alone until morning. Then she tapes them to the wall and looks for a thumbnail that does three jobs at once, reading from across the street, printing in black on the library copier and feeling like the band. It’s a bus-stop sign with the show listed where the schedule goes. By Thursday forty copies are up around town, one inside the bus shelter on Grand Street.

  1. Where it stallsShe judges every idea as it appears and has two thumbnails in an hour.
  2. What changes itShe draws for fifteen minutes with the eraser in a drawer, judging nothing.
  3. What happens nextShe picks one of 31 thumbnails, and forty posters go up by Thursday.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

Judge it now or later

Use the two-minute timer below for both lists, and write on any scrap of paper.

  1. Start the timer and list every use you can think of for a pen. Cross nothing out, and keep going when the ideas get strange.
  2. Start it again for a paper cup, but this time give each idea a score from 1 to 5 and write the score down before you think of the next one.
  3. Count each list, counting only new directions, since red pen, blue pen and green pen are one idea in different colors, while a pen propping a door open is a new one.
  4. Now switch jobs. Read both lists and circle the single idea that would best keep a door from slamming in the wind, ruling out everything else.

The timer needs JavaScript. A phone stopwatch works just as well.

What you’ll probably notice. The scored list often comes out shorter, because every score stops the spreading out to do a narrowing job, and judging too early can cut exploring short before many options show up. The last step is the other half of the work, ruling things out until one idea fits, and it has more to work with when the list is long.

Step by step

Explore first, judge later

Try it on your next creative job, like a poster, a song, a costume or a name for something.

  1. Let thoughts come for a while. Spend five to ten minutes sitting or lying down, letting whatever comes up come and go without judging it.
  2. Then hold one point. Spend another five to ten minutes on your breath, bringing your attention back whenever it drifts. Doing the two back to back is practice for switching modes.
  3. List options with judgment off. On the task itself, set a timer and get every option down, obvious and strange, without crossing anything out.
  4. Judge in a separate session. Come back later, even the next morning, and only then compare the options and rule some out.
  5. Pick the one that fits every need. Write down what the result has to do, then look for the single option that does all of it at once.

How to tell it’s working. Count your options before you start choosing, and compare that with how many you usually end up with. If the list is longer and the one you pick fits more of what the job needs, keep the two sessions apart.

When it gets hard. The urge to judge tends to show up halfway through an idea, as “that’s dumb.” Write that thought in the margin and keep drawing or listing, since judging gets its own turn later.
