Why Opponents Seem Smarter When You Hear Them

Voice Versus Text in Disagreements

Heard aloud, the same words can make someone seem more thoughtful than in text, especially if you disagree.

The Science

  • When someone explains a political choice aloud, they can seem more thoughtful, intelligent and rational than with the same words in writing.
  • As you speak, pace and pitch can track your thinking in real time: you may speed up when excited and slow down when working something out.
  • Listeners hear those shifts as a mind at work. Text strips them out, and people do not seem to realize this.

The Protocol

  • If a group project decision bothers you, try explaining your reason in a voice message.
  • Before writing someone off from their text, hear them out by call or voice message.
  • Judge the argument on its merits, since a thoughtful-sounding voice does not make it correct.

See it in real life

Example

Two minutes of Tobin thinking out loud

“Switching the intake to the roller. Rebuild starts Saturday.” Tobin posted it in the robotics team chat at eleven at night, two weeks before the regional, and when someone asked why, he answered, “Claw’s too slow. Not debating it.”

Rafa spent a month writing the code that runs the claw, and in practice it grabs the ball nine times out of ten. Reading those two lines, he decides Tobin hasn’t thought about it at all. He has rewritten his reply three times, each version longer and sharper than the last, and he hasn’t said a word to Tobin since.

Text leaves a lot out. When people talk, their pace and pitch can move with their thinking, speeding up when they’re excited and slowing down when they’re working something out, and listeners hear that as a mind at work. Heard aloud, someone explaining a political choice can seem more thoughtful and rational than the same words in writing, especially to a listener who disagrees, and people don’t seem to realize how much a typed message strips out. None of that makes a spoken argument right.

Rafa deletes his draft and records a voice message, explaining why he’d keep the claw and pausing to get a number right. Then he asks Tobin to send one back. Tobin’s message runs two minutes. He speeds up describing the roller, then slows way down, with a long pause, while he works through what happens when a defender shoves the robot on the ramp. Rafa can hear him thinking, and he stops writing Tobin off. Whether Tobin is right is a separate question.

At Saturday’s practice they test it. A teammate plays defense while the robot runs twenty cycles with the claw and twenty with a roller borrowed from last year’s robot. The claw drops the ball six times under pushing, and the roller drops it once. That night Rafa starts rewriting his code for the roller.

  1. Where it stallsReading two short texts, Rafa decides Tobin hasn’t thought it through.
  2. What changes itHe sends a voice message and asks Tobin to send one back.
  3. What happens nextThey run twenty cycles each under defense, and the numbers decide.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

Type it, then say it

Pick a small opinion you’d defend, like the best seat in the car or the right time to do homework. You need your phone’s voice recorder.

  1. Type your reason in two or three sentences, the way you’d post it in a group chat.
  2. Without looking at what you typed, explain the same reason out loud into the recorder for about thirty seconds.
  3. Play it back and listen for where you speed up and where you slow down or pause to find a word.
  4. Read your typed version again and look for any sign of those moments.

What you’ll probably notice. The recording usually has speed-ups where you care and slow patches where you’re working something out, and the typed version carries the same reasons with none of that. Listeners hear those shifts as a mind at work, and text strips them out. Because you knew what you meant, the typed version probably felt complete to you, and that gap is easy to miss from the inside.

Step by step

Hear them out before you decide

For the next disagreement that starts in a group chat, about a project, a team plan or a decision someone made without you.

  1. Say your reason out loud. When a decision bothers you, record your reason in a voice message so the other person can hear the thinking behind your disagreement.
  2. Ask to hear theirs. Before you write someone off from a text, ask for a call or a voice message, like “Can you send me a voice note on why?”
  3. Leave your pauses in. You don’t need to sound smooth. When you pause to pick a word, the listener can hear you working it out.
  4. Judge the argument on its own. A thoughtful-sounding voice doesn’t make an idea right, and a flat text doesn’t make it wrong. Check the reasons, with a test or the facts if you can.

How to tell it’s working. For your next few disagreements, rate from 0 to 10 how much thought the other person seems to have put in, once after their text and again after hearing them. Keep a separate note of whether their argument changed your mind, since those two can move apart.

When it gets hard. Some people would rather type, and some things belong in writing, like times, addresses and lists. Use voice for the reasons and text for the details, and respect someone who prefers text.

Good to know. This is for ordinary disagreements. If someone is cruel or threatening, you don’t owe them a hearing, so save the messages and tell an adult you trust.
