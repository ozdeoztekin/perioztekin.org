Why Eating Boring Food Might Be Making You Anxious

Microbiome Diversity

The link between your gut microbes and your anxiety is direct, measurable, and largely controlled by what you put on your plate.

The Science

  • Cryan & Dinan (Nature Reviews Neuroscience, 2012): the gut microbiota communicates with the brain via the vagus nerve, immune signaling, and microbial metabolites. The pathway is bidirectional and modulates mood, anxiety, and cognition.
  • Foster & McVey Neufeld (Trends in Neurosciences, 2013): low microbiome diversity is associated with elevated anxiety and depressive symptoms in human studies, and germ-free animal models show clear behavioral and HPA-axis alterations.
  • Yatsunenko et al. (Nature, 2012): microbiome composition is shaped substantially by diet, narrow diets produce narrow strain populations; diverse plant intake produces diverse, resilient microbial ecosystems.

The Protocol

  • Aim for 30 different plant foods per week. Each distinct plant counts ONCE.
  • Examples that count: apple, almonds, paprika, oats, kale, blueberries, parsley, chickpeas, sunflower seeds, all separate items.
  • Add 1 serving of fermented food per day: kimchi, sauerkraut, plain yogurt, kefir, miso.
  • Reduce ultra-processed food, it feeds less helpful bacteria.

See it in real life

Example

Peas in the mac and cheese

Monday is buttered noodles, Tuesday quesadillas, Wednesday frozen pizza, Thursday boxed mac and cheese. Rafael has cooked all of it for his brothers, Benny and Arlo, since his mom started working evenings. Lately his mood goes flat by evening on the noodle-and-pizza days, and he can’t tell whether that’s real.

On Sunday he counts the different plants they ate all week, on the grocery pad on the fridge. Wheat in the noodles and tortillas, tomato in the sauce, corn, potato, apple, banana. Six.

The gut holds trillions of microbes, and different ones specialize in different fibers, so eating the same few things can shrink the range of jobs they do. Microbes turn fiber into short-chain fatty acids that help tune the gut lining, immune activity and inflammation, and the brain keeps reading the body’s state through immune signals, hormones and the vagus nerve. Low microbial variety has been linked with more anxiety and low mood. It isn’t food making serotonin; it’s the body sending a different status report.

He doesn’t change the menu, and nothing gets cut. He adds one new plant a day to what they already make: frozen peas in the mac and cheese, black beans in the quesadillas, spinach wilted into the noodles, garlic and oregano on the pizza. Each new plant goes on the pad, once.

The pad turns into a contest. Benny finds paprika and cinnamon and argues, correctly, that they count, and Arlo picks the vegetable at the store. By the next Sunday the pad says 21. Whatever that does in their guts will take weeks to show, if it shows at all. What Rafael can already see is Arlo asking for the bean quesadillas again.

  1. Where it stallsFour dinners on repeat, six different plants in a week
  2. What changes itOne new plant a day stirred in, each one on the list
  3. What happens nextTwenty-one plants by Sunday, and a request for the bean ones

Try it yourself · 10 minutes

Count your plants

You need a pen, paper and three days of memory. This is about noticing range; nothing here is about eating less.

  1. List every different plant you’ve eaten in the last three days, from meals, snacks and drinks. Each one counts once, however much you had.
  2. Translate food into plants: bread is wheat, fries are potato, salsa is tomato, onion and pepper. Herbs and spices count too.
  3. Circle the ones that show up almost every day.
  4. Then look through your kitchen and find five plants you haven’t eaten this week: a spice, a can of beans, frozen vegetables, oats, a bag of seeds.

What you’ll probably notice. Your list is likely shorter than you expected, with the same handful repeating, often wheat, potato and corn in different shapes. That narrow range is the kind of diet The Science above describes: fewer kinds of fiber arriving, so fewer kinds of microbes get fed. The kitchen hunt usually turns up several plants already in the house, which is why the plan adds instead of starting over.

Step by step

One new plant a day

Nothing gets cut. Everything here is something you add.

  1. Add one new plant a day. Berries, beans, oats, herbs, seeds, lentils, greens or nuts. Stir it into something you already eat instead of making a new meal.
  2. Keep a list where you’ll see it. On the fridge or in a phone note. Each different plant goes on once a week, and herbs and spices count.
  3. Aim for 30 different plants a week. Thirty is a target to aim at. Going from six to fifteen is already a much wider range.
  4. Add a fermented food if it suits you. Plain yogurt, kefir, kimchi, sauerkraut or miso, once a day, only if it fits your body and your taste.
  5. Give it a few weeks. Microbes shift over weeks. Keep the list going for two to four weeks before you judge anything.

How to tell it’s working. Count your list at the end of each week and compare it with the week before. Once a week, also write one line about your energy and mood, so after a month you can look at both side by side.

When it gets hard. The list usually stalls on busy days. Keep two easy adds in the house for those, like a bag of frozen mixed vegetables or a jar of mixed seeds; each one can put several plants on the list at once.

Good to know. If you have ongoing anxiety, low mood or stomach and digestion problems, talk to a doctor or a parent; adding plants is a good habit, but it isn’t a treatment. Skip anything you’re allergic to or that doesn’t agree with you.
