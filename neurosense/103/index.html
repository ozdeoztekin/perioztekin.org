Why Your Brain Can Rewire Around Damage

Neuroplasticity & Cortical Remapping

You would think that if part of the brain is damaged, that ability is just gone. Often it is not. The brain has a backup plan built into how it is wired.

The Science

  • Kleim & Jones (2008, J Speech Lang Hear Res): neural plasticity is experience-dependent, and specific, repeated, effortful practice is what drives the brain to reorganize.
  • Nudo (2013, Front Hum Neurosci): after brain injury, surviving regions can take over lost functions, and rehabilitation shapes this remapping.
  • Cortical maps are not fixed; use and practice redraw which circuits control which abilities.

The Protocol

  • Practice the exact skill you want to regain, not a vague substitute.
  • Use short, frequent sessions rather than rare long ones.
  • Work at the edge of difficulty, where change is triggered.
  • Protect sleep and rest, when the rewiring consolidates.

See it in real life

Example

Comic 62, drawn left-handed

The cast runs from Sam’s knuckles to her elbow, on the hand she draws with. For 61 Fridays in a row she has posted a four-panel comic about a pigeon named Gus. The doctor says six weeks in the cast.

On Wednesday she tries one panel with her left hand. It takes fifty minutes, and Gus’s head comes out shaped like a potato. On Saturday she sits down to do a whole strip at once and keeps going for three hours, until her hand cramps and she tears the page out.

Her brain isn’t injured, but it learns by the same rule a brain uses to recover. The maps that decide which circuits run which skills aren’t fixed; use and practice redraw them, and after a brain injury that’s how nearby regions can slowly pick up a lost job. What drives it is specific, repeated, effortful practice: the exact skill, in short, frequent doses, pushed to the edge of what’s hard. The change locks in during rest. Her three-hour Saturday was one long dose, mostly cramp.

The next week she breaks the comic into pieces she can practice in a few minutes: Gus’s head, a speech bubble, a straight panel border, one line of lettering. Ten minutes before school, ten after, ten before bed, always at the size she uses in the strip. When a line starts to wobble, she draws three more and stops until the next session.

By day nine Gus’s head takes four minutes instead of fifteen, and it looks like Gus again. Comic 62 goes up that Friday, a week late, with three panels and “drawn left-handed” in the corner. Comic 64 has all four, and it took an hour and forty minutes.

  1. Where it stallsOne wobbly panel in fifty minutes, then a three-hour Saturday and a cramp
  2. What changes itTen minutes, three times a day, on Gus’s head and the borders
  3. What happens nextComic 62 posted on Friday, and comic 64 back to four panels

Try it yourself · 10 minutes

Draw it in the mirror

In a mirror, the usual link between what you see and how you move gives the wrong answer, so you can watch a new one form. You need a mirror you can stand up, or a bathroom mirror at a counter, plus paper, a pen and a book to block your view.

  1. Draw a star about the size of your hand, then draw a second outline around it, a finger’s width outside the first, so you have a path to trace.
  2. Put the paper flat in front of the mirror. Hold the book above your drawing hand so you can see the paper only in the mirror.
  3. Trace the path all the way around with your usual writing hand, watching only the mirror. Time it, and count how often you leave the path.
  4. Trace it four more times, timing and counting the fifth.
  5. Compare the first try with the fifth.

The timer needs JavaScript. A phone stopwatch works just as well.

What you’ll probably notice. The first try is usually slow and jerky, because every time the line turns, your hand heads the wrong way. By the fifth, most people are faster and leave the path less. The problem is like the one in the video, minus the damage: the usual route gives the wrong answer, and only repeating this exact task builds a new one. Rebuilding a real skill takes far more repetitions than five, spread over weeks, with rest in between.

Step by step

Rebuild one skill in short doses

Pick one skill you need back, or one you need to build with a different hand.

  1. Practice the exact skill. Not a vague substitute. If you need to write with your other hand, practice the words you actually write, at the size you write them.
  2. Break it into small pieces. Pieces you can practice in a few minutes each: one letter shape, one chord change, one movement.
  3. Keep sessions short and often. Ten minutes, two or three times a day, instead of one long session on the weekend.
  4. Stop at the edge of hard. Work where it’s difficult but still possible. When it starts to wobble, do a few more and stop.
  5. Rest so it can set. Sleep and breaks are when the change consolidates. Spread the sessions out instead of stacking them back to back.

How to tell it’s working. Time one piece on the first day and again once a week, and keep the first try next to the latest. If the time drops and the piece starts to feel automatic after a few weeks, it’s working.

When it gets hard. The middle days feel like nothing is changing. Compare with day one, not with yesterday, and keep the sessions short even when you feel like pushing for an hour.

Good to know. Follow your doctor’s instructions about an injured hand or arm. After a stroke or any brain injury, practice should be planned with a rehabilitation team, and how much comes back is different for every person.
