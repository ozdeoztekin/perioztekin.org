Why New Words Need Your Mouth

Speech as a Motor Skill

New words do not only live in your ears; they need your mouth too, as movement.

The Science

  • Speech is a movement skill built from breath, lips, tongue, timing, and sound.
  • Hearing a language gives the brain patterns, but speaking turns those patterns into motor commands.
  • Real conversation adds rhythm, emotion, correction, and context that silent app exposure cannot.

The Protocol

  • Say new words slowly, then normally, then inside a sentence you would actually use.
  • Use conversation when you can, not only silent app practice.
  • Treat your accent as history and practice data, not failure.

See it in real life

Example

Two bolillos and a concha

Rhys has a 186-day streak on his language app and has said maybe ten words of Spanish out loud. On Saturday mornings he works the counter at a bakery where half the regulars order in Spanish.

Last Saturday he called Lupe over from the ovens seven times. When Mr. Ruiz asked for “dos bolillos y una concha,” Rhys understood every word and still said, “Sorry, one second,” and went to get her. On the app he taps the right answer in two seconds. At the counter, with a line, nothing comes out.

Every one of those 186 days was silent. Speech is a movement skill, built from breath, lips, tongue and timing. Hearing a language gives the brain its patterns, but speaking is what turns them into motor commands, and his mouth has barely practiced. A real person adds what a silent app can’t: rhythm, correction and context.

In the slow hour, Lupe writes on a receipt the five lines he’d actually say at the counter: taking the order, anything else, the price, when the conchas come out, and goodbye. He says each one slowly, then at normal speed, then inside a real exchange with Lupe playing the customer. She stops him on “Las conchas salen en diez minutos,” which he’s saying one word at a time, and says it back as one smooth phrase. He copies the rhythm. His accent stays obvious. Lupe shrugs: that’s just where he’s from.

The next Saturday Mr. Ruiz is first in line. “¿Qué le doy?” Rhys asks. Two bolillos, Mr. Ruiz says, and are the conchas out? “Salen en diez minutos.” He grins at the accent and waits. Lupe gets called over once all morning, for a cake order.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

Three speeds, one word

Pick a word below, or a hard word from a language you’re learning, and find a recording of a native speaker saying it (a dictionary site with audio works). Your phone’s voice recorder helps.

  1. Play the recording three times and just listen. Don’t say it yet.
  2. Record yourself saying it once at full speed. Notice where your mouth trips.
  3. Say it slowly, one syllable at a time, three times. Feel where your tongue and lips go.
  4. Say it at normal speed three times, then three times inside a short sentence.
  5. Record it once more and play both recordings back.
Spanishtrabalenguas (tra · ba · len · guas): tongue twister
GermanEichhörnchen (eich · hörn · chen): squirrel
Frenchgrenouille (gre · nouille): frog

Sentences to try: Me encantan los trabalenguas. Da ist ein Eichhörnchen. J’ai vu une grenouille.

What you’ll probably notice. The first full-speed try usually trips in one spot, often on a sound English doesn’t use. By the second recording most people sound smoother, without hearing the word a single extra time. Listening gave you the pattern; saying it slowly, then normally, then in a sentence turned that pattern into movement your mouth can repeat.

Step by step

Get new words into your mouth

For a language you’re learning, a speech or lines for a role.

  1. Write the sentences you’ll really say. Five at most, for the place you’ll need them: a counter, a team, a dinner table, a stage. A real sentence gives every word a job.
  2. Slow, then normal, then in the sentence. Say each new word slowly enough to feel your tongue and lips move, then at normal speed, then inside its sentence. Three times each, out loud.
  3. Say them out loud every day. Run your five sentences once a day, on a walk or in the shower. Under your breath counts. Silent doesn’t.
  4. Practice with one real person. A coworker, a classmate, a relative or a conversation partner. Ask them to play the other side and to correct you, especially the rhythm.
  5. Treat your accent as data. Record yourself once a week. Your accent will show for a long time, and that’s history, not failure. Listen for one sound to work on next.

How to tell it’s working. Count how often you switch to English, point, or hand the conversation to someone else in the place where you need the language. If that number drops over two weeks, or a sentence comes out before you’ve rehearsed it in your head, it’s working.

When it gets hard. Your first real try will probably come out slower than in practice. If you freeze, say one of your five sentences, even a little wrong, and let the other person help with the rest.
