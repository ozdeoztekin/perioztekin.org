A Face Tells You Less Than You Think

Facial Expressions & Context

A face is a clue to what someone feels, not a readout. Your brain guesses the rest from the whole scene.

The Science

  • A scowl can go with anger, but it is not a reliable readout of what someone feels. A facial movement is a clue, not a one-to-one emotion label.
  • Your brain interprets a face in context, guessing from the whole scene: what is happening, the sounds, even signals from inside your own body.
  • Feeling, body and face often travel together but can split, and cultural habits also shape how much emotion people show.

The Protocol

  • Treat a face as one clue, and look at what is happening around the person before you decide what they feel.
  • If someone looks tense while carrying a heavy stack of books, ask what is happening before assuming they are angry.
  • Read a display in its social setting, and do not take a composed face or a small smile as proof of how someone feels.

See it in real life

Example

Five-dollar portraits at the book sale

Asha is drawing five-dollar portraits at the public library’s book sale, and every dollar goes toward new books. By noon she has drawn three strangers and apologized to all of them. Each time a face went stern, her pencil sped up, and the third sitter left with a drawing Asha knew was rushed.

Now a man in a fishing hat sits down with a tall stack of hardcovers in his lap, and from the first line he scowls. His jaw is tight, and his eyes narrow at her. Her heart has been thumping all morning, and she’s sure he already hates it. Her pencil starts to hurry, and she’s planning her apology.

A scowl can go with anger, but it isn’t a reliable readout of what someone feels, since a face is a clue and not a label. The brain makes sense of a face in context, guessing from the whole scene, including what’s happening, the sounds and even signals from inside your own body. Asha’s pounding heart is part of what she’s reading into his face.

Asha makes herself treat the scowl as one clue and look at the rest of the scene. His arms are locked around the books, and the afternoon sun has slid under the tent and onto his face. She stops and asks, “Do you want to set those down? Is the sun in your eyes?” He breathes out. “I’m trying not to move. You said hold still.” She stacks the books on her table and turns his chair into the shade, and his face loosens. She takes her usual ten minutes.

When she turns the pad around, he studies it with no expression at all, and she waits rather than apologizing. “My wife is going to frame this,” he says, and pays double. Her last sitter of the day, a girl frowning hard at a puzzle book, gets the full ten minutes too.

  1. Where it stallsA sitter scowls the whole time, and she’s sure he hates the drawing.
  2. What changes itShe looks at the scene, the heavy books and the sun, and asks.
  3. What happens nextHe relaxes in the shade, and she takes her usual ten minutes.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

One frown, five scenes

You’ll need a friend and the list of scenes below. One of you makes the faces, and the other guesses.

  1. Your friend secretly picks a scene from the list and makes the face they’d have in it, with no words or gestures.
  2. Looking only at the face, say what you think they’re feeling, and write your guess down.
  3. Your friend tells you the scene. Mark whether your guess fits it.
  4. Go through all five scenes in a mixed-up order, then swap jobs.
Scene 1Carrying a heavy stack of books up the stairs
Scene 2Annoyed at someone who cut in line
Scene 3Concentrating on a hard puzzle
Scene 4Reading the small print on a label
Scene 5The sun shining straight into your eyes

Keep the scene secret until the guess is written down.

What you’ll probably notice. A few of the guesses usually miss, because the same frown fits a heavy load, a hard puzzle, bright sun and annoyance. The face was a real clue, but without the scene you were guessing. Your brain does that kind of guessing all the time, using what’s happening around a person, so giving it the scene, or asking, gives it far more to go on.

Step by step

Read the scene, then ask

This is for the moments when a face makes you sure of something.

  1. Treat the face as one clue. When a face makes you sure someone is angry, bored or upset, hold that as a guess rather than a fact.
  2. Look at what’s happening around them. Check what they’re carrying, what they’re looking at and what they’re busy with. A tense look can come from a heavy load, bright sun or hard concentration.
  3. Check your own body too. If your heart is racing, that feeds into how faces look to you, so give your first guess less weight.
  4. Ask before you decide. Ask about the situation rather than their mood, like “Want a hand with those?” or “Is the sun in your eyes?”
  5. Don’t treat a calm face as proof either. A composed face or a small smile doesn’t measure what someone feels, and how much people show depends partly on their habits and the setting.

How to tell it’s working. For a few days, whenever a face makes you sure of something, write down your first guess and then what the scene or a question turned up. Count how often the first guess held.

When it gets hard. Asking can feel awkward, especially with adults. Ask about the situation, not the feeling, since “Can I help with that?” is a lot easier to say than “Are you mad at me?”
