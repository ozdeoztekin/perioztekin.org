Why Kids Stop Preparing When Warnings Keep Being Wrong

Cue Reliability & Proactive Control

When warnings keep proving wrong, a child may stop preparing, and that need not mean they lack the ability.

The Science

  • Children may prepare when a warning reliably predicts the next demand, yet wait and react when it repeatedly proves unhelpful.
  • Preparing ahead, called proactive control, holds the next rule in working memory; one theory says a brain region weighs its cost against the benefit.
  • Adults adjust too: after repeated demands that discourage preparing, they can keep reacting for a while, even once preparing becomes possible.

The Protocol

  • Make each cue predict what actually comes next, such as a raised hand that always means a turn is next.
  • Follow through every time you give the cue, so preparing has a reason to pay off.
  • When a child waits instead of preparing, check how reliable your recent warnings have been before judging ability.

See it in real life

Example

Coats still on at the whistle

At the first whistle of the second quarter, Hamza calls “Ruthie, you’re in,” and Ruthie is still on the bench in her puffy coat, eating an orange slice. The referee waits, then restarts play without her.

Hamza volunteers with the recreation center’s youngest basketball team on Saturday mornings, seven- and eight-year-olds in jerseys that hang to their knees, and his job is the subs. On the first day he told them that when his hand goes up, they’re in next. Three Saturdays later the referee waits on his bench every quarter, and Hamza has started to think the little ones can’t handle subs on their own.

Getting ready ahead of time, called proactive control, means holding the next step in mind, and that takes effort. Kids tend to make the effort when a warning reliably predicts what comes next and to wait and react when it keeps proving wrong, which doesn’t mean they can’t do it. One theory says a part of the brain weighs the cost of getting ready against the payoff before it commits.

That night he thinks back over the game. His hand went up about ten times, and maybe half of those turned into a sub at the next whistle. The rest were kids the head coach then kept on the floor, or kids Hamza forgot. The next Saturday he checks with the head coach before every sub and raises his hand only when that kid is going in at the very next whistle. If the plan changes, his hand stays down.

For the first quarter the kids still wait for their names. By halftime, when his hand goes up, a coat is already coming off. Late in the game Ruthie is standing at the scorer’s table with her shoes tied before the ball even goes out of bounds, and the referee waves her in.

  1. Where it stallsWhen his hand goes up, the next player is still in her coat.
  2. What changes itHe raises his hand only when that kid goes in at the next whistle.
  3. What happens nextBy halftime, coats come off the moment his hand goes up.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

Warnings you trust

Grab a pen and paper. This one is about the warnings in your own week, so you have nothing to set up.

  1. Write down four warnings you hear often, like a bus app’s “arriving in 2 minutes,” a parent’s “dinner in five,” a coach’s “last drill” or a teacher’s “this will be on the test.”
  2. Next to each one, write whether it comes true almost always, about half the time or hardly ever.
  3. Next to that, write what you do when you hear it, whether you get ready right away or wait to see what happens.
  4. Look down both columns and see whether they line up.

What you’ll probably notice. Lists like this tend to line up. The warnings that almost always come true are the ones you act on, like grabbing your bag when the bus app says two minutes, and the hardly-ever ones are the ones you wait out. Getting ready costs effort, so a warning’s track record helps decide whether it seems worth it. Kids make the same call, and a kid who waits after a run of wrong warnings may be making a sensible bet.

Step by step

Make your signals mean something

For anyone who gives kids a heads-up, like a coach, a babysitter, a camp counselor or an older sibling.

  1. Tie one cue to one thing. Choose a signal and link it to exactly what comes next, like a raised hand meaning that player goes in at the next whistle, or “two minutes” meaning shoes on in two minutes.
  2. Follow through every time. Whenever you give the cue, make the thing happen, so getting ready has a reason to pay off.
  3. Hold the cue when you’re unsure. If the plan might still change, wait before you give the signal, since every cue that proves wrong gives kids another reason to wait.
  4. Check your track record first. When a kid waits rather than getting ready, count how many of your recent cues came true before you decide they can’t do it.
  5. Give it a few sessions. Don’t expect a change in one session. Even adults, after a run of demands that made preparing pointless, can keep reacting for a while once preparing makes sense again.

How to tell it’s working. For a few sessions, keep a tally of how many cues came true and how often the kid was ready before you called their name. If both numbers climb, the cue is starting to mean something.

When it gets hard. The hard moment is when the plan changes right after you’ve given the cue. Follow through if you possibly can, and if you can’t, tell the kid right away that the plan changed, so the signal itself keeps its meaning.
