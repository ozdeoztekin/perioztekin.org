Why Effort Can Feel Better Later

Endorphins

After practice, dancing, laughing, or a hard walk, your body may feel lighter later. Effort can feel rough at first, then leave the body lighter afterward.

The Science

  • Pert and Snyder (1973), Science: the brain has opioid receptors, the docking sites for internal relief signals.
  • Hughes et al. (1975), Nature: the brain makes its own opioid peptides (endorphins) that act on those receptors.
  • Boecker et al. (2008), Cerebral Cortex: endurance exercise releases opioids in the human brain, but the runner's high is multi-system, not endorphins alone.
  • Effort can feel rough at first, then leave the body lighter later.

The Protocol

  • Aim for safe effort, not chasing pain: about ten to twenty minutes.
  • Walk briskly, dance, cycle, swim, or do bodyweight work.
  • Keep it challenging, not punishing, because pain is a stop signal.
  • Notice the signal, name the mechanism, and change one input before autopilot.

See it in real life

Example

Thirty rereads of one comment

“Dancing like a robot with a dead battery.” The comment sits under Ana’s newest dance cover, the one she spent two weekends learning, between two nice ones from people she actually knows. Since dinner she has read it about thirty times.

Her jaw is tight and her shoulders are up near her ears. Twice her thumb has hovered over Delete video. She’s typed three replies and erased all of them, and every reread lands as hard as the first.

Effort tends to feel rough at the start and leave the body lighter afterward. Part of that is chemistry the brain makes itself: endorphins, opioid peptides that act on opioid receptors as relief signals for pain and stress, and exercise can release them in the brain. They’re not the whole story, since other systems join in, and laughing and play can call up relief too.

She sets the phone face down on her dresser, plays the song from the video on her speaker, and dances the routine three times through, then two more songs, about fifteen minutes. The first run is awful. She’s stiff and angry and almost quits after the first chorus. By the third she’s sweating, and when she overshoots the spin and lands on her bed, she laughs out loud.

She drinks a glass of water and sits on the floor until her breathing slows. Her shoulders have come down on their own. When she picks up the phone, the comment reads like something a stranger typed in four seconds, because it was. She hides it, leaves the video up, and before bed she’s learned the first sixteen counts of the next one.

Try it yourself · 10 minutes

Rough first, lighter after

A before-and-after check on your own body. You need two or three songs you like and room to move, indoors or out.

  1. Before you move, rate how tight or heavy your body feels: shoulders, jaw, chest.
  2. Dance to the songs, or walk briskly outside or up and down a hallway. Aim for breathing harder but still able to talk.
  3. At the end of the first song, notice how it feels. For a lot of people this is the worst part. Keep going unless something hurts, because pain means stop.
  4. When the music ends, drink some water and stand or sit still for a few minutes while your breathing settles.
  5. Rate again, and check: are your shoulders lower, is your jaw looser?

The timer needs JavaScript. A phone stopwatch works just as well.

What you’ll probably notice. Moving tends to feel hardest in the first few minutes and lightest in the few minutes afterward. That after-state is the point: relief signals like endorphins, along with other systems, settling in once the effort stops. If the first song made you want to quit, that’s a common pattern, and it’s why the plan asks for ten to twenty minutes.

Step by step

Safe effort, then the after-state

  1. Pick movement your body can handle. A brisk walk, dancing in your room, cycling, swimming, or bodyweight moves like squats.
  2. Go for ten to twenty minutes. Set a timer so you aren’t deciding whether to stop every minute.
  3. Challenging, not punishing. Breathing harder is the goal. Pain is a stop signal, not a sign it’s working.
  4. Laughing and play count too. Dance with a sibling, walk with a friend, play a game that makes you run.
  5. Come down slowly afterward. Water, a few easy breaths, and a few minutes to come down before the next thing.
  6. Notice the after-state. Are your shoulders lower, your mind less sharp-edged? Write one line about it.

How to tell it’s working. Rate how tight or sharp-edged you feel from 0 to 10 before each session and again fifteen minutes after. If the after number is lower on most days, the effort is paying off where you can feel it.

When it gets hard. The first minutes are when most people quit, because that’s when it feels worst. Decide the time before you start, and judge it only afterward.

Good to know. If you have an injury, a heart or breathing condition, or you get chest pain, dizziness or sharp pain while moving, stop and check with a doctor before you go on.
