Why Effort Can Feel Better Later

Endorphins

After practice, dancing, laughing, or a hard walk, your body may feel lighter later. Effort can feel rough at first, then leave the body lighter afterward.

The Science

  • Pert and Snyder (1973), Science: in animal brain tissue they found opioid receptors, docking sites used by opioid drugs and by the body's own relief signals.
  • Hughes et al. (1975), Nature: pig brain makes its own opioid peptides, called enkephalins, close cousins of the endorphins, and they act on those receptors.
  • Boecker et al. (2008), Cerebral Cortex: scans of ten athletes suggested a two-hour run released the brain's own opioids, linked to euphoria, but later work points to other systems too.
  • Effort can feel rough at first, then leave the body lighter later.

The Protocol

  • Aim for safe effort, not chasing pain: about ten to twenty minutes.
  • Walk briskly, dance, cycle, swim, or do bodyweight work.
  • Keep it challenging, not punishing, because pain is a stop signal.
  • Notice the signal, name the mechanism, and change one input before autopilot.

See it in real life

Example

Thirty rereads of one comment

“Dancing like a robot with a dead battery.” The comment sits under Ana’s newest dance cover, the one she spent two weekends learning, between two nice comments from people she actually knows. Since dinner she has read it about thirty times.

Her jaw is tight and her shoulders are up near her ears. Twice her thumb has hovered over the button to delete the video. She has typed three replies and erased all of them, and every time she rereads the comment it lands as hard as the first time.

Hard effort tends to feel rough at the start and leave the body lighter afterward. Part of that may come from chemicals the brain makes itself, called endorphins, which work on the same receptors as strong painkillers like morphine and act as relief signals for pain and stress. In one brain-scan study of ten athletes, a two-hour run seemed to release them. They aren’t the whole story, since other systems join in too, and laughing can bring on some of that relief as well.

She sets the phone face down on her dresser, plays the song from the video on her speaker and dances the routine three times through, then two more songs, about fifteen minutes in all. The first run is awful. She’s stiff and angry and almost quits after the first chorus. By the third run she’s sweating, and when she overshoots the spin and lands on her bed, she laughs out loud.

She drinks a glass of water and sits on the floor until her breathing slows, and her shoulders come down on their own. When she picks up the phone again, the comment reads like something a stranger typed in four seconds, which is what it was. She hides it, leaves the video up and learns the first sixteen counts of the next dance before bed.

  2. What changes itShe dances the routine for fifteen minutes and is laughing by the third run.
  3. What happens nextHer shoulders come down, she hides the comment, and the video stays up.

Try it yourself · 10 minutes

Rough first, lighter after

This is a before-and-after check on your own body. You’ll need two or three songs you like and room to move, indoors or out.

  1. Before you move, rate how tight or heavy your body feels, especially your shoulders, jaw and chest.
  2. Dance to the songs, or walk briskly outside or up and down a hallway. Aim for breathing harder while still being able to talk.
  3. At the end of the first song, notice how it feels, since for a lot of people this is the worst part. Keep going unless something hurts, because pain means stop.
  4. When the music ends, drink some water and stand or sit still for a few minutes while your breathing settles.
  5. Rate again, and check whether your shoulders are lower and your jaw is looser.

The timer needs JavaScript. A phone stopwatch works just as well.

What you’ll probably notice. Moving tends to feel hardest in the first few minutes and lightest in the few minutes afterward. That after-feeling is the point, and mood tends to lift even after short, easy sessions. Endorphins and other systems may play a part, though brain scans have found endorphin release after much longer or much harder workouts. If the first song made you want to quit, that’s common, and it’s why the plan asks for ten to twenty minutes.

Step by step

Safe effort, then the after-feeling

Turn to it when something has you wound up and your body feels tight.

  1. Pick movement your body can handle. Try a brisk walk, dancing in your room, cycling, swimming or bodyweight moves like squats.
  2. Go for ten to twenty minutes. Set a timer so you aren’t deciding whether to stop every minute.
  3. Make it challenging, not punishing. Breathing harder is the goal. Pain is a signal to stop, not a sign it’s working.
  4. Laughing and playing count too. Dance with a sibling, walk with a friend or play a game that makes you run.
  5. Come down slowly afterward. Drink some water, take a few easy breaths and give yourself a few minutes before the next thing.
  6. Notice the after-feeling. Check whether your shoulders are lower and your mind feels less sharp-edged, and write one line about it.

How to tell it’s working. Rate how tight or sharp-edged you feel from 0 to 10 before each session and again fifteen minutes after. If the after number is lower on most days, the effort is paying off where you can feel it.

When it gets hard. The first few minutes are when most people quit, because that’s when it feels worst. Decide the time before you start, and judge it only afterward.

Good to know. If you have an injury, a heart or breathing condition, or you get chest pain, dizziness or sharp pain while moving, stop and check with a doctor before you go on.
