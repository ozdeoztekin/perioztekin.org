The Rumination Switch

rumination to action

Thinking about the same problem again can feel productive because your mind is still working.

The Science

  • Rumination often stays abstract, asking why this always happens without building a next step.
  • That can keep attention on distress, and in a low mood it can weaken problem solving.
  • Concrete thinking is different, because it asks what, where, when, and next.

The Protocol

  • Write the repeating thought exactly once.
  • Turn it into one concrete question that starts with what or when.
  • Take one visible action that answers part of that question.

See it in real life

Example

Three restarts since Tuesday

By the third restart, Kenji has stopped calling it a scarf. Every time, it starts as a neat strip and grows into a fan, wider at the top with wavy edges. His grandfather turns 75 in two weeks, and Kenji promised him a crocheted scarf in the blue and orange of his old baseball team.

Since Tuesday he has spent every spare hour on it, or that’s how it feels. Mostly he lies on his bed asking himself the same thing, which is why he always ruins the things that matter. An hour goes by with his mind busy the whole time, and the fourth start is planned exactly like the third.

A busy mind can still be stuck in place. A loop like that asks why, and that kind of why has no next step in it. It keeps his attention stuck to the bad feeling while his problem solving gets weaker. Concrete thinking asks different questions, like what, where, when and what’s next.

On Friday he writes the why sentence once, on the back of the pattern. Under it he writes a question that starts with what, “What happens at the end of each row?” He doesn’t know the answer, so he does one thing he can check. He counts the stitches in the first five rows and writes down each number.

The numbers are 20, 21, 22, 23 and 24, one extra stitch every row, always at the edge, where he has been working into the turning chain as if it were a stitch. He clips a marker into the first and last stitch of every row, and on Saturday forty rows come out at exactly 20. On his birthday, his grandfather runs a hand along the straight edge and asks how many tries it took, and Kenji tells him four.

  1. Where it stallsHe makes three fan-shaped starts and spends hours asking why he ruins things.
  2. What changes itHe writes the thought once, asks what happens at the end of each row and counts.
  3. What happens nextHe finds one extra stitch a row, adds two markers and makes forty straight rows.

Try it yourself · 2 minutes

Why list, what list

Pick a small thing that keeps going wrong, like always running late or a room that never stays clean, not your biggest problem. You’ll need paper, a pen and the timer below.

  1. At the top, write “Why does this always happen?” and your small problem in a few words. Start the timer and write every answer that comes for 60 seconds.
  2. Draw a line, write “What’s one thing to try before tomorrow night?” and write answers for another 60 seconds.
  3. Put a check next to anything on either list that you could do in the next day.
  4. Count the checks in each list.

The timer needs JavaScript. A phone stopwatch works just as well.

What you’ll probably notice. The why list usually fills up with explanations and verdicts, like calling yourself lazy or bad at this, and very few of them get a check. The what list is often shorter and plainer, but most of it gets checks. Why questions keep circling the problem, while what questions point at a next step.

Step by step

From why again to what next

Bring it out the third time the same thought comes around.

  1. Catch the replay. When the same thought comes back again, treat that as the signal, since a busy mind can feel like work without moving anything.
  2. Write it down exactly once. Use the words your head uses, once, without adding to it.
  3. Turn it into a what or when question. Change “Why does this always happen?” into “What happens right before it goes wrong?” or “When is the next chance to try one change?”
  4. Do one visible thing that answers part of it. Choose something someone could watch you do, like testing one step, asking one person, writing one line or setting one alarm.
  5. Write what it showed you. Put the answer under the question, and when the thought comes back, read the page instead of starting the loop again.

How to tell it’s working. For a week, keep a tally of the loops you catch and how many of them end in a visible action. If the second number grows, or the same thought shows up less often, it’s working.

When it gets hard. Sometimes the what question is a why in disguise, like “What’s wrong with me?” If it doesn’t point at something you could do by tomorrow, shrink it until it does.

Good to know. If a loop like this is heavy, keeps you up at night or shows up most days, don’t try to outthink it alone. Tell a parent or a counselor what keeps coming back, and see a doctor if it’s costing you sleep.
