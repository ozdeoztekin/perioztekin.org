Give Sweat a Way to Evaporate

Sweat Evaporation & Heat Balance

Sweat cools you by evaporating, so a soaked shirt shows you are sweating, not that enough heat is leaving.

The Science

  • The hypothalamus integrates temperature signals from across the body and, when heat needs to leave, helps raise sweating and skin blood flow.
  • Sweat glands deliver water to the skin, but evaporation carries the heat away. Humid air makes that harder, and moisture-trapping clothing can too.
  • Working muscles keep producing heat, so cooling is a balance between heat arriving and heat leaving, not a contest to produce more sweat.

The Protocol

  • Before activity in heat, choose clothing that lets air and moisture escape, rather than trying to sweat harder.
  • If the air is very humid and sweat keeps accumulating, move somewhere cooler or less humid, or reduce your effort.
  • Do not use a wet shirt as permission to keep pushing: there is no single safe number of minutes, so change the conditions.

See it in real life

Example

The gray shirt that never dried

Keziah’s gray cotton shirt is soaked from collar to hem by the second game of Thursday’s pickup soccer, and slaps her back every time she turns. The fields are down by the river, it’s July, and the evening air is heavy and damp. To her, a shirt like that has always meant she’s working hard.

Tonight her legs say otherwise. By the fourth game she’s a step late to every loose ball and her face is bright red, and between games she stands on the sunny sideline because sitting down feels like quitting. When Tilda asks if she wants to sit one out, Keziah plucks at her shirt and says she’s sweating fine, so she’s fine.

Sweating is only the first step. Her hypothalamus pulls together temperature signals from across her body and, when heat needs to leave, helps turn up sweating and blood flow to her skin. But sweat carries heat away only when it evaporates, and humid air slows that, as does a heavy cotton shirt holding the sweat against her. Meanwhile her legs keep making heat with every sprint, so cooling is a balance between heat coming in and heat going out, and a soaked shirt can’t show which side is winning.

So she works on both sides of that balance. She swaps the cotton for the loose mesh jersey in her bag and spends the breaks in the shade by the fountain, where a breeze comes off the river. Her arms still won’t dry in the heavy air, so she sits out the fifth game with Tilda and plays the sixth at an easier pace, leaving the fastest forward to someone else.

The mesh gets wet too, but in the shade it dries at the shoulders, which the cotton never did. In the sixth she tracks back on defense every time, and when the streetlights come on, she’s the one who says they should call it.

  1. Where it stallsHer soaked cotton shirt feels like proof that she’s working hard.
  2. What changes itShe switches to mesh, rests in the breeze and sits out a game.
  3. What happens nextThe mesh dries in the shade, and she suggests they call it a night.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

Open spot, covered spot

You need a cup of room-temperature water, a small piece of plastic wrap or a sandwich bag, and a notebook to fan with.

  1. Dip a fingertip in the water and wet two coin-sized spots on the inside of one forearm, a few inches apart.
  2. Lay the plastic loosely over one spot and leave the other open to the air.
  3. Fan your forearm with the notebook for about a minute, and notice which spot feels cooler.
  4. Lift the plastic and check which spot is still wet.

What you’ll probably notice. The open spot usually feels cooler and starts to dry, while the covered one stays wet and feels about as warm as the rest of your arm. Both got the same water, but only the open spot could let it evaporate, and evaporation is what carries heat away. A soaked shirt in sticky air works more like the covered spot, with plenty of sweat that isn’t leaving.

Step by step

Help sweat leave, then adjust

Use this for practices, games or any hard activity in hot, sticky weather.

  1. Dress so sweat can escape. Before you play in the heat, choose a loose, light shirt that air can pass through, and skip layers that trap moisture. The aim is to let sweat evaporate, not to sweat harder.
  2. Rest somewhere cooler. Spend breaks in the shade, in a breeze or indoors where it’s cooler, and keep out of the direct sun.
  3. Ease off when the air is sticky. If it’s very humid and your sweat keeps dripping without drying, slow the pace, sit out a game or a drill, or move somewhere cooler. Less work means less heat added.
  4. Treat a wet shirt as information. A soaked shirt shows that you’re sweating, and that’s all it shows. There’s no safe number of minutes to aim for, so change the conditions rather than pushing on.
  5. Stop at the warning signs. If you feel dizzy, confused, sick to your stomach or unusually tired, or you get a headache, stop, get somewhere cool and tell a coach or an adult right away.

How to tell it’s working. During breaks, check whether your shirt dries at all, and notice how the last part of the session feels compared with similar days. If the sweat keeps pooling, it’s the conditions that need changing.

When it gets hard. On a sticky day it’s hard to sit out while everyone else plays. Agree with a friend beforehand that either of you can call a shade break, and take it when it’s called.

Good to know. This isn’t a treatment for heat illness, and there’s no safe number of minutes in the heat. If you or a teammate feels dizzy, confused, faint or sick in the heat, stop, get somewhere cool and tell a coach or an adult right away, and if you have a health condition, ask a doctor how heat affects you.
