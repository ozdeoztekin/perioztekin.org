Why Chili Burns, and What Can Help

Capsaicin, TRPV1 & Chili Burn

Spicy heat starts as a chemical signal, not a temperature reading, which is why even cool chili can burn.

The Science

  • Capsaicin, the pungent molecule in chili peppers, activates a channel called TRPV1 on sensory nerve endings in your mouth.
  • The channel lets positively charged ions into neurons that also help signal painful heat, so you can feel burning without the tissue heating up.
  • Skim and whole milk can both help reduce ordinary chili burn, and milk has performed better than room-temperature water.

The Protocol

  • If a bite is much hotter than you expected, stop adding more spicy food while the burn is building.
  • If dairy is suitable for you, take a small drink of cool skim or whole milk and swallow normally; water can also give some relief.
  • Give the burn time to settle before deciding whether to eat more, and let your own comfort, not anyone else's, set the amount.

See it in real life

Example

Room-temperature curry, still burning

Tarek’s first spoonful tastes like tomato and garlic, and then the back of his mouth catches fire. He’s never had dinner at Arturo’s house before, the curry Arturo’s dad made has been cooling on his plate for ten minutes, and Arturo’s little sisters are eating it like soup.

Tarek does what he always does with food that’s too spicy, which is to keep going so nobody notices. He takes two more quick bites and gulps half his water, which helps a little, but the burn keeps building until his eyes water. The last thing he wants is for Arturo’s dad to think he hates the food.

Nothing on the plate is hot, though. Capsaicin, the molecule that makes chili peppers pungent, opens a channel called TRPV1 on nerve endings in the mouth. Positively charged ions flow in and change the nerve’s electrical activity, and these nerves also help signal painful heat, so his brain reads burning while the food sits at room temperature. Swallowing doesn’t necessarily end it, since the chemical and the nerves’ response can outlast the mouthful, and each quick bite he added gave them more of the same trigger.

So he sets his spoon down and stops adding bites. Milk is fine for him, so he asks Arturo’s dad for a small glass of cold milk, takes a sip and swallows normally. The burn doesn’t vanish, but over the next few minutes it settles. He talks with Arturo about the weekend while it fades, and he spoons the green chili he finds in the sauce onto the rim of his plate.

Once his mouth has calmed down, he decides how much more he wants, and it’s less than the sisters are eating. He finishes with rice, bread and small spoonfuls of sauce, and when the cake comes out, he still has room for a slice.

  1. Where it stallsA bite much hotter than expected keeps burning, and he keeps eating.
  2. What changes itHe stops adding bites, sips cold milk and waits for the burn to settle.
  3. What happens nextHe finishes at his own pace and still has room for cake.

What you’ll probably notice. The cold foods on the chili list can still burn, because spicy heat starts as a chemical signal, not a temperature reading. Capsaicin opens a channel called TRPV1 on nerve endings in your mouth, and those nerves also help signal painful heat, so the brain gets a burning message from food that may be colder than the room. The same signal can keep going after you swallow.

Step by step

Stop, sip, wait, then decide

For an ordinary meal, when a bite is much hotter than you expected.

  1. Stop adding spicy bites. While the burn is building, put your spoon or fork down. Another spicy bite gives the nerves in your mouth more of the same trigger.
  2. Take a small sip of cool milk. If dairy is suitable for you, drink a little cool skim or whole milk and swallow normally. Both kinds can help, so you don’t need the richest one. Nobody has pinned milk’s effect on a single ingredient, and its coolness may play a part too.
  3. Water can help too. If milk isn’t an option, a drink of water can also give some relief. Milk has done better than room-temperature water, but no drink makes the burn vanish at once.
  4. Give the burn time to settle. Talk, wait and let it ease before you decide whether to eat more, since swallowing doesn’t always end it.
  5. Let your own comfort set the amount. Go on with the milder parts, like rice or bread, or with as much sauce as you enjoy. What a friend or a cousin can handle doesn’t decide how much you need.

How to tell it’s working. Next time a bite is hotter than expected, notice whether you stopped adding bites and roughly how long the burn took to settle. Compare that with a time you kept eating straight through it.

When it gets hard. At someone else’s table, the pull to keep eating so nobody notices is strong. A line like “It’s great, it’s hotter than I’m used to” buys you the pause without any fuss.

Good to know. Skip the milk if you’re allergic to it or can’t have dairy, and keep chili away from your eyes and skin. This is for the ordinary burn of a meal, never a dare or a challenge, and if you have trouble breathing, swelling or a burn that keeps getting worse, get an adult right away.
