Why a Walk Loosens a Stuck Idea

Walking and Divergent Thinking

If an idea gets tighter the longer you stare at it, more staring may not be the answer.

The Science

  • Walking can shift attention off one fixed route and open more associations.
  • The effect is strongest for generating ideas, not for every kind of problem.
  • Movement gives your mind a changing background while the question simmers.

The Protocol

  • Write one clear question before you stand up.
  • Walk for five quiet minutes without feeding your brain new input.
  • Capture three possible answers when you sit back down.

See it in real life

Example

The second verse of Porch Light

The first verse of “Porch Light” took Simone twenty minutes. The second has taken nine days. On Friday she’s playing it at the library’s open mic, her first original song in front of anyone, and so far it’s a verse, a chorus and a page of crossed-out lines.

The song is about waiting up for her mom on the nights she works late: the porch, the moths around the bulb, the street going quiet. Every line she tries for verse two lands back on that porch. Tonight, after forty minutes, the page shows the same two lines crossed out four ways. The harder she stares, the tighter it gets.

Staring has locked her onto one route, and every attempt runs down it. Walking can shift attention off a fixed route and open up more associations. It helps most with coming up with options rather than finding one exact answer, and a second verse is nothing but options. Moving also gives the question a changing background to simmer against.

She writes one question at the top of a clean page: What changes between verse one and verse two? She leaves her phone and earbuds on the bed and walks the block, five quiet minutes, past the bus stop and the laundromat, without trying to write in her head. When she sits back down, she writes three answers before she can judge them.

The sprinkler next door clicking on at eleven. Headlights sliding across her ceiling when her mom pulls in. The night she fell asleep before the car came. She picks the headlights, because they move. Twenty minutes later verse two is four lines long, and she sings the whole song into a voice memo for the first time.

  2. What changes itOne written question, then five quiet minutes around the block
  3. What happens nextThree answers, four new lines, the first full run-through

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

Song titles on your feet

You need your phone’s voice recorder and a hallway, a yard or a quiet sidewalk where you can walk for a couple of minutes.

  1. Sit facing a blank wall. Start a recording, read prompt 1, and say every title that comes to you. Stop when the recording reaches two minutes.
  2. Read prompt 2 and start a new recording. Put the phone in your pocket and walk at an easy pace, eyes up, for about two minutes out and back, saying every title that comes.
  3. Play both back. Count the titles in each, and mark the ones that are only a version of a title you’d already said.
  4. Another day, do the walk first, so a warm-up isn’t doing all the work.
Prompt 1Titles for a song about a rainy Saturday
Prompt 2Titles for a song about the last day of summer

What you’ll probably notice. On your feet, the list may run longer, and the walking titles tend to jump farther, pulling in a smell, a street or a memory, while the sitting ones circle the first idea. That fits The Science above: walking can shift attention off a fixed route and open more associations, and it helps most when the job is coming up with options. A math step with one right answer usually gets less out of a walk.

Step by step

Take the question for a walk

For stuck idea work, like a verse, a design, a name or an angle, and not for problems with one right answer.

  1. Put in real effort first. Work on it properly before you walk: drafts, attempts, crossed-out lines. The walk has more to work with once you’ve wrestled with the problem.
  2. Write one clear question. Before you stand up, write the question at the top of a clean page, like “What changes in verse two?” or “What could the poster show?”
  3. Walk five quiet minutes. No earbuds, no scrolling, no calls. A hallway, a yard or the block all work.
  4. Write three answers right away. The second you sit down, write three possible answers before you judge any of them.
  5. Pick one, write the next line. Choose the one with the most life in it and draft the smallest next piece. If nothing came, go back to the smallest next draft anyway.

How to tell it’s working. Keep your question-and-three-answers pages for a week. Count how many walks gave you at least one answer that made it into the work, and note how long you’d been stuck before each walk.

When it gets hard. The urge to put earbuds in is strong, especially on a route you know well. Leave them on the desk next to the question, where you’ll see them when you get back.
