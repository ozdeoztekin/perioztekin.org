You Can Predict the Feeling and Still Miss How Long It Lasts

Affective Forecasting & Durability Bias

You usually know if a result will feel good or bad, but often misjudge its strength and how long it lasts.

The Science

  • Forecasts of feelings are usually right about good versus bad, but often go wrong on how strong the feeling will be and how long it will last.
  • Overestimating strength is impact bias and overestimating duration is durability bias; the duration miss is much bigger for bad news than good.
  • Habituation: the nervous system can turn down its response to unchanging input, as when you soon stop feeling the chair under you.

The Protocol

  • Before an ordinary risk, like an audition, write down how bad you expect a setback to feel.
  • Write down for how many days you expect that feeling to last.
  • If the setback happens, compare your forecast with how you feel when those days are up.

See it in real life

Example

A forecast in his wallet

Last spring Laszlo memorized a monologue for the spring play auditions and never performed it for anyone. For two weeks he walked past the sign-up sheet on the drama room door, and every time he pictured the cast list going up without his name and himself feeling awful about it for months. The picture won, and the sheet came down without him.

Forecasts like his are usually right about whether a result will feel good or bad. Where they often go wrong is how strong the feeling will be and how long it will last, and the miss on how long is much bigger for bad news than for good. Part of what forecasts leave out is something the nervous system does all the time. It can turn down its response to input that doesn’t change, the way you soon stop feeling the chair under you, and getting used to a disappointment may work partly in a similar way, though that’s an explanation, not a schedule.

This year the sheet comes down on Friday, and he turns the picture into a forecast he can check. On an index card he writes how bad it would feel to miss the part he wants, a 9 out of 10, and for how many days, thirty. He puts the card in his wallet and writes his name on the sheet.

The cast list goes up the next week, and his name is next to a servant with a handful of lines, not the part he read for. That night feels about as bad as he predicted, and he goes to his room without answering the group chat. Thirty days later he takes the card out in the hallway before rehearsal and rates the day a 2. Somewhere in the second week, between blocking rehearsals and painting flats, he stopped checking how he felt. That afternoon he signs up for the summer theater program’s auditions.

  1. Where it stallsPicturing months of feeling awful kept him from auditioning last year.
  2. What changes itHe writes down nine out of ten for thirty days, then signs up.
  3. What happens nextThirty days after the cast list, he rates the day a two.

Try it yourself · 2 minutes

The coin that fades

Grab a coin, find a table and use the stopwatch below.

  1. Rest your forearm on the table, palm down, and use your other hand to set the coin on the back of your hand.
  2. Start the stopwatch and keep the hand completely still while you pay attention to the coin.
  3. Stop the watch when you can no longer clearly tell where the coin is.
  4. Wiggle one finger and notice the coin come back.

The timer needs JavaScript. A phone stopwatch works just as well.

What you’ll probably notice. The coin often fades within a minute or so of holding still, and one small movement brings it right back. Nothing about the coin changed. Your nervous system turned down its response to a touch that stayed the same, which is called habituation, and it’s why you stop feeling the chair under you. Getting used to a disappointment may work partly in a similar way, and forecasts about feelings tend to leave that out.

Step by step

Put your forecast to the test

For an ordinary risk you’ve been putting off, like an audition, a tryout or an application.

  1. Name the ordinary risk. Pick the thing you’ve been avoiding because of how a no might feel.
  2. Write how bad it would feel. If it goes wrong, how bad would it feel, from 0 to 10? Write the number down before you decide anything else.
  3. Write how many days it would last. Guess how many days the feeling would stay that strong, and mark the day those days are up.
  4. Go ahead and try. Keep the card somewhere you’ll see it, like your wallet, and sign up, send it in or show up.
  5. Compare when the days are up. If the setback happens, rate how you feel on the marked day and put it next to your forecast. The comparison checks the forecast, and it isn’t a deadline for feeling better.

How to tell it’s working. Put the two numbers side by side, the forecast and how you feel on the marked day. Over a few forecasts, notice whether you tend to predict more hurt, for longer, than shows up.

When it gets hard. If it goes well, there’s nothing to compare, and that’s a fine problem to have. Keep the card anyway, since another ordinary risk will come along, and every forecast you check tells you more.

Good to know. This is for ordinary setbacks, not for grief or for hard things that keep going on. If a disappointment still weighs on you after a long while, or starts to get in the way of sleep, school or friends, tell a parent, a school counselor or someone else you trust.
