Recharge Without Sleeping.

NSDR

NSDR is deliberate, low-stimulation wakefulness. A 10 to 20 minute session is a way to recover midday, and quiet rest after learning may help new material stick.

The Science

  • Kjaer et al. (Cognitive Brain Research, 2002): in PET scans of eight experienced meditation teachers, guided Yoga Nidra (a structured form of NSDR) lowered raclopride binding in the ventral striatum by 7.9%, which the authors estimated as about a 65% rise in dopamine release.
  • Tambini, Ketz & Davachi (Neuron, 2010): in quiet rest after learning, the hippocampus stayed more connected with a brain area that had processed the material, and stronger connections predicted better memory later that day.
  • Mednick, Nakayama & Stickgold (Nature Neuroscience, 2003): a 60 to 90 minute daytime nap that contained both slow-wave and REM sleep improved a visual learning task about as much as a full night of sleep.
  • NSDR is not falling asleep. It is intentional quiet wakefulness with eyes closed, no input, and no attempt to sleep, usually guided by a body scan or attention on the breath. Plain quiet rest can help memory too: in Dewar et al. (Psychological Science, 2012), 10 minutes of eyes-closed rest after hearing a story improved recall a week later in older adults.

The Protocol

  • 10-20 minutes, ideally after a focus block or post-lunch.
  • Eyes closed. No phone, no music, no input. Lie down or fully recline.
  • Don't try to sleep, let attention wander. The state itself is the work.

See it in real life

Example

One stroke early off the flags

Isabel’s district meet runs all day, and for two weeks her coach, Joanne, has drilled her on counting her strokes from the backstroke flags so she can flip at the wall without looking for it. In the morning session every turn comes right on count.

She spends the hour-and-a-half break on the bleachers scrolling through videos, and in her first race after it, the 100 backstroke, she loses count after the flags, flips one stroke early and barely reaches the wall with her feet. “You had that turn this morning,” Joanne says when she climbs out.

There’s some evidence that the brain keeps working on what it has practiced during quiet rest afterward, as well as during sleep. In one study, when people rested after learning something, the hippocampus, a key memory area, stayed in step with the part of the brain that had handled the material, and the more in step it was, the more they remembered later. A steady stream of new input may get in the way, and an hour and a half of videos is about as steady as it gets. Non-sleep deep rest is quiet on purpose. You lie still with your eyes closed and nothing coming in, awake but not trying to sleep. It isn’t a nap, and it doesn’t replace a night’s sleep.

Before her last race she hands her phone to her mom, who promises to knock on the car window in twenty minutes, and lies down across the back seat. With her eyes closed, she moves her attention slowly up from her toes. Whenever her mind slides back to the missed turn, she notices and picks up where she left off. At the knock she sits up slowly and heads inside to warm up.

In the 200 backstroke every turn comes right on count. When she climbs out, all Joanne says is “There she is.” Isabel finishes fourth and clips the ribbon to her swim bag.

  1. Where it stallsAfter an hour and a half of scrolling, she flips one stroke early.
  2. What changes itShe spends twenty minutes on the back seat with her eyes closed and no phone.
  3. What happens nextEvery turn in the 200 comes on count, and she finishes fourth.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

The five-minute scan

This is a short way into the state the page describes. You’ll need somewhere to lie down or recline all the way, and a timer you can hear with your eyes closed, like a kitchen timer or a phone alarm set face down out of reach.

  1. Lie down, or recline as far as you can. Set the timer for five minutes and put it out of reach.
  2. Close your eyes and start at your feet. Notice them for a breath or two, then move on to your ankles, calves and knees.
  3. Keep moving up slowly, all the way to the top of your head. When your mind wanders, notice where it went and go back to the last place you were.
  4. Don’t try to sleep, and don’t worry about doing it perfectly. When the timer rings, open your eyes and sit up slowly.

What you’ll probably notice. Somewhere between your feet and the top of your head, your attention probably drifted off a few times. That’s fine, because drifting and coming back is part of the scan. A lot of people also start hearing small sounds they had tuned out, or lose track of how long it has been. You were awake with your eyes closed and nothing coming in, which is the state the protocol describes. Right after a block of practice or studying, that quiet may give your brain a chance to keep working on what you did.

Step by step

Rest after the hard part

Do it for ten to 20 minutes a day, for a week.

  1. Pick your slot. Right after your hardest block of practice or studying works well, or after lunch. Using the same slot each day makes it easier to keep up.
  2. Lie down or recline all the way. A couch, a bed, the floor or the back seat of a parked car all work. Leave your phone in another room or with someone else, and set a kitchen timer for 10 to 20 minutes.
  3. Close your eyes and cut the input. Skip the music, podcasts and videos, so that nothing is coming in.
  4. Scan instead of sleeping. Move your attention slowly from your feet to your head. When it wanders, let it, then come back. Don’t try to fall asleep.
  5. Get up slowly and go back in. When the timer rings, sit up, stand and start the next thing right away. Notice how the first 20 minutes go.

How to tell it’s working. Each day this week, write down whether you rested or scrolled, and rate the first 20 minutes afterward from 0 to 10, or count something real, like mistakes or problems finished. Then compare the two kinds of days.

When it gets hard. If you keep falling asleep, recline instead of lying in bed, and keep it to 20 minutes at most. Dozing off every time can also be a sign you need more sleep at night.

Good to know. This is rest, and it doesn’t replace sleep. If you’re tired most days even after full nights, or if lying still with your eyes closed brings up thoughts that upset you, talk to a parent, a doctor or another adult you trust.
