Why “I See It Everywhere” Can Mislead You

Availability Heuristic & Frequency Judgments

How easily examples come to mind is not the same as how often something actually happens.

The Science

  • The availability heuristic is a mental shortcut that judges how often something happens by how easily examples come to mind.
  • Words starting with k come to mind more easily and seem more numerous, yet in English, words with k in third place are about three times as common.
  • Dying in a plane crash is extremely rare, yet people often overestimate the odds, because crashes tend to be highly memorable and publicized.

The Protocol

  • After a plane-crash headline, ask yourself: out of how many flights?
  • Look for that total before you judge how common crashes are.
  • When you feel rushed, slow down before trusting whatever examples pop into mind.

See it in real life

Example

One crash, nine replays

By Thursday night Aziz has seen the same crash nine times. It happened overseas on Wednesday, and the clip of smoke over a runway keeps coming back, on the news in the kitchen, in three different apps and twice in the group chat. On Saturday he’s supposed to fly alone for the first time, to spend two weeks with his grandparents in Detroit.

At dinner he asks if he could take the bus instead. It’s nineteen hours with two transfers, and his mom sets down her fork. The best reason he can give is that planes seem to be going down all the time, and to him it feels true.

That feeling comes from a mental shortcut called the availability heuristic, which judges how often something happens by how easily examples come to mind. It can work, since common things tend to come to mind easily, but it can mislead when something is rare and easy to recall. Dying in a plane crash is extremely rare, yet people often overestimate the odds, because crashes are so memorable and so widely covered. Rushing makes it easier to trust whatever pops into mind, and slower, deliberate thought is a way to check it.

He tries a different question, out of how many flights? He opens a live flight map, and the planes over the country are packed so tightly that the coastlines disappear under them. Then he looks up how many flights took off worldwide on the day of the crash and finds a total with more digits than he expected. The clip he watched nine times is one flight in that total, and he tells his mom he wants to sleep on it.

His stomach still drops during takeoff on Saturday, and he holds the armrest until the seatbelt sign goes off. Somewhere over Ohio he sends his mom a photo of the wing above the clouds, with no caption.

  1. Where it stallsHe has seen one crash nine times and asks to take the bus.
  2. What changes itHe asks “out of how many flights?” and finds the day’s total.
  3. What happens nextHe flies on Saturday and texts his mom a photo of the wing.

Try it yourself · 2 minutes

The k-word race

Use the stopwatch below and something to write with, and don’t look anything up.

  1. Start round 1 and write five English words that start with k, like kite. Stop the clock when you have five.
  2. Start round 2 and write five words that have k as the third letter, like bike or make. Stop the clock again.
  3. Before you read the result, guess which kind of word is more common in English.

The timer needs JavaScript. A phone stopwatch works just as well.

What you’ll probably notice. The second list usually takes longer, because words that start with k are easier to pull from memory, and that ease makes them seem more numerous. In English, words with k as the third letter are about three times as common. Judging how often something happens by how easily examples come to mind is the availability heuristic, and a crash replayed all week is easy to recall in exactly the same way.

Step by step

Ask out of how many

For the next headline that makes something rare feel like it’s happening everywhere.

  1. Notice the pull of the headline. When one rare event is everywhere for a few days, notice that it has started to feel common, and treat that feeling as a first impression to check.
  2. Ask out of how many. After a plane-crash headline, the question is out of how many flights. For other events, ask how many chances there were for it to happen.
  3. Find the total before you judge. Look for that total before you decide how common it is, from a source that counts every case, including the ones that never make the news.
  4. Count the replays. Notice how many times you’ve seen the same event. Nine clips of one crash are still one crash.
  5. Slow down before you decide. If you feel rushed, give it time, even a night, so the decision rests on more than what pops into mind.

How to tell it’s working. Next time a headline makes something feel common, write your first guess at how often it happens, then the total you found. After a few headlines, look at how often the first guess and the total pointed in different directions.

When it gets hard. Finding the total doesn’t always make the worry go away, and it doesn’t have to. It means the decision gets made with the total in view as well as the replay.
