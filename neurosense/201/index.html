Better at the Game. Better at Thinking?

Practice Transfer & Effort Habits

A rising brain-game score shows you got better at that game, not that your thinking got sharper everywhere.

The Science

  • In children, brain-training gains tend to stay with the practiced task or very similar ones, and a higher score does not establish better schoolwork.
  • One explanation: a task score mixes what you can do with whether the effort feels worth it, and that second part is learned setting by setting.
  • In adult lab studies, rewarding harder choices built an effort habit that can reach other mental tasks; everyday benefits are not established.

The Protocol

  • Practice the task you actually need, such as a tricky passage in a piece of music, rather than chasing a puzzle-app score.
  • Pick a manageable harder version of that task and give yourself credit for choosing it, not only for scoring well.
  • Check your progress on the real task itself, and do not treat a higher game score as proof of better schoolwork.

See it in real life

Example

Four bars the app never touched

Anaya’s brain-training score has climbed past 2,400 in three weeks. She plays the app before piano practice every night and figured the quickness would carry over to the keyboard.

It hasn’t reached her waltz. Four bars on the second page, where her left hand leaps down an octave on every beat, still fall apart the way they did in September. Mrs. Varga, her teacher, starts a new piece only when the old one is clean at the marked tempo, so the one Anaya chose herself keeps waiting.

The rising score is real progress at that game, but in children, brain-training gains tend to stay with the game or tasks very like it. A score also mixes what you can do with whether the effort feels worth it, and that second part is learned setting by setting. In the app, effort pays off every few seconds, so leaning in has become a habit there. At the piano, that passage has never paid off once, so most nights she plays from the top, enjoys the parts she knows and slides through the jump.

From Monday on, the app minutes go to the piano, passage first. Each day she picks a version a notch harder but still manageable, like hands together or the metronome a few clicks faster. She ticks a sticky note on the music stand whenever she chooses the harder version, clean or not, because the choice is what she wants to reward, though nobody knows how far a habit like that travels. To see whether the passage itself is changing, she plays it five times at the day’s speed and counts the clean ones.

By the second week, four of every five runs come out clean at the tempo in the score. At Thursday’s lesson she gets through the passage without a stop, and Mrs. Varga writes the name of the new piece in her notebook.

  1. Where it stallsHer app score keeps climbing, and four bars of her waltz still fall apart.
  2. What changes itShe gives her app minutes to those bars and ticks every harder choice.
  3. What happens nextAt her lesson the passage comes out clean at the marked tempo.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

Half the alphabet, backward

Your voice and the stopwatch below are enough. Say everything out loud, and keep going if you stumble.

  1. Start round 1 and say the end of the alphabet backward, from Z down to N, as quickly as you can without skipping a letter. Stop the clock on N.
  2. Say Z to N backward three more times without timing it.
  3. Start round 2 and say Z to N once more at full speed.
  4. Start round 3 and say the first half backward, from M down to A, which you haven’t practiced at all.
  5. Compare round 3 with round 2, and then with round 1.

The timer needs JavaScript. A phone stopwatch works just as well.

What you’ll probably notice. Z to N usually comes out much faster by the fifth try, and it can feel as if your brain has gotten quicker at going backward. Then M to A trips you up almost as much as Z to N did at first. Most of what you practiced was those thirteen letters in that order, and a job that similar may borrow only a little of it. Brain-training gains in children tend to behave the same way, staying with the game that was practiced or with tasks very like it.

Step by step

Practice the thing itself

Pick the one skill you most want to improve this month.

  1. Name the task you need. Write down the exact thing, like the four bars that break, free throws from the left side or the first minute of a speech. A game that feels related doesn’t count as practice for it.
  2. Give it your first minutes. Start each session with that task while you’re fresh, before you get to the parts you already enjoy.
  3. Pick a harder version you can manage. Make it a notch harder than last time, like hands together, a few clicks faster or the whole passage where you used to play half. It should stretch you without falling apart every time.
  4. Give yourself credit for choosing it. Make a tick each time you pick the harder version, whether or not it comes out clean. In adult lab studies, rewarding the harder choice, rather than the score, built a habit of choosing effort, but nobody has tested it in everyday practice, so treat this part as an experiment.
  5. Measure the task itself. Once a day, do it five times at a set speed and write down how many come out right. A higher game score can’t tell you that, so leave it out of your progress notes.

How to tell it’s working. Look at your clean count at the same speed on the first day and again a week later. A rising number is progress on the thing you care about, whatever the app says.

When it gets hard. The game pays off in seconds and the hard bars don’t, so your hands will drift toward the easy parts. If you like the game, keep it for after practice and give the first minutes to the task.
