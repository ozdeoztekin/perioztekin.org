Your Worst-Case Scenario Has Three Parts

Threat Appraisal & Realistic Hope

A worst-case fear bundles three estimates: how likely it is, how bad it would be, and how well you could cope.

The Science

  • Appraisal is not just the event itself: it is how you judge what the event could cost you and whether you have the resources to handle it.
  • Those judgments can be realistic or tilted toward threat. In the theory behind this, your stress reaction follows your estimate of the threat.
  • In that theory, repeatedly overestimating threats sets off bigger stress reactions than needed, which may add wear and tear on brain and body.

The Protocol

  • Split the dread into three questions: How likely is this, really? How bad would it be? What could I do if it happened?
  • Answer each one honestly from the facts you have, and where the facts allow, lean a little hopeful.
  • Skip forced positivity: do not swap dread for wild optimism, and still take steps that reduce the stress itself.

See it in real life

Example

Three minutes at the microphone

Jonas is signed up to speak for three minutes at Tuesday’s city council meeting. The council wants to close the teen center at five on weeknights instead of nine, and Jonas, who collected 212 signatures, offered to say why it matters. Since then one picture keeps playing in his head. He opens his mouth, nothing comes out, somebody films it, and the council cuts the hours because he froze.

Worst cases like that feel like one wall of dread, but each bundles three estimates, how likely it is, how bad it would be and how well he could cope. Those judgments, called appraisal, can be realistic or tilted toward threat. In the theory behind this, the stress reaction follows the estimate, so a threat sized too big sets off a bigger reaction than it needs. Wild optimism can backfire too, so a hopeful estimate still has to fit the facts.

On Monday night he answers the three questions with facts. How likely is it, really? He froze once in a class presentation, for a few seconds, and then kept going. How bad would it be? The petition goes into the record either way, and the vote isn’t until next month. What could he do if it happened? He could look down, read the next line off his card and keep going. So he might stall, but he probably won’t fall apart. He also cuts the stress itself where he can, standing at the empty microphone before the meeting while his friend Ama saves a seat in the front row.

His voice shakes on the first sentence. Halfway through he loses his place, reads the next line off the card and finishes with eleven seconds to spare. A council member asks how many people use the center after five, and Jonas answers from the sign-in sheet, about sixty a night.

  2. What changes itHe answers how likely, how bad and what he could do, with facts.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

Split one worry

Pick something coming up that you’re dreading a little, like a tryout, a hard conversation or a presentation. Have a pen and paper ready, along with the two sliders below.

  1. Write the worst case as one sentence, the way it plays in your head.
  2. Rate the dread on the first slider.
  3. Underneath your sentence, answer three questions in writing. How likely is this, really? How bad would it be? What could I do if it happened?
  4. Use facts you have, like how often it has happened before, and where the facts allow, let each answer lean a little hopeful without promising yourself a perfect day.
  5. Read your three answers once and rate the dread again.

The timer needs JavaScript. A phone stopwatch works just as well.

What you’ll probably notice. Written out, the worst case is usually several guesses stacked together, and one of them is often shakier than the rest, like how likely it is or how little you could do. If your stress reaction follows your estimate, as the theory behind this suggests, a fairer estimate can bring a smaller reaction. Sometimes the facts say it will be hard, and then the third answer, what you’d do, is the one to build on.

Step by step

Three questions for the dread

The three questions put the idea to work, though they haven’t been tested as a program. Try them the night before something you’re dreading, or any time the worst case starts looping.

  1. Write the worst case down. Put the whole thing in one sentence, like “I’ll freeze and everything will fall apart.”
  2. Ask how likely it is, really. Answer with facts you have, like how often it has happened before and what was different then.
  3. Ask how bad it would be. Name what would happen next and for how long, rather than the movie version in your head.
  4. Ask what you could do. Write one or two moves for the moment it happens, like reading from a card or asking for a minute.
  5. Lean hopeful, then cut the stress. Where the facts allow, let your answers tilt a little hopeful, without a pep talk. Then do what makes the moment easier, like seeing the room first, practicing with a timer or bringing a friend.

How to tell it’s working. Give the dread a number out of 10 before the three questions and another one after, and later write down what happened. Over a few worries, compare what you predicted with what came true.

When it gets hard. The worst case may come back a minute later, and that’s normal. Read your three answers again rather than arguing with it, and if one answer feels weak, go find a fact for it.

Good to know. This is for ordinary worries about ordinary events, not a treatment. If worst-case thoughts show up most days, keep you awake or keep you from things you care about, a parent or your doctor can help you sort out what kind of support fits.
