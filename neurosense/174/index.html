Mix the Practice, Sharpen Recall

interleaving practice

Practice feels amazing when every problem is the same type, but that smoothness can trick you.

The Science

  • Blocked practice trains repetition, while mixed practice trains discrimination.
  • Real performance asks your brain to choose the method before using it.
  • The struggle of choosing the right approach is part of what makes recall stronger.

The Protocol

  • Learn one type cleanly before you mix it with older types.
  • Shuffle a small set so the method stays hidden until you choose.
  • After each attempt, name why that method fit.

See it in real life

Example

The birthday blackout story, in Spanish

Nadia got halfway through her first sentence and froze. It was Sunday’s video call with Ximena, the host sister she’ll live with in Querétaro this summer, and Nadia was trying to tell her about the night the power went out at her little brother’s birthday party.

She knows both Spanish past tenses. Her app teaches them one unit at a time, the preterite for things that happened and ended, the imperfect for background and habits, and she scored 95 percent on each unit. But on the call every verb stopped her: fue or era? Ximena waited, kindly, and Nadia finished the story in English. She leaves in five weeks.

The app had been doing half the work. Inside a unit the tense is already chosen, so the practice trains repetition. A real conversation never announces which tense comes next, so she has to choose before she can use it. Mixed practice trains telling the types apart, and the struggle of choosing is part of what makes recall stronger later.

She writes thirty sentences about her own life on index cards, each with the verb left blank, and shuffles them so she can’t see which tense is coming. Before she fills in a verb, she says which tense and why: “Preterite, it happened once and ended.” “Imperfect, it’s the background.” Her first shuffle gets fifteen right, and it feels like she knows less Spanish than she did on Saturday.

A week later a fresh shuffle gets twenty-six. On Sunday’s call she tells the whole birthday story in Spanish, from the lights going out to the cake candles being the only light left. Ximena corrects two verbs and laughs at the right part.

  1. Where it stalls95 percent on each tense, then frozen on every verb on the call
  2. What changes itThirty shuffled cards, the tense named and explained before each verb
  3. What happens nextTwenty-six of thirty, and the whole story told in Spanish

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

Which code is it?

A quick feel for the difference between practice that repeats and practice that makes you choose. You need the stopwatch here, and you’ll decode out loud.

  1. Round 1: every word is written backward. Start the stopwatch, say all five real words out loud, and stop it.
  2. Round 2: every letter is shifted one step forward in the alphabet, so b stands for a and c stands for b. Decode all five the same way.
  3. Round 3 mixes both codes in ten new words, and nothing tells you which is which. Before you decode each word, say which code it is and what gave it away.
  4. Compare round 3 with rounds 1 and 2 added together. It’s the same number of each kind of word.
Round 1tekcor · nedrag · wodniw · tenalp · nottub
Round 2bqqmf · ujhfs · dmpve · csfbe · tupsn
Round 3lemac · hpbu · egdirb · sjwfs · gmbh · eldnac · mjpo · tocsam · qfbs · nekcihc

Answers: rocket, garden, window, planet, button. Apple, tiger, cloud, bread, storm. Camel, goat, bridge, river, flag, candle, lion, mascot, pear, chicken.

The timer needs JavaScript. A phone stopwatch works just as well.

What you’ll probably notice. Round 3 is usually slower, and it’s where people start decoding a word with the wrong code. The decoding didn’t get harder. The extra time is choosing, and in rounds 1 and 2 the round’s label did the choosing for you, the way a unit or chapter title does. A game, a test or a conversation never hands you the label, which is why the plan below practices the choosing on purpose.

Step by step

Mix the set, name the reason

It works for anything with types to choose between: verb tenses, math problems, chess tactics, tennis shots.

  1. Learn each new type cleanly first. Practice a new type on its own until you can do it without help. Mixing works on types you already know, not on brand-new ones.
  2. Build a small mixed set. Take a few examples of each type you’ve learned, two or three types at most, and put them on cards or a list.
  3. Shuffle so the type stays hidden. Mix the order so you can’t tell what’s coming, and cover any labels or chapter titles.
  4. Name the type and why, every time. Before you solve it, say which kind it is and what gave it away. Afterward, check whether you were right.
  5. End each session with a mixed round. On days you drill one type, finish with five or six shuffled items from everything you’ve learned so far.

How to tell it’s working. Score yourself on the mixed set, not on single-type sets. If the score climbs over a week and you name the type faster, it’s working. Expect it to start lower than your unit or chapter scores.

When it gets hard. Mixed practice feels messier, and your score drops at first, which can feel like getting worse. If it gets overwhelming, cut the set down to two types until those are easy to tell apart.
