When “You’re So Smart” Makes Learning Riskier

Praising Effort Over Labels

Praise can steer a child toward protecting a smart label or toward trying the harder problem.

The Science

  • A child who hears "smart" may choose the familiar problem, because getting it right protects the label and a hard one may feel like a threat.
  • Praise carries more than approval: it can signal whether you value showing an ability a child already has, or working to build one.
  • One possible explanation is reinforcement: praise can make the action that earned it more likely to happen again.

The Protocol

  • Watch for an actual action, such as a child working again after a wrong answer or picking the harder problem.
  • Praise the verb, not the label: say "you kept working after a wrong answer and tried again" instead of calling them smart.
  • Keep praise tied to what really happened, and do not judge a child's motivation from a single response.

See it in real life

Example

The blue problem with the big reach

Junie tops out the yellow problem for the fourth time this morning and hops down onto the mat, grinning. She’s nine, the smoothest climber in the Saturday kids’ class at the climbing gym, and everyone there calls her a natural.

Anwar, the class’s assistant coach since September, says it more than anyone. For three Saturdays he has watched her climb the same yellow problems while the blue one beside them, which ends with a big reach for the top hold, goes untouched. “Next week,” she says, and heads back to yellow, even after he tells her a natural like her could do it easily.

Praise carries more than approval. It can tell a child whether you value showing an ability she already has or working to build one, and a word like natural can turn into a label she has to protect. A problem she might fall off can feel like a threat to that label, so she picks the one she knows she’ll top out. Reinforcement may play a part too, since praise can make the action that earned it more likely to happen again, and every “natural” has come right after a clean yellow climb.

Anwar drops natural and starts watching for what Junie does. Near the end of class she pulls onto the blue problem’s first holds and comes straight off, and he says, “You tried the start of the blue one.” She tries again, gets higher, falls, chalks up and changes her foot, and he says, “You came off and went right back with a new way.” He doesn’t decide from one morning that she’s scared. He names what he saw.

By the end of class she has been on the blue problem six times and touched the top hold once before peeling off onto the mat. The next Saturday she warms up on yellow, walks over to blue and asks Anwar to spot her.

  1. Where it stallsCalled a natural, she climbs the same easy problems and skips the blue one.
  2. What changes itAnwar drops the label and names what she does, like trying again.
  3. What happens nextShe tries the blue problem six times and heads straight there next week.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

Labels into verbs

Find a pen and the back of an envelope or any other scrap. The first step is about you.

  1. Write down a label people gave you as a kid, like the smart one, a natural or the artistic one.
  2. Next to it, write one harder thing you avoided at some point because it might have shown the label wasn’t true.
  3. Rewrite each compliment in the box as a verb, naming something a person could do that you’d be able to see.
  4. Read your versions out loud and check that each one names something the person could do again tomorrow.
Label 1You’re so smart.
Label 2You’re a natural.
Label 3You’re so talented.
A verbYou kept working after a wrong answer and tried again.

The last row shows what a verb version sounds like.

What you’ll probably notice. If you found something you skipped, you’ve felt the pull of a label worth protecting, with the harder problem as a threat to it. The verb versions usually come out longer and more specific, because a verb has to name something that happened. That makes them useful, since a verb points to a choice someone can repeat, and a label points to something they have to keep proving.

Step by step

Praise what they did

Use it with a younger sibling, a team you help coach or a kid you babysit.

  1. Watch for an action. Look for something the child does, like working again after a wrong answer, trying a new way or picking the harder problem.
  2. Praise the verb, not the label. Say what they did in one sentence, like “you kept going after you fell off,” where you might have said “you’re so smart” or “you’re a natural.”
  3. Keep it tied to what happened. Only praise what you saw. Praise for something that didn’t happen points to nothing they can repeat.
  4. Don’t judge from one response. One morning of skipping the hard one tells you little about a child’s motivation, so keep naming verbs for a few weeks.
  5. Note their choices each week. When they get to choose, jot down whether they picked the easier or the harder option.

How to tell it’s working. Compare your notes from the first week with the last. More picks of the harder option is a good sign, though a child’s choices depend on plenty besides praise, and reinforcement is one possible explanation, not a rule for every kid.

When it gets hard. Labels slip out by habit, especially when a kid does something impressive. When one does, follow it with the thing you saw, like “you went back up after falling three times.”
