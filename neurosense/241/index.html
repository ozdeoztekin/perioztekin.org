Everyone Saw the Request. Why Did Nobody Answer?

Group Requests & Diffusion of Responsibility

More people seeing a request often means less help, partly because each person may wait for the others.

The Science

  • Pluralistic ignorance: when a need is unclear, people may look to each other, and each reads the others' stillness as a sign nothing needs doing.
  • Diffusion of responsibility: alone, the job falls on you, but with others around, knowing someone else could help seems to lift it off each person.
  • People also weigh the cost of helping: help that takes little time, money or risk is more likely to be given.

The Protocol

  • If a group request meets silence, make the need specific, such as carrying this box to that shelf.
  • Direct the ask to one person by name, such as Maya, instead of repeating it to the whole group.
  • Keep the effort small and clear, so the person knows exactly what saying yes involves.

See it in real life

Example

The coat chat nobody answered

Eleven bags of donated coats are stacked in the community center gym, and Mikael has to get them sorted by size before Saturday’s giveaway opens. On Wednesday he asked the volunteer club’s group chat, “Can people come early Saturday to help sort?” By Friday night 26 people have seen it, and it has two heart reactions and no replies.

These are the people who spent October collecting the coats. Mikael pictures families waiting in the cold while he digs through bags alone for a kid’s size eight.

When a request goes to the whole group, everyone can see everyone else not answering. If it isn’t clear whether help is needed, people may look to each other, and each reads the others’ stillness as a sign that nothing needs doing. That’s called pluralistic ignorance. Diffusion of responsibility adds to it, since knowing someone else could help seems to lift the job off each person. And his message never said how early or for how long, which makes a yes sound expensive.

So he stops writing to the group. He splits Saturday into jobs he can describe in a sentence and sends five messages, each to one person by name. “Halima, can you sort the kids’ coats into small and big on the left table, for the half hour before the doors open?” “Nikos, can you carry the boxes of hats from my mom’s car to the front table?” Now the need is plain, each job has an owner, and each person knows exactly what saying yes involves.

Four yeses come back within the hour, and the fifth says she has a swim meet but her brother can come. On Saturday the last bag is empty with time to spare, and the coats hang on two racks from small to large. When the doors open, a boy in a soccer jacket tries on a red parka, zips it to his chin and keeps it on.

  1. Where it stallsHis request in the group chat is seen by 26 and answered by nobody.
  2. What changes itHe sends five people one small job each, by name, with an end time.
  3. What happens nextFour say yes within the hour, and the coats are sorted before opening.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

Scroll back and count

Pick one group chat with at least five people in it, like a team, a club or your cousins, and grab something to write two numbers on.

  1. Scroll back through the last couple of weeks and find every message that asked the whole group for something, like “can someone” or “anyone free Saturday.”
  2. For each one, check whether a single person clearly said yes, and write down how many asks there were and how many got a yes.
  3. Then find the messages that asked one person by name for something specific, like bringing the ball or saving a seat, and count those the same way.
  4. Pick one of the group asks that went nowhere and rewrite it with a name, the exact job and how long it would take.

What you’ll probably notice. Asks sent to everyone often collect reactions, jokes or silence without anyone taking the job, while asks with a name on them more often get an answer, even if it’s no. When everybody can see that nobody has answered, the silence starts to look like a sign that nothing is needed, and with many possible helpers the job belongs to no one in particular. Your rewrite gives the job an owner and tells that person what yes would cost.

Step by step

When a group ask goes quiet

For the next request to a whole group that gets seen and not answered.

  1. Read the silence as no answer yet. A quiet chat isn’t proof that nobody can help or that help isn’t needed. It may mean everyone is waiting on everyone else.
  2. Make the need specific. Turn “help on Saturday” into jobs you can describe in a sentence, like carrying this box to that shelf or sorting one table.
  3. Ask one person by name. Send each job to one person in a message of its own, away from the group, so the job has a clear owner.
  4. Say what yes involves. Give it a start and an end, like “the half hour before the doors open.” A small, clear job is easier to agree to than an open one.
  5. After a no, ask the next person. Thank them and send the same job to someone else by name, rather than going back to the whole group.

How to tell it’s working. For your next few requests, keep two counts, how many named asks you sent and how many got a clear answer within a day. Compare them with the group asks you sent before. If the named ones get answered more often, keep asking that way.

When it gets hard. Singling someone out can feel pushy. Keep the job small and make no an easy answer, with something like “no worries if you can’t,” so it reads as a request and not as pressure.
