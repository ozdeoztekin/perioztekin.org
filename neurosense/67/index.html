Why Expectation Changes What You Feel

The Placebo Effect

A playlist, hoodie, lucky pencil, or pre-game routine can make starting feel easier. Expectation can change how effort or discomfort feels before the task even starts.

The Science

  • Wager et al. (2004), Science: placebo expectation changes brain activity in anticipation and experience of pain, so symptoms are not fake.
  • Petrovic et al. (2002), Science: placebo analgesia recruits the brain's own opioid network, a real physiological response.
  • Benedetti et al. (2005), Journal of Neuroscience: belief is one input to a predicting brain that pre-tunes the body before a task.
  • A playlist, hoodie, or pre-game routine can make starting feel easier.

The Protocol

  • Pair an honest ritual with action: choose one small pre-work cue.
  • Use the same place, same cue, and same first step each time.
  • Say the words now I begin, then take the first real step immediately.
  • Notice the signal, name the mechanism, and change one input before autopilot.

See it in real life

Example

Twenty pounds of onions first

Every home game at the ballpark starts with twenty pounds of onions for the hot dog stand, and every game day Moses finds something else to do first. He’s been on the concession crew since May, and the stand manager has promised him the grill once prep is done before the gates open at 5:30.

He wipes counters that are already clean. He restocks the mustard. By the time he picks up the knife it’s after four, he’s squinting before the first cut, and he’s still chopping when the first fans walk up. Three game days in a row.

The brain uses cues to guess what the body is about to feel, and that guess tunes the body before the task starts. Expectation changes what the brain does before discomfort arrives and while it’s happening, and some of that runs through the body’s own opioid system, so the dread isn’t fake. But belief isn’t magic. It’s one input to a predicting brain, and no amount of it changes what an onion does to your eyes.

His new plan is an honest ritual. Same place: the end of the prep counter. Same cue: he ties his apron in a double knot and gives the knife three strokes on the steel. Same first step: the first onion, halved. He says “now I begin” under his breath, which the kid on the register hears once and grins at, and the knife is moving before he can think of counters.

The onions still sting. But by the third home stand he’s at the counter with the apron knotted before he has decided anything, and prep is done at 5:05. The next week he’s on the grill, and somebody newer is on onions.

  1. Where it stallsThree game days of wiping clean counters, then chopping as the gates open
  2. What changes itDouble-knotted apron, three strokes on the steel, “now I begin”
  3. What happens nextPrep done at 5:05, and a shift on the grill

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

Same weight, two bags

A quick way to feel your brain predicting before you move. You need a small bag, a much bigger bag, two full water bottles of the same size, and some crumpled newspaper or a light sweater.

  1. Put one bottle in the small bag.
  2. Put the other bottle in the big bag, and stuff the rest of it with the crumpled paper or the sweater so it looks full. Now the big bag weighs the same or a little more.
  3. Lift the small bag by its handles, just off the floor. Then lift the big one. Which feels heavier?
  4. Try it on someone else without telling them what’s inside, and ask the same question.

What you’ll probably notice. The small bag feels heavier to most people, even though the big one weighs as much or slightly more. Your brain guessed the weight from the size and got your arm ready before the lift began, and that guess changed what the lift felt like. Expectation is real input. An honest ritual uses the same machinery for a start, not a promise: it tells your brain what’s coming, and then you do the real thing.

Step by step

An honest ritual, then the first step

  1. Pick one place. The same spot every time: a corner of your room, one chair, the end of a table.
  2. Choose one small cue. A song, an apron knot, a hoodie, a sharpened pencil. Small, honest and the same each time, with no promises attached.
  3. Decide the first real step. Not “practice” or “prep” but the first stretch, the first scale, the first onion. Write it down.
  4. Say the words: now I begin. Out loud or under your breath, right after the cue.
  5. Take the first step immediately. No checking anything in between. The ritual only helps if it leads straight into the real thing.

How to tell it’s working. Count how many days you started this week, and time the gap between showing up and the first real step. If the starts go up and the gap shrinks, the cue is turning into a start signal.

When it gets hard. Rituals like to grow: one more song, a snack, the perfect playlist. If yours takes longer than a minute, cut it back to one cue.

Good to know. A ritual can make starting easier, but it can’t treat an injury or an illness, so never promise yourself a fake cure: if something hurts or a symptom keeps coming back, get it checked.
