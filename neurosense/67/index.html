Why Expectation Changes What You Feel

The Placebo Effect

A playlist, hoodie, lucky pencil, or pre-game routine can make starting feel easier. Expectation can change how effort or discomfort feels before the task even starts.

The Science

  • Wager et al. (2004), Science: placebo expectation changes brain activity in anticipation and experience of pain, so symptoms are not fake.
  • Petrovic et al. (2002), Science: in a brain scanning study, placebo pain relief and an opioid painkiller both raised activity in the same pain-control network, suggesting a related brain mechanism.
  • Benedetti et al. (2005), Journal of Neuroscience: placebo effects run on expectation and on learned pairings between cues and relief, and some involve the brain's own opioids or dopamine.
  • A playlist, hoodie, or pre-game routine can make starting feel easier.

The Protocol

  • Pair an honest ritual with action: choose one small pre-work cue.
  • Use the same place, same cue, and same first step each time.
  • Say the words now I begin, then take the first real step immediately.
  • Notice the signal, name the mechanism, and change one input before autopilot.

See it in real life

Example

Toes over the gutter at 5:45

Morning practice starts at 5:45 in a pool that feels cold for the first minute, and every morning Moses finds something else to do first. He moved up to the senior group in May, and the coach says whoever starts the first set on time leads the lane. Moses wants lane four.

He adjusts his goggle strap and refills his water bottle. Then he stands at the edge with his toes curled over the gutter while the others push off, tense and shivering before his feet are even wet, and by the time he slides in he’s a full length behind. It has happened three practices in a row.

The brain uses cues to predict what the body is about to feel, and gets the body ready ahead of time. It’s part of how the placebo effect works. Expecting relief changes brain activity before and during pain, partly through the body’s own pain-relief chemicals, so the effect is real. The same machinery can work in the other direction, which may be part of why his dread feels so physical. But belief isn’t magic. It’s one input to a brain that’s always predicting, and no amount of it warms up the water.

So he builds an honest ritual. He always starts at the end of lane four. He snaps his goggles on, swings his arms twice and slaps each thigh once, and jumps in feet first instead of using the ladder. Right before he jumps he says “now I begin” under his breath, which the girl in the next lane hears and grins at, and he’s in the water before he can think about his water bottle.

The water is still cold. But by the third week he’s at the edge with his goggles on without thinking about it, and he pushes off with the first set on the clock. The week after that he’s leading lane four, and somebody newer is curling their toes over the gutter.

  1. Where it stallsHe fixes his goggles and refills his bottle until he’s a full length behind.
  2. What changes itHe snaps his goggles, swings his arms, says “now I begin” and jumps in.
  3. What happens nextHe starts on time and is leading lane four a few weeks later.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

Same weight, two bags

This is a quick way to feel your brain predicting before you move. You’ll need a small bag, a much bigger bag, two full water bottles of the same size, and some crumpled newspaper or a light sweater.

  1. Put one bottle in the small bag.
  2. Put the other bottle in the big bag and stuff the rest of it with the crumpled paper or the sweater so it looks full. Now the big bag weighs the same or a little more.
  3. Lift the small bag by its handles, just off the floor, and then lift the big one. Notice which one feels heavier.
  4. Try it on someone else without telling them what’s inside, and ask them the same question.

What you’ll probably notice. The small bag feels heavier to most people, even though the big one weighs as much or slightly more. Your brain guessed the weight from the size before the lift began, and that guess changed how the lift felt. Expectation is real input. An honest ritual puts expectation to work to help you start, not to promise anything. It tells your brain what’s coming, and then you do the real thing.

Step by step

An honest ritual, then the first step

Use it for anything you put off starting, even though you know how to do it.

  1. Pick one place. Use the same spot every time, like a corner of your room, one chair or the end of a table.
  2. Choose one small cue. Pick a song, a goggle snap, a hoodie or a sharpened pencil. Keep it small, honest and the same each time, with no promises attached.
  3. Decide the first real step. Make it specific, like the first stretch, the first scale or the first length instead of practice or homework, and write it down.
  4. Say the words now I begin. Say them out loud or under your breath, right after the cue.
  5. Take the first step right away. Don’t check anything in between. The ritual only helps if it leads straight into the real thing.

How to tell it’s working. Count how many days you started this week, and time the gap between showing up and the first real step. If the starts go up and the gap shrinks, the cue is turning into a start signal.

When it gets hard. Rituals like to grow, with one more song, a snack or the perfect playlist. If yours takes longer than a minute, cut it back to one cue.

Good to know. A ritual can make starting easier, but it can’t treat an injury or an illness, so never promise yourself a fake cure. If something hurts or a symptom keeps coming back, get it checked.
