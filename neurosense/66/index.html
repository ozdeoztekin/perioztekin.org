How Your Body Talks Back to Your Brain

The Vagus Nerve

Before a talk, game, family conflict, or test, your stomach and heart can vote before words arrive. Body signals can quietly tilt the story the brain starts to tell.

The Science

  • Breit et al. (2018), Frontiers in Psychiatry: the vagus nerve carries signals between brainstem, heart, lungs, and gut in both directions.
  • Breit et al. (2018), Frontiers in Psychiatry: about 80 to 90 percent of vagal fibers carry body data up, so the body helps set the brain's context.
  • Zaccaro et al. (2018), Frontiers in Human Neuroscience: a review of 15 studies found that slow breathing, under ten breaths a minute, increases the heart's natural beat-to-beat variation and goes with feeling calmer and less anxious.
  • Your stomach and heart can vote before words arrive.

The Protocol

  • Send steady data upward: inhale gently through the nose.
  • Exhale longer than the inhale for five quiet rounds.
  • Keep shoulders loose; if you feel dizzy, stop and breathe normally.
  • Notice the signal, name the mechanism, and change one input before autopilot.

See it in real life

Example

Hands that won’t stay still

In ASL class your hands are your voice, and Robin’s won’t stay still in her lap. The final is a two-minute story signed in front of the class, and she goes fourth. Hers is about the day her grandmother’s dog ate a whole birthday cake, and she has practiced it in the mirror for two weeks.

As the first student starts, her stomach drops and her heart pounds, and a story shows up right behind them. She’ll blank on the sign for cake, everyone will see her hands shake, and she’ll have to start over. By the time the second student sits down, the story feels like a fact.

The vagus nerve carries signals both ways between the brainstem and the heart, lungs and gut, and most of its fibers carry information up to the brain. Her pounding heart and dropping stomach reach her brain before any words do, and a racing heart can make everything feel urgent. A slow breath out sends different information. It changes the pressure in her chest and the rhythm of her heart, and the brain tends to read that as calmer.

While the third student signs, she works from her seat. She takes five quiet breaths, each one gentle on the way in through her nose and longer on the way out, with her shoulders loose. Then she names a few real things in the room, the hum of the projector, the yellow pencil on her desk and Noemi giving her a thumbs-up. When her name is called, she walks up with her first sign ready.

Her hands still tremble through the first two signs. Then the story takes over, with the cake, the dog up on its hind legs and her grandmother’s face. She signs the whole two minutes without starting over, and when she gets to the frosting on the dog’s nose, half the class laughs before she has finished the sign.

  1. Where it stallsBefore her turn, her stomach drops, her heart pounds and her hands shake.
  2. What changes itIn her seat she takes five quiet breaths and names three real things.
  3. What happens nextShe signs the whole story without starting over, and the class laughs.

Try it yourself · 2 minutes

Two texts, two bodies

This is a quick test of whether your body changes how words land. You’ll need a little floor space and the sliders below.

  1. Sit still and do five quiet rounds of a gentle breath in through your nose and a longer breath out, with your shoulders loose. If you feel dizzy, stop and breathe normally.
  2. Read text 1 once, then rate how worrying it sounds on the first slider.
  3. March in place for thirty seconds, enough to feel your heart beat a little faster and nowhere near your limit.
  4. Right away, read text 2 once and rate it on the second slider.
  5. Compare the two numbers.
Text 1“Hey, can you call me when you get a minute?”
Text 2“Can we talk after practice?”

The timer needs JavaScript. A phone stopwatch works just as well.

What you’ll probably notice. Text 2 gets the higher worry rating for many people, even though it isn’t any scarier. A faster heart is information from the body, much of it carried up the vagus nerve, and the brain uses it as background for whatever it reads next. That’s the body weighing in before the words arrive, and it’s why the plan sends steadier signals first, with five slow breaths before the talk, the game or the test.

Step by step

Five rounds before the hard moment

It’s made for the minutes before a talk, a game, a family argument or a test.

  1. Catch the body’s vote. Notice what your stomach, heart and hands are doing, and the story that’s starting right behind them.
  2. Breathe in gently and out longer, five times. Take a quiet breath in through your nose, then a breath out that runs longer than the breath in, five times.
  3. Keep your shoulders loose and notice the pause. Keep your shoulders down and notice the small, natural pause after each breath out. If you feel dizzy, stop and breathe normally.
  4. Name something safe and specific. Look around and name one or two real things, like the clock over the door, a pencil on the desk or a friend in the stands.
  5. Then start with your first line. Have your first sentence, sign or move ready before you begin, and start right after the five breaths.

How to tell it’s working. Each time, rate how loud your body’s reaction felt from 0 to 10, before and after the breaths, and write down whether you started when you meant to. After a few tries, look at both columns.

When it gets hard. If counting breaths makes you more nervous, drop the count. Make each breath out a little longer than the breath in and stop at five.

Good to know. If your heart races or your stomach hurts often, or you ever feel faint, tell a parent and see a doctor. If a conversation could put you in danger, skip this and talk to a trusted adult first.
