Why You Feel Different Around Different Friend Groups

Status & Serotonin

Your confidence can shift between groups, and animal studies link a sense of social rank to serotonin.

The Science

  • Raleigh et al. (Brain Research, 1991): in groups of adult male vervet monkeys whose top male had been removed, males given drugs that boosted serotonin activity rose to the top every time, while males given drugs that lowered it did not.
  • Edwards & Kravitz (Current Opinion in Neurobiology, 1997): a review of links between serotonin, social status and aggression, from lobsters and crayfish to vertebrates; the links are real but complicated, and serotonin's effects can depend on an animal's social history.
  • Wood et al. (Neuropsychopharmacology, 2006): healthy adults who temporarily lowered their serotonin with a tryptophan-depleting drink cooperated less in a trust game, at least on their first day of testing.
  • One idea, not yet tested directly, is that social feeds stir up this sense of rank by inviting constant comparison. In a three-week study of college students, cutting social media to about 30 minutes a day lowered loneliness and depression (Hunt et al., 2018).

The Protocol

  • Weekly: find ONE setting where you help someone with something hard.
  • Examples: tutor a younger student, help a friend with a project, teach a skill you have.
  • Being useful to someone builds a kind of standing that doesn't depend on likes.
  • Bonus: keep comparison scrolling to about 30 minutes a day; in one study, that cut lowered loneliness.

See it in real life

Example

The joke he kept deleting

In one group chat, Liam is the funny one. It’s the five guys from his street, friends since second grade. In the other, he types a joke, deletes it, types it again and sends a thumbs-up instead.

The second chat is Jaylen’s group. Liam started sitting with them in September, after his best friend switched schools. They’re louder and quicker, and always ranking something: shoes, haircuts, who got invited where. At night he watches the chat to see who reacted to what, and on Tuesday his phone counted 58 pickups after dinner.

Around any group, the brain keeps asking a quiet question: am I safe, useful and respected here? Serotonin is one system involved. In monkey groups, raising serotonin activity helped males rise in rank when the top spot was open, though it isn’t a happiness button. People who feel valued in a group tend to act calmer and more flexibly. A chat that ranks everything is a scoreboard that never stops refreshing, and it can keep people chasing approval.

Desmond from next door knocks on Friday in a borrowed jacket, a tie hanging loose, half an hour before his first middle school dance. He has been fighting a how-to video since five. Liam learned the knot for his uncle’s wedding. At the hall mirror he stands behind Desmond and walks him through it twice, then makes him do it alone. The third knot is crooked. The fourth is right.

Desmond leaves at a jog. Liam doesn’t open Jaylen’s chat again until after nine, and when he does, it’s rating everyone’s shoes. He types “Desmond’s tie 10/10, coach also 10,” sends it on the first try, and puts the phone down without checking who reacted.

  1. Where it stallsDeleted jokes, thumbs-ups and 58 pickups after dinner
  2. What changes itAt the hall mirror, teaching Desmond to tie his own tie
  3. What happens nextThree hours without the chat, then a joke on the first try

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

Your groups on paper

Grab a pen and paper. Nobody else needs to see this, so be honest with yourself.

  1. Write down three or four groups you spend time with in a normal week: a team, a family table, a group chat, a feed, a job, a club.
  2. Next to each, give a number from 0 to 10 for how often you check where you stand there: who reacted, who got picked, who’s ahead.
  3. Give each a second number from 0 to 10 for how useful you are there. Do people come to you for anything?
  4. Circle the group where you feel most like yourself, and star the one you check the most. Then look at each pair of numbers side by side.

What you’ll probably notice. Your starred group, the one you check most, often has the lowest usefulness number, and the groups where you’re useful tend to feel steadier. That’s the quiet question from the story, am I safe, useful and respected here, showing up in your own numbers. The usefulness number is the one you can change this week, which is where the plan below starts.

Step by step

One real favor a week

Once this week, in person if you can.

  1. Pick one specific, hard thing. Something one person is stuck on that you can actually help with: a skill you can teach, a small problem you can solve, part of a team task you can carry.
  2. Do it face-to-face. In person beats a message. Show them instead of doing it for them, and hand it over for the second try.
  3. Keep it off your feed. Skip posting about it. The point is the help, and the person who got it.
  4. Notice the signal afterward. That evening, write one line: your mood, roughly how often you checked a chat or a feed, and whether you said what you meant somewhere.
  5. Put a limit on the scoreboard. If a feed or chat keeps you checking rank, give it a 30-minute daily limit in your phone settings.

How to tell it’s working. After two or three weeks, put your favor-day lines next to ordinary days. Fewer checks, a steadier mood, or saying things on the first try more often are signs it’s working for you.

When it gets hard. The hardest part is finding the favor. If nothing comes up, ask one person what they’re stuck on this week; younger siblings, neighbors and teammates usually have an answer.
