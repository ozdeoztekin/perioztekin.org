Rest Without Turning It Into Another Concentration Test

Interoception & Guided Rest

Watching every heartbeat can make rest effortful. Guided rest asks your attention to spread out, not lock on.

The Science

  • Your body keeps sending signals; close your eyes and focus inward, and some move into the foreground. Sensing them is called interoception.
  • Body-sensing regions such as the insula and anterior cingulate register that inner state, and prefrontal networks help steer attention.
  • Non-sleep deep rest, or NSDR, uses guided breathing and a body scan with the aim of relaxing and diffusing attention, not narrowing it.

The Protocol

  • Try a ten-minute guided NSDR audio: sit or lie comfortably somewhere quiet, close your eyes and follow its instructions.
  • Let the guide carry the sequence instead of checking every heartbeat or measuring how relaxed you are.
  • If inward attention feels uncomfortable, stop that practice, open your eyes and focus on a stable object, such as a plant.

See it in real life

Example

Counting heartbeats after four parties

Frosting is still on Cosima’s sneakers when she gets home. On Saturdays she hosts birthday parties at the indoor play gym, four in a row, each with twenty kids who want the foam pit at once. Belen’s birthday dinner is at six, and Cosima wants an hour of rest first.

Last Saturday that hour went wrong. She lay on her bed with two fingers on her wrist, counting, and kept opening the heart-rate screen on her watch to see whether she was calm yet. The harder she listened, the louder the thumping got, and she left for dinner as wound up as she’d lain down.

Her body sends signals all the time, like pressure, breathing and movement in her chest, and sensing them is called interoception. With her eyes closed, the visual world recedes, and focusing inward brings some of those signals into the foreground. Body-sensing regions such as the insula and anterior cingulate register that inner state, and prefrontal networks behind the forehead help steer attention toward those signals or elsewhere. A heartbeat that stands out more isn’t necessarily beating any harder, and by inspecting every beat, Cosima had turned rest into another concentration test.

This Saturday she gives her attention a different job. Non-sleep deep rest, or NSDR, uses guided breathing and a body scan with the aim of relaxing and spreading attention out rather than pinning it to one spot. She puts her watch in the nightstand drawer so there’s nothing to measure, lies down, closes her eyes and starts a ten-minute guided audio. Twice she catches herself checking whether she’s calm yet, and both times she follows the voice on to the next part.

When the voice tells her to open her eyes, the ten minutes are over and the watch is still in the drawer. She never went looking for her pulse. Twenty minutes before six she’s on her bike with Belen’s present in the basket.

  1. Where it stallsShe tries to rest by counting heartbeats, and they only get louder.
  2. What changes itHer watch goes in a drawer, and a guided audio leads the way.
  3. What happens nextShe never checks her pulse and leaves for the dinner on time.

Try it yourself · 2 minutes

Where your heartbeat goes

Sit somewhere quiet with one ordinary object in view, like a plant, a mug or a lamp. If any step feels uncomfortable, open your eyes and stay with the object.

  1. With your eyes closed, spend about thirty seconds trying to feel your heartbeat without touching your wrist or neck. Notice whether it gets easier to find the longer you search.
  2. Open your eyes and rest them on the object for another thirty seconds, following its edges, its colors and where the light falls on it.
  3. Without moving, notice how much of your heartbeat you can still feel while your eyes are on the object.
  4. Compare the two halves and ask yourself where the heartbeat was easier to notice.

What you’ll probably notice. The longer you search for your heartbeat, the easier it often is to find, and it tends to slip into the background once your eyes are busy with something outside. A heartbeat that stands out more hasn’t necessarily gotten any stronger. Attention shapes which body signals reach the foreground, so turning every sensation into something to inspect can make rest feel like work.

Step by step

Let the voice do the steering

One option for resting during the day, especially after a long one.

  1. Choose a ten-minute guided NSDR audio. Pick one that leads you through breathing and a body scan, and have it ready before you lie down.
  2. Sit or lie down somewhere quiet. Get comfortable, shut your eyes and follow the voice’s breathing and body instructions.
  3. Leave the measuring out of it. Put the watch away and skip the pulse checks and the calm scores. When you catch yourself checking how relaxed you are, let the voice carry you on to its next step.
  4. If looking inward feels uncomfortable, stop. Open your eyes and rest them on something steady, like a plant. That’s a different attention exercise, and there’s no need to finish the audio first.
  5. Note how often you checked. Afterward, jot down whether you followed the voice or kept checking on yourself, and roughly how many times.

How to tell it’s working. Over a few tries, count how many times you reached for your pulse or your watch during the ten minutes. If the count drops and the audio has become something you follow rather than something you grade, the new instruction is doing its job.

When it gets hard. The urge to check if you feel calm yet tends to come back, especially in the first few minutes. Each time, let the next instruction take your attention somewhere else, without scolding yourself for drifting.

Good to know. NSDR is a way to rest while awake, not sleep and not a replacement for it, and it isn’t a treatment for anxiety. If paying attention to your body often feels upsetting, or you feel worn out most days even with enough sleep, bring it up with your family and a doctor.
