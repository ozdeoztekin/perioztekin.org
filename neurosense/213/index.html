Why Learning Something New Can Trip Up an Old Memory

Retroactive & Proactive Interference

Similar memories compete, so new learning can block an old memory, and old memories can block new learning.

The Science

  • Newer memories can block an older one (retroactive interference), and old ones can block new learning (proactive interference).
  • Similar memories compete: other passwords get in the way of the one you need, while a vacation town's name likely will not.
  • Related memories may compete even during sleep: if one cue reactivates two versions, the stronger may gain at the weaker one's expense.

The Protocol

  • When a new password, rule or word resembles an older one, pause before you move on.
  • Ask what detail distinguishes this version from the older one, and spell it out.
  • When an old pattern intrudes, like your native grammar in a new language, name the difference again.

See it in real life

Example

Fourteen yellow belts turned left

Fourteen yellow belts turned left at move seven, because Matthias did. He was leading the beginners through their form on Tuesday, and his body turned and blocked when it should have stepped straight and punched. His instructor clapped once to stop the class.

Matthias has done that form for a year. Since September he has also been learning the next one for his own rank, which opens with the same six moves and then turns left into a knife-hand block where the old form steps straight into a punch. Now each form keeps leaking into the other, and on Thursday he leads the beginners again.

Memories that look alike compete. A vacation town’s name is unlikely to get in the way of a password, but two forms that open the same way crowd each other. When newer learning blocks an older memory, it’s called retroactive interference, which is the new turn breaking into the old form. It runs both ways, and old learning that blocks new learning is proactive interference, like the old punch showing up in his new form. Memories that share too many details may be harder to tell apart, so the place to work is the one detail where these two split.

So he stops at the split, asks what is different and spells it out on an index card, “Old form, step straight and punch. New form, turn left and knife-hand.” In every practice he pauses there and says the word for the form he’s in, straight or left, before going on. When the wrong move sneaks in anyway, he stops, says the difference out loud again and starts over a few moves back.

On Thursday he counts the beginners through from the front. Right before the split he says “straight” under his breath, and fourteen yellow belts step and punch with him. After class he runs the new form for his instructor, and at move seven he turns left.

  1. Where it stallsLeading the beginners, he turns the wrong way and fourteen kids follow.
  2. What changes itHe names the one move where the two forms split, out loud.
  3. What happens nextOn Thursday the whole row steps straight and punches with him.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

Buzz on threes, then fours

Your voice and the counter below are all you need, and a friend can keep count if one is around.

  1. Count out loud from 1 to 30, saying “buzz” in place of every number in the three times table, so 3, 6, 9 and on. Do it twice.
  2. Switch the rule to the four times table, so 4, 8, 12 and on, and count to 30 again. Tap the counter each time a threes buzz slips out or almost does.
  3. Write down that count and reset the counter. Then say the difference out loud, like “fours now, so 6 and 9 are plain numbers.”
  4. Count to 30 once more with the fours rule, tapping for every slip.
  5. Compare round two with round three, and look at where the slips happened.

The timer needs JavaScript. A phone stopwatch works just as well.

What you’ll probably notice. The slips tend to land on 6, 9 and 15, numbers where buzz had already become the habit, so the old rule blocks the new one. The more two rules share, the more they compete, and these two even agree on 12 and 24. So the useful place to slow down is the exact spot where they differ, and whether naming the difference changed your count is worth noticing too.

Step by step

Name the difference

Save it for anything new that looks a lot like something you already know, like a new combination, a new rule at work, a play or a form.

  1. Notice when new looks like old. When a new rule, word, sequence or combination starts out the same as one you know, pause right there before you move on.
  2. Spell out what’s different. Ask which detail belongs only to the new version and write it in one short sentence, like “the new form turns left at move seven.”
  3. Slow down at the split. In practice, stop where the two versions part and say the detail out loud before you go on.
  4. Name the difference again after a slip. Stop, say the difference out loud once more and redo the stretch from a little before the split.
  5. Check the other direction too. Run the old version now and then, since newer learning can creep into it as well.

How to tell it’s working. Whether naming the difference helps you is something your own counts can show. Write down how often the wrong version shows up at the split in each practice and compare across a week, and if the slips move to a new spot, that’s where the next difference needs naming.

When it gets hard. If the same slip keeps coming back, the difference you wrote may be too vague. Make it more exact, like the direction, the hand or the first word, and say it every time, even on days you feel sure.
