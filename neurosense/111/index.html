Why Pain Is More Than Damage

Nociception, Pain & Context

Pain is not a volume meter bolted to the body. The nervous system does not just measure damage. It interprets threat. Two people can have the same tissue signal and very different pain.

The Science

  • Nociception, the body's damage signaling, is not the same as pain, which includes perception and interpretation.
  • Pain is shaped by physical signals, attention, context, memory, and expectation.
  • Safe movement and calmer threat appraisal can give the system evidence that lowers its danger setting.

The Protocol

  • Do not ignore new, spreading, intense, or injury linked pain.
  • For ordinary aches, give the system safe evidence through gentle range and slow breathing.
  • Track whether movement becomes easier as threat drops.

See it in real life

Example

A stiff neck before the 200 freestyle

The pullout couch in the hotel room had a metal bar right where Nia’s neck landed. In warm-up at the league championship, turning her head right sends a sharp ache down her neck. She swims two lengths breathing only to the left and hangs on the wall. The 200 freestyle is at 10:40.

On the bleachers she keeps testing it, turning until it bites and pressing the sore spot, half sure something tore. Last season a relay teammate hurt her shoulder in this pool and missed the rest of the year. Nia tenses before every turn. When Coach Ruiz asks how bad, one to ten, she says seven.

The ache is real. But nociception, the body’s damage signaling, isn’t the same thing as pain. Her brain decides how loud the signal from her neck feels, using everything it has: a big meet, a pool with a bad memory, the jolt she’s braced for. Fear can turn the volume up. Calm, safe movement can sometimes turn it down, because it gives her nervous system evidence that turning is safe.

Nothing hit her and nothing is spreading. She slept on a bar. She sits tall and turns right only as far as it goes without the bite, breathing out slowly, five times, never pushing. On the first turn she can see the end of the bleachers. On the fifth, the timing board. Then easy lengths in the warm-down lane, adding a right breath every fourth stroke. Coach’s rule: if it gets sharper, she’s out of the race.

It doesn’t. At 10:40 she swims the 200 breathing every third stroke, both sides, the way she trained. She finishes a second slower than her best, and when Coach asks again, she says three.

  2. What changes itFive slow turns, only as far as easy, breathing out
  3. What happens nextSwims the 200 breathing both sides, and says three

Try it yourself · 2 minutes

The easy turn

You need a chair and a room to look around. Nothing here should hurt: every turn stops where it’s still easy, and if turning your head hurts at all today, do only the first step.

  1. Sit tall and notice the chair pressing on your back. It was there a moment ago, but you probably weren’t feeling it until your attention went there.
  2. Turn your head slowly to the right, only as far as it goes easily, with no stretch or push. Note the farthest thing you can see, like a light switch or a door frame. Come back to center.
  3. Take three slow breaths, each exhale longer than the inhale.
  4. Do five slow turns to the right, each one a little short of where you stopped, breathing out as you turn.
  5. Turn once more as far as it goes easily and find your marker. If you like, try the left side the same way.

What you’ll probably notice. The chair was pressing on you the whole time, and attention changed how loud that signal felt. Attention is one of the things that shapes pain, too. On the last turn, a lot of people see a little past their first marker. Part of that is just warming up. Part of it is that slow, easy movement with a calm breath can give your nervous system evidence that the movement is safe, which is the same evidence an ordinary ache needs.

Step by step

Give an ordinary ache better evidence

For everyday aches, like a stiff neck after a bad night or tight legs after a long hike.

  1. Check for red flags first. Pain that’s new after a fall, a hit or a twist, pain that’s severe, and pain that’s spreading all get checked by a doctor. This plan is only for ordinary aches.
  2. Move gently instead of freezing. A few times a day, move the sore part slowly through the range that feels easy, and stop short of the sharp part.
  3. Breathe out slowly as you move. Let each exhale run longer than the inhale. Slow breathing can lower the sense of threat while you move.
  4. Rate it and mark how far you get. Before and after each round, rate the ache from 0 to 10 and note a marker for your range, like the farthest thing you can see over your shoulder.
  5. Add a little each day, with real rest. Build the easy evidence gradually: a few more turns, a slightly bigger range, one more ordinary activity. Sleep, and give the sore part breaks from whatever set it off.

How to tell it’s working. Look at your numbers and markers across a few days. If the ache tends to drop during a round and your marker creeps a little farther over the days, it’s working.

When it gets hard. The usual mistake is pushing into the sharp part to prove it’s fine. The plan runs on easy movement, so go smaller and slower and let the range come to you.

Good to know. See a doctor for pain that’s new after an injury, severe, spreading or getting worse, or that hasn’t eased after a few days. Don’t use movement to push through that kind of pain.
