The Loss Alarm

loss aversion in decisions

A possible loss can feel twice as loud as an equally possible win.

The Science

  • Your nervous system often weighs losses more heavily than matching gains.
  • That alarm is useful for survival, but it can distort small everyday risks.
  • It can make a chance to grow feel more dangerous than it really is.

The Protocol

  • Write the realistic loss in one short sentence.
  • Write the realistic gain and the learning value beside it.
  • Choose from both columns after the loss column has been heard.

See it in real life

Example

The challenge sheet on the band room door

The sign-up sheet for chair challenges is taped to the band room door at eye level, and it comes down Friday at three. Imani plays third chair clarinet. If she challenges Owen and wins, she gets second chair and the duet in the spring concert, the one she has played in her room fifty times.

She has walked past the sheet for nine days. In her head the challenge only goes one way: she squeaks on the high notes in bar 40, the whole section hears it, Owen gets weird, and everyone knows she tried and lost. The duet barely gets a thought.

That lopsided picture is the loss alarm. Her nervous system weighs a possible loss more heavily than a matching gain, so the risk sounds louder than it is. The alarm helps when something is dangerous. Aimed at two minutes of clarinet, it makes a chance to grow feel like a threat.

Thursday night she draws a line down a page of her practice notebook. Left side, the realistic loss in one sentence: “Might squeak, stay third chair.” Right side, the gain and what she’d learn: “Second chair and the duet. Either way, the director’s written comments, and knowing what a challenge feels like.” She reads the left, then the right, and decides. At 7:50 on Friday morning she signs the sheet.

The challenge is Wednesday, behind a screen. She squeaks once in bar 40, exactly where she was afraid she would, and plays on to the end. Owen keeps second chair. Her comment sheet says “Lovely tone. Rushing in bar 44.” On the bus she circles bar 44 in her part and writes the date of the March challenge at the top.

  1. Where it stallsNine days walking past the sign-up sheet, sure she’ll lose
  2. What changes itOne-sentence loss on the left, gain and lesson on the right
  3. What happens nextSigns, plays, squeaks once, then circles bar 44 for March

Try it yourself · 2 minutes

Your coin-flip number

No real money changes hands. You need a pen, plus a coin to hold if you want one.

  1. Read the first bet: a fair coin, tails you lose $10, heads you win $10. Would you take it? Write yes or no.
  2. Keep the loss at $10 and raise only the win, one step at a time. Stop at the first amount where you’d say yes, and write it down.
  3. Compare your number with the $10 you could lose.
  4. Ask one or two other people the same questions, without telling them your number, and compare.
TailsYou lose $10. This never changes.
HeadsYou win $10, then $12, $15, $18, $20, $25, $30

Imaginary money only. A fair coin lands heads half the time, so $10 against $10 is an even trade.

What you’ll probably notice. An even bet, $10 against $10, is a common no, and the win often has to climb close to $20 before the loss feels worth the risk. That gap is the loss alarm from The Science above: the possible loss weighs more than the matching gain. You don’t have to get rid of it. It’s the reason the gain column needs to be written down, or it never gets a fair hearing.

Step by step

Two columns before you decide

Use it the next time a chance shows up with an automatic no attached.

  1. Notice the quick no. A tryout, a challenge, a new class, a solo, raising your hand. If the answer arrived before you’d thought about it, that’s the alarm talking.
  2. Write the loss in one sentence. The realistic version, not the movie: “Might squeak, stay third chair.” If it runs past one period, cross out the rest.
  3. Write the gain and the lesson beside it. What you’d get if it goes well, and what you’d learn even if it doesn’t. Keep the page private so you can be honest.
  4. Choose with both columns in view. Read the loss side once more, then the gain side, then decide. Check how it went once afterward, and then stop going over it.
  5. If the risk is big, shrink the attempt. If the loss column holds something real and large, find a smaller version first: a practice round, a trial week, one class instead of three.

How to tell it’s working. Keep the pages. After two or three decisions, put what you wrote in the loss column next to what actually happened. If the real losses tend to be the size of your sentence or smaller, and you’re saying yes to more of the chances you wanted, it’s working.

When it gets hard. The alarm will try to write a paragraph in the loss column. Stop at the first period, and if the gain column stays empty, ask someone who knows you what you’d get out of it.

Good to know. This check is for small, everyday risks. If a choice involves your safety, your health or money you can’t afford to lose, talk it through with a parent or another adult you trust.
