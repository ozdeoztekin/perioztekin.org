Why Sugar Hits Your Brain BEFORE It Hits Your Stomach

The Gut-Brain Axis

Your gut has sensor cells wired to your brain, and in mice they report sugar within milliseconds, long before hormones catch up.

The Science

  • Kaelberer et al. (Science, 2018): in mice, sensor cells in the gut lining, called neuropod cells, form synapses with the vagus nerve and pass along a signal about sugar within milliseconds, much faster than the gut's hormone signals.
  • Bohórquez et al. (Journal of Clinical Investigation, 2015): in mice, some hormone-making cells in the gut lining were found to connect directly to nerves, evidence for a nerve route from gut to brain alongside the slower hormone route.
  • Mayer (Nature Reviews Neuroscience, 2011): a review of the many channels between gut and brain, including nerves, hormones, immune signals and gut microbes, and how they may shape appetite and mood.

The Protocol

  • Every day, eat at least one thing your great-grandmother would recognize as food.
  • Examples: whole apple, handful of almonds, plain Greek yogurt, hard-boiled egg.
  • Boring on purpose: doing it every day matters more than variety.
  • Stack it on something you already eat, for example an apple before the snack, not instead of it; that is easier to actually do.

See it in real life

Example

Three numbers on the paint bucket

The mural on the recreation center wall is forty feet of river, and the giant koi in the middle is Owen’s. It gets unveiled at the block party in two weeks.

Every afternoon at about the same time, the same pull shows up. The summer program leaves fruit punch and a box of cookies on a folding table, and he puts down his brush for two cups and a handful of cookies. An hour later he’s back for more. Most days by three his stomach is sloshing in the heat, and he has lost track of which scales he already outlined.

That doesn’t make him weak. The lining of the gut has sensing cells called neuropods, which are wired to the vagus nerve, a main line between the gut and the brain. In mice, these cells pick up sugar and pass the news along in milliseconds, far faster than hormones traveling through the blood, which may be part of why a sweet drink can feel so powerful so quickly. But a fast signal isn’t always a wise one.

On Monday he only keeps score, on a strip of masking tape on his paint bucket, rating his focus, his stomach and the craving from 0 to 10. The craving gets an 8. Starting Tuesday, a plain whole food comes first. He brings a banana and a handful of peanuts and eats them in the shade of the wall before he decides anything about the table.

By Friday the craving numbers have dropped to 4s and 5s, his stomach has stopped complaining, and his focus looks about the same. Most afternoons he makes one trip to the table instead of two. On Thursday he still has the punch, because he wants it, and drinks it on the curb looking up at a koi that finally has all its scales.

  1. Where it stallsTwo cups of punch every afternoon leave him with a stomachache by three.
  2. What changes itHe eats a banana and peanuts first, then decides about the table.
  3. What happens nextBy Friday the craving is down, and the koi has all its scales.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

The tap and the sugar cube

This is a model of the two ways news can travel from your gut to your brain. You’ll need a clear glass of water, a sugar cube or a heaped spoonful of sugar, and the stopwatch below.

  1. Fill the glass and let it sit for a minute so the water is completely still.
  2. Close your eyes and tap the back of your hand with one fingertip. Notice how fast the touch arrives, before you could even say “now.”
  3. Start the stopwatch and drop the sugar into the glass. Don’t stir it or bump the glass.
  4. Watch the wavy trails rising off the sugar as it dissolves, and stop the stopwatch when you can’t see any crystals left, or at five minutes.

The timer needs JavaScript. A phone stopwatch works just as well.

What you’ll probably notice. The tap reached your brain in a blink because a nerve carried it straight there, while the sugar took minutes because it had to spread. Neuropods work like a direct wire between the gut and the brain. In mice, cells in the gut lining hand the news to the vagus nerve in milliseconds instead of waiting on hormones in the blood. The model exaggerates the slow side, since blood moves much faster than still water, but the difference between a wire and something spreading is the point.

Step by step

Real food first, for seven days

This plan only adds one food, in front of the one you already reach for.

  1. Find your usual midday snack. Notice the snack, drink or quick meal you reach for in the middle of the day, and about when. That’s the one to stack on.
  2. Pick one fiber-rich whole food. Fruit, oats, beans, nuts, yogurt and carrots all work, or whatever fits your body, your family, your culture and your budget. Something easy to carry beats something impressive.
  3. Eat it first, then decide. Have it before the usual snack, and if you still want the snack afterward, have it. This isn’t a punishment meal.
  4. Rate three things afterward. About half an hour later, give your focus, your stomach comfort and the craving a number from 0 to 10.
  5. Keep it up for seven days. Then line your numbers up next to a couple of ordinary days at about the same time of day.

How to tell it’s working. Compare the craving numbers on food-first days with your ordinary days, and look at focus and stomach comfort next to them. If the craving tends to run lower and the other two hold steady or get better, food first is a good fit for you.

When it gets hard. The usual snag is not having the food with you when the craving hits. Keep a backup in your bag, like a small bag of nuts or a piece of fruit, so the first step doesn’t depend on remembering that morning.

Good to know. If you have diabetes, a food allergy or a diet from your doctor, check with them before changing anything. If eating often feels stressful or out of control, let a parent and a doctor help, and remember that this plan only ever adds food.
