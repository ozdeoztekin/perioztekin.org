Why Your Outer Ear Helps You Find a Sound

Outer Ear & Sound Direction

Arrival times help tell left from right, and your outer ear's folds add clues that timing alone cannot give.

The Science

  • A sound to one side usually reaches the nearer ear first, and neurons in the brainstem compare those arrival times to help estimate left or right.
  • A sound above you and one below can reach both ears at similar times, so timing alone leaves that distinction unresolved.
  • The folds of your outer ear change which sound frequencies reach the ear canal, depending on where the sound comes from.

The Protocol

  • For an ordinary sound, like a ringing phone, gently cup your hands behind your outer ears.
  • Notice whether the sound becomes easier to locate, then remove your hands and compare.
  • Treat it as a demonstration with ordinary sounds, not a method for restoring hearing or locating hazards.

See it in real life

Example

Nugget, somewhere along the left wall

The family adopting Nugget is due at eleven, and at a quarter to, the gray kitten has vanished. Junia volunteers on Saturday mornings in the adoption room at the cat rescue, and he has to be inside, because the door has been shut all morning and every half minute a thin mew comes from somewhere along the left wall.

Along that wall stand a low cabinet of food bins and a cubby tower taller than she is, full of baskets. She has spent ten minutes on her stomach with a flashlight under the cabinet, while the radio plays and two older cats watch from the windowsill. Every mew could be coming from the floor or from up near the ceiling.

Left or right is the easier part. A sound off to one side usually reaches the nearer ear first, and neurons in the brainstem compare those arrival times. A sound from above and one from below can reach both ears almost together, though, so timing alone can’t settle it. The outer ear adds that clue, since its folds change which sound frequencies reach the ear canal, depending on where the sound comes from.

Junia stops crawling. She asks Leander, the other volunteer, to turn off the radio, stands still facing the left wall and rests her hands lightly behind her ears, cupped forward, which changes the shape collecting the sound before her brain compares anything. The next mew seems fuller, and higher than where she has been looking. With her hands down it goes vague again, so she cups them once more, and it still seems to come from near the top of the cubby tower.

She drags over the step stool. In the highest basket, curled on a folded towel, Nugget blinks at her and mews. When the family walks in at eleven, he’s in the carrier by the door, and their youngest kneels down to say hello through the grate.

  1. Where it stallsShe can hear the kitten mewing but can’t tell if it’s high or low.
  2. What changes itShe turns off the radio and cups her hands behind her ears.
  3. What happens nextShe climbs to the top basket and finds Nugget before the family arrives.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

High shelf or low chair

Find a friend, a quiet room and a phone that can play a ringtone at its normal volume, never louder.

  1. Sit in a chair with your eyes closed. Your friend puts the phone somewhere to your left or right, either up high, like on a shelf, or down low, like under a chair, and starts the sound.
  2. Point to where you think it is, and say left or right and high or low. Your friend tells you how you did and moves the phone for the next round.
  3. Do five rounds with your hands in your lap, then five with your hands resting lightly behind your ears, cupped forward.
  4. Compare how many left-or-right calls and how many high-or-low calls you got right each way.

What you’ll probably notice. Left or right is usually the easy call, because the sound reaches one ear slightly before the other and your brainstem compares the arrival times. High or low tends to be harder, since the sound reaches both ears almost together, and that clue comes from the way the folds of your outer ear change which frequencies get in. Cupping changes the collecting shape before your brain compares anything, so it may change your high-or-low calls or it may not, and five rounds is a small sample.

Step by step

Cup, listen, compare

For the next ordinary sound you can hear but can’t find, like a phone ringing under a pile of laundry.

  1. Use an ordinary sound. Try it with everyday sounds at their normal volume, like a ringtone, a kitchen timer or a pet, and never with anything loud.
  2. Quiet what you can. Pause the music or the video and ask people nearby to hold still for a moment, so the sound you want isn’t competing with anything.
  3. Cup your hands behind your ears. Rest your hands lightly behind your outer ears, cupped forward, and listen for whether the sound seems high or low.
  4. Lower your hands and compare. Listen again without them. If the cupped version seemed clearer, look there first, and let your eyes confirm it.
  5. Treat it as a demonstration. It shows how the shape around your ear changes the clues your brain gets, and it may help with one sound and not another. It isn’t a way to fix hearing or to track down anything that could be dangerous.

How to tell it’s working. Each time you try it, note whether cupping changed where you looked first and whether the sound turned up there. After a few tries you’ll know whether it helps you, since it doesn’t help every person or every sound.

When it gets hard. Moving while you listen covers the sound with footsteps and rustling. Stand still for the few seconds the sound plays, then go and look.

Good to know. Keep to ordinary sounds at normal volume, and leave alarms and anything that might be dangerous to an adult. If sounds often seem muffled or one ear seems to hear less than the other, let a parent know and have your hearing checked.
