Why Your Clearest Memory Can Still Be Wrong

Flashbulb Memories & Confidence

A memory can feel vivid and certain and still be wrong in its details; feeling sure is not proof.

The Science

  • Flashbulb memories of hearing shocking news seem captured like a photograph. People feel highly confident in them, yet accuracy is far from perfect.
  • Emotional chemistry may add staying power: a jolt can release adrenaline, and the amygdala can help strengthen recently active memory connections.
  • Encoding, where experience becomes memory, is selective. Recoding, turning what you saw into a form that makes sense, can add details you never saw.

The Protocol

  • If the timing of a vivid memory matters, compare it with a dated note or message from that day.
  • Treat the vividness of a memory as a feeling of clarity, not as proof that every detail is right.
  • Use the dated record as evidence to compare with your memory, since a record can be wrong too.

See it in real life

Example

The storm was the night before

The family is writing letters for Leilani’s niece to open when she turns eighteen, and Leilani’s starts with the moment she heard. “You were born in a thunderstorm. The phone rang at two in the morning, Mom shouted from the kitchen, and rain hammered the skylight.” She can see all of it, down to the green kettle on the stove.

Her mom reads it over her shoulder. “It was breakfast. The sun was out.” Leilani laughs, because it’s the clearest memory she has, and then she stops, because her mom looks sure too.

Big news can leave what’s called a flashbulb memory, one that seems captured like a flash photograph. People feel very confident about memories like these, yet their accuracy is far from perfect. An emotional jolt can release adrenaline, and the amygdala can help strengthen the memory connections that were active, which may be why the moment stuck. But that strengthening marks the emotion and doesn’t check whether each detail is true. Only some details get in, and making sense of what you saw can add details you later remember as if you’d seen them.

The letter will stay sealed for seventeen years, so she checks the timing against something made that day. In the family group chat, her brother-in-law’s “SHE’S HERE” came in at 6:52 in the morning, with her own hearts right after, and her camera roll has the kettle from that hour, in sunlight. Further up, her sister’s “leaving for the hospital, it’s pouring” is from the night before. The chat shows when he sent the message, not the minute the baby arrived, so she treats it as evidence, not the final word.

She keeps the kettle and her mom’s shout, which they both remember. Then she crosses out the rain and two in the morning and writes, “You were born the morning after a thunderstorm, and the group chat says I heard a little before seven.”

  1. Where it stallsShe’s sure she heard the news at two in the morning, in a storm.
  2. What changes itShe checks the time in the group chat and on her camera roll.
  3. What happens nextShe keeps the kettle, moves the storm and rewrites the first line.

Try it yourself · 10 minutes

Check one vivid memory

Pick a vivid moment from a day you have records of, like a birthday, a snow day, a first day somewhere new or the day you heard big news. Keep your phone’s photos or messages from that day handy, along with something to write on.

  1. Write down six details of the moment, like where you were, the time, the weather, who was there, what you wore and what you heard first.
  2. Next to each detail, write how sure you are from 0 to 10.
  3. Find whatever you can from that day, like dated photos, messages, a calendar or a post, and check each detail against it.
  4. Mark each detail as matching, not matching or can’t tell.
  5. Look at the details you rated 9 or 10 and see how they did.

What you’ll probably notice. Many people find at least one detail that doesn’t match, often the time, the weather or who was there, and it can be one they rated a 9 or a 10. Feeling sure and being right about the details are separate things. A strong emotion can help a moment stick without checking each detail, and making sense of a scene can add pieces that later feel seen. Records can be off too, so use them to compare, not as the final word.

Step by step

Check it against the record

It matters most when the details of a vivid memory are going somewhere, like into a letter, a story you’re about to tell or an argument about what happened when.

  1. Treat vividness as a feeling. A clear, emotional memory feels like proof, but how clear it feels doesn’t test each detail.
  2. Pick the details that matter. Decide which details count this time, like the hour, the day or the order things happened in.
  3. Find a record from that day. Look for something dated and made at the time, like a note, a message, a photo or a calendar entry.
  4. Compare detail by detail. Put your memory next to the record and change whatever doesn’t hold up.
  5. Remember the record can be wrong too. A message shows when it was sent, a camera’s clock can be off and anyone can write the wrong date, so look for a second source when it counts.

How to tell it’s working. Each time you check, count how many of your details matched and how sure you felt about each one. After a few memories, look at whether your surest details were also your most accurate ones.

When it gets hard. It can sting to find out a clear memory is off, especially in the middle of an argument. Keep the parts that hold up, fix the parts that don’t, and say “the message says” rather than “you’re wrong.”
