Why Practice Is About Thinking LESS, Not More

The Cerebellum & Mastery

Mastery isn't thinking harder while you do the skill. It's practicing until the skill takes less thought.

The Science

  • Ito (Nature Reviews Neuroscience, 2008) proposed that the cerebellum builds "internal models" of skilled actions, predicting how a movement will turn out and correcting errors, which may help skills become automatic.
  • Schmahmann (Neuroscience Letters, 2019): the cerebellum, which holds most of the brain's neurons in about a tenth of its volume, contributes to thinking and emotion as well as movement.
  • Bengtsson et al. (Nature Neuroscience, 2005): in professional pianists, more hours of practice went with more organized white matter in specific tracts, including ones that carry movement commands, with the strongest links for childhood practice.
  • Poldrack et al. (Journal of Neuroscience, 2005): as people practiced a key-press sequence until they could do it while counting tones, activity fell in prefrontal regions that handle effortful control, one reason experts can look effortless.

The Protocol

  • Practice one specific move or sequence until it feels easy and a little boring.
  • Then make it slightly harder: a bit faster, a harder variation or a longer combination.
  • Once the move is solid, mix in variations; varied practice feels harder but often helps skills last (Shea & Morgan, 1979).
  • Sleep after practice; in lab studies, a night of sleep made a newly practiced motor skill faster.

See it in real life

Example

Facing the wrong wall on move sixteen

Move sixteen of Sadie’s new taekwondo form is a quarter turn that should land her in a low block facing the judges’ table. In the ten run-throughs she filmed last night, she ended up facing the side wall seven times.

The tournament is Saturday, and her instructor posted a video of the form two weeks ago. Every night since, she has run it at full speed forty times. Moves 9 to 16, a fast run of kicks and blocks traveling left, fall apart the same way. Her feet blur, she rushes, and the turn at the end swings too far.

The cerebellum, a small structure packed with neurons, is built for timing, correction and coordination. It compares what she meant to do with what her body actually did and adjusts, but only when the error is clear enough to fix. At full speed her mistakes blur together, so fast, sloppy run-throughs have mostly been practice for the wrong ending. Slow, accurate repetitions give it clean data.

On Tuesday she slows the video to 70 percent and does only moves 9 to 16, three times, watching her feet in the hallway mirror. Now the mistake is obvious: on move 12 she steps with her right foot instead of her left, and that extra half step flings the turn too far. She fixes that one step. After three clean repetitions in a row she nudges the speed up about five percent, to 75, then 80, 85 and 90. At 90 she stops and goes to bed.

On Wednesday her first run at 90 is clean, and by evening she’s at full speed. Somewhere along the way she stops thinking about move 12. On Thursday she films ten run-throughs again. Nine end facing the front.

  1. Where it stallsForty full-speed runs a night, facing the wrong wall seven times in ten
  2. What changes itMoves 9 to 16 at 70 percent, one fix, then five percent faster
  3. What happens nextNine of ten run-throughs facing the front by Thursday

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

The eyes-closed toss

You need five pairs of socks balled up and a laundry basket, a bin or a big bowl. Stand about two big steps away.

  1. Look at the basket, close your eyes, and toss all five socks one after another without peeking. Then open your eyes and count how many went in.
  2. Collect them. This time look, close your eyes, toss one, then open your eyes and see exactly where it landed before the next toss. Do all five that way.
  3. Count again. Then step back one shoe length, a small bump in difficulty, and toss five more with a look after each.
  4. Notice how your misses changed from one toss to the next: long or short, left or right.

What you’ll probably notice. More socks usually land in the basket on the second set, even with your eyes closed for every throw. In the first set you couldn’t see your errors, so there was nothing to correct; in the second, each miss showed exactly how far off you were, and the next toss adjusted. That’s the comparison the cerebellum runs between what you meant and what happened, and it only works when the error is clear.

Step by step

Slow, clean, plus five

Use it on the one move that keeps falling apart.

  1. Pick one move, not the whole skill. Eight counts of a routine, two bars of a piece, one shot, one combo. Small enough to repeat many times in a row.
  2. Do three slow repetitions. Slow enough that you can feel or see exactly where it goes wrong. Filming yourself helps.
  3. Fix one detail. Just one, like which foot lands on a count or where your elbow points. One change at a time tells you what worked.
  4. Add about five percent. Only after three clean repetitions in a row. On a metronome that’s about 80 to 84 beats a minute; on a video, 90 to 95 percent speed. If it falls apart, go back a step.
  5. Stop clean and sleep on it. End on a clean repetition. Your nervous system keeps tuning after practice, so start the next day a step below where you finished.

How to tell it’s working. Film the move at the start of the week and again at the end, at the same speed, and count the clean repetitions. Write down the fastest speed you could do it cleanly each day, too. If both numbers climb, it’s working.

When it gets hard. Full speed will tempt you every day, especially close to a tournament or a game. Allow yourself one full-speed try at the end of a session to see where you are, then go back to the slow work the next day.
