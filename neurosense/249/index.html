Why Warming Up Can Help You Cool Down for Sleep

Warm Baths & Core Cooling

A warm bath can help your core cool afterward, and a falling core temperature tends to go with sleepiness.

The Science

  • People often feel sleepiest as their core temperature, the temperature deep inside the body, is falling most quickly.
  • In the evening, your body clock lowers the brain's temperature target, and warm blood moves out to your hands and feet, where heat can leave.
  • Warm water widens blood vessels in the skin, called vasodilation; after you step out, that surface blood can release heat, helping the core cool.

The Protocol

  • In the evening, take a warm bath or shower lasting no more than twenty or thirty minutes.
  • Get out afterward and let the heat escape from your skin, rather than staying in the warm water.
  • Fit it into your evening wind-down before bed, and treat it as one option to try, not a fix.

See it in real life

Example

Bundled up before the festival bus

The youth choir’s bus to the state festival leaves at five on Saturday morning, and Odile has the alto solo in the second piece. The last two nights she lay awake until after midnight, bundled up in a hoodie and two comforters, because being warm is supposed to make you sleepy. She showers in the mornings, and a warm shower at night sounds like it would only wake her up.

People often feel sleepiest while their core temperature, the temperature deep inside the body, is falling fastest. In the evening the body clock lowers the brain’s temperature target, a bit like turning down a thermostat, and to reach it, the body pushes warm blood out to the hands and feet, where heat can leave. Warm water can help, because it widens the blood vessels in the skin, which is called vasodilation. After you step out, that warm blood near the surface can give off heat, and the core cools. Warm skin and a cooling core can go together, and Odile has been bundling up so heavily that the heat her body gives off has nowhere to go.

The night before the festival, she moves her shower to the evening. After dinner she takes a warm one, comfortable and never hot, for about fifteen minutes, and then gets out and gives the heat somewhere to go. She skips the hoodie, sits on the edge of the tub in a T-shirt to comb out her hair and lays out her concert black barefoot on the cool tile. At bedtime she uses one comforter, cracks the window and leaves her feet out at the end.

Her hands and feet feel warm, and the rest of her feels pleasantly cool. The last time she remembers seeing on the clock is twenty past ten, and the next thing is her alarm. At five she’s on the bus with her folder on her lap, running the first line of her solo under her breath.

  2. What changes itShe takes a warm evening shower, then gives the heat a way out.
  3. What happens nextThe last time she sees on the clock is twenty past ten.

Try it yourself · 10 minutes

The pink-hand test

All it takes is a sink with warm running water, comfortable on your skin and never hot, and a towel.

  1. Hold one hand under comfortably warm running water for two minutes, and keep the other hand dry.
  2. Dry the warm hand and hold both hands side by side in good light. Compare the color of the skin.
  3. Hold the warmed hand a couple of inches from your cheek without touching, and then the other hand. Notice which one you can feel warmth coming off.
  4. Wait five minutes in the room, and check both hands the same way again.

What you’ll probably notice. The warmed hand often looks pinker, because warm water widens the blood vessels in the skin and brings more warm blood to the surface, which is called vasodilation. You can feel heat leaving that hand near your cheek, and over a few minutes it gives the extra warmth away to the room. After a warm shower the same thing happens across your skin, which is how stepping out can help your core cool.

Step by step

Warm shower, cool room

One evening option to try, especially before a morning that matters. It isn’t a fix.

  1. Take a warm evening shower or bath. Keep the water comfortably warm, never hot, and stay in no longer than twenty or thirty minutes.
  2. Get out and let the heat escape. Skip lingering in the water or bundling up right away. Spend a few minutes in cooler air in a T-shirt, barefoot if the floor is comfortable.
  3. Keep the bedroom on the cool side. A cool room makes it easier for heat to leave. Use as many blankets as you like, but leave your hands and feet free to give off heat.
  4. Make it part of your wind-down. Put it at the same point in your evening each time, before the rest of your bedtime routine.
  5. Note roughly when you fall asleep. For a week or two, jot down about when you fell asleep on nights with the shower and nights without it.

How to tell it’s working. After a week or two, compare the nights with the evening shower and the nights without it. If you tend to fall asleep sooner after the shower, keep it in your wind-down, and if there’s no difference, treat it as one option among several.

When it gets hard. A long, hot soak feels great in the moment, but the cooling afterward is the part you’re after. Keep the water comfortably warm, keep it short and give yourself some cool air when you get out.

Good to know. If falling asleep is hard most nights, or you feel sleepy most days, bring it up with a parent or guardian and a doctor. A warm shower is one thing to try, not a treatment.
