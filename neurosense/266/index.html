Count the Chances You Actually Have Left

Finite Opportunities & Time Framing

Counted in hours or in days, the same window can feel spacious or limited, which can change what you put off.

The Science

  • Sometimes the obstacle is how the remaining opportunity is represented: the same period can feel abundant or limited without a single day changing.
  • A large number of hours can make the window seem spacious, while a smaller number of days can make its endpoint easier to notice.
  • When an opportunity feels finite, an experience you kept postponing can become something to do while it is still available.

The Protocol

  • Pick one real situation with an actual endpoint, such as a move, the end of a course, or your last weeks sharing a neighborhood.
  • Count the occasions left in a truthful unit, such as four Saturday mornings together rather than a vague "couple of months."
  • Choose one thing you would value, like a familiar walk with a friend, and put it on one of the remaining occasions.

See it in real life

Example

Seven Thursdays and a lullaby for two

On top of Hester’s piano sits a lullaby written for two people to play side by side, and for two years Isla has said she’ll learn it next year. Hester has been her teacher since Isla could barely reach the pedals. In March she said she’s moving to Oregon at the end of June, and in mid-May, when she holds up the lullaby again, Isla hears herself say “soon.”

It doesn’t feel urgent, because there’s still plenty of time. That night she works it out on her phone, and the time until the move comes to more than a thousand hours, which sounds like forever. Then she counts the Thursdays on the kitchen calendar and gets seven lessons.

The same window can feel roomy or short depending on the unit you count it in. A big number of hours can make it seem spacious, while a smaller number of days, or of lessons, can make the end easier to see. When a chance feels limited like that, something you kept putting off can become something to do while it’s still there.

She picks the one thing she would most like to do in those seven lessons, the lullaby, and doesn’t try to cram anything else in. At the next lesson she asks Hester to save the last fifteen minutes of each lesson for it and to play it through together on the sixth Thursday. Hester pencils the date on the first page.

For three weeks Isla’s top part keeps coming in a beat early, and Hester counts out loud. On the sixth Thursday they play it from the first bar to the last, and when Isla is slow with a page turn, they keep going. Her dad, waiting in the hall with the car keys, records the last minute on his phone.

  1. Where it stallsFor two years she has put off learning the duet on Hester’s piano.
  2. What changes itShe counts seven lessons left and puts the duet on the sixth.
  3. What happens nextOn the sixth Thursday they play it through, and her dad records it.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

Hours, then boxes

Pick a stretch of time that will end soon for real, like a season, a course, a school year or a friend’s last weeks in town. Paper and a pencil are enough.

  1. Work out roughly how many hours are left until it ends, the days times twenty-four, and write that number big at the head of a page.
  2. Underneath, draw one small box for each remaining occasion of one kind, like Saturday mornings, practices or lessons.
  3. Look at the big number, then at the row of boxes, and notice which one makes the end easier to see.
  4. Inside one of the boxes, write one thing you’d like to do in that window.

What you’ll probably notice. The hours come out as a big number, while the boxes may fit on one line, and both describe the same window. A large number can make the time seem spacious, and a short row of actual occasions makes the endpoint easier to notice. Counting in the occasions where your plan would happen keeps the window honest, without inventing any rush.

Step by step

Count the chances you have left

Save this for a window with a true end, like a move, the end of a course or the end of a season. Never invent a deadline to hurry yourself.

  1. Find a real endpoint. Pick one situation that will end, like a friend moving away, the last weeks of a class or your last season in a club.
  2. Count the occasions left. Count in a truthful unit, like four Saturday mornings together, rather than a vague “couple of months.”
  3. Choose one thing you’d value. Pick one experience you’ve been putting off. A familiar walk with a friend is enough, and it doesn’t need to become a big event.
  4. Put it on one of those occasions. Choose the date, write it down and tell anyone who’s part of it, so “eventually” turns into a plan with a day.
  5. Keep the deadline honest. Don’t count down your whole life or try to fill every moment before the window closes. One chosen experience is enough.

How to tell it’s working. When the window closes, look at what you put on the calendar and whether it happened. Compare that with the things you meant to do in an earlier window that never got a date.

When it gets hard. Counting can make a good stretch feel sad or rushed. If it does, come back to the one thing you chose and let the rest of the time stay ordinary.
