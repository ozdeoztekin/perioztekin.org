Why You Can Feel Wide Awake Just Before Bed

Sleep Pressure & Evening Alertness

A burst of alertness near bedtime does not mean your need for sleep has gone down.

The Science

  • Pressure to sleep builds while you are awake, and adenosine, a chemical that can build up outside brain cells during long waking, is one contributor.
  • In mouse brain tissue, adenosine acting on A1 receptors on nerve terminals reduced glutamate release onto wake-supporting basal forebrain cells.
  • Your circadian clock's support for wakefulness can strengthen in the evening, opposing sleep pressure that has kept growing.

The Protocol

  • With a parent or caregiver, agree on a consistent wind-down and wake time that leaves enough opportunity for the sleep you need.
  • When you suddenly feel alert near bedtime, avoid adding another task, more scrolling or caffeine.
  • If homework regularly pushes sleep aside, ask an adult or teacher to help adjust the workload or when you tackle it.

See it in real life

Example

The lighthouse cat after midnight

Every Sunday at noon, Ilya sends his cousins the next page of his comic, a mystery about a cat who keeps a lighthouse. Lately he’s been inking it at night.

After dinner he can barely keep his eyes open over homework, and on Tuesday he fell asleep on his math worksheet. Around ten, though, he feels wide awake, decides he can’t need sleep yet and uncaps his pen for another page. Three nights this week he was still drawing after midnight, and each morning his mom has had to knock twice.

How awake he feels depends partly on two influences that change on different schedules. Pressure to sleep keeps building while he’s awake, and adenosine, a chemical that can build up outside brain cells during long waking, is one contributor. In mouse brain tissue it reduced the release of a messenger that excites wake-supporting cells. Meanwhile his body clock’s support for staying awake can strengthen in the evening, pushing back against pressure that has grown all day. The burst at ten doesn’t mean he needs less sleep, and a new page stretches his waking while that need stays.

On Saturday he and his mom agree on a daily wind-down at half past nine and a wake time of 6:45, leaving enough room for the sleep he needs. Since the alert feeling says more about his clock than his need for sleep, the pen gets capped on time however awake he feels. The next panel idea goes on an index card clipped to the sketchbook. He reads a few pages of a book, turns off the lamp and lies there without checking the clock or forcing sleep.

The first two nights he’s awake for a while, and once he uncaps the pen before capping it again. He draws the page after school, a few panels a day, from the cards. His mom knocks once all week, and the next page goes out on Sunday at noon.

  1. Where it stallsHe dozes over homework, then feels wide awake and draws past midnight.
  2. What changes itHe caps his pen at the agreed wind-down, however awake he feels.
  3. What happens nextHis mom knocks once all week, and the Sunday page goes out.

Try it yourself · 2 minutes

The ruler seesaw

All it takes is a ruler, a pencil and a handful of coins. It’s a rough model of two influences on how awake you feel, not a reading of your brain.

  1. Lay the pencil flat on a table and balance the ruler across it at the middle, like a seesaw.
  2. Call the left end sleep pressure, which keeps building while you’re awake. Put a coin there for each part of your day so far, like the morning, school, the afternoon and homework, and watch that end sink toward sleepy.
  3. Call the right end your body clock’s push to stay awake, which can get stronger in the evening. Add coins there one at a time until the ruler levels out or tips the other way.
  4. Now look at the left end. It holds more coins than it has all day, and none of them came off.

What you’ll probably notice. The tilt of the ruler stands for how awake you feel, and the left pile stands for the pressure to sleep. Near bedtime the ruler can swing toward awake while that pile is at its biggest, because the clock’s support for wakefulness can strengthen in the evening and push against pressure that kept building. So a late burst of energy can’t tell you that you need less sleep. Brains don’t run on coins, and the two influences don’t come in neat units, so treat this as a picture of the balance and nothing more.

Step by step

Wind down on time, awake or not

For evenings when the hour before bed feels like the most awake part of your day.

  1. Treat the alert feeling as information. When you feel wide awake near bedtime, take it as news about how you feel right now. Pressure to sleep has been building all day, and your body clock’s evening push for wakefulness can work against it, so the feeling isn’t proof that you need less sleep.
  2. Agree on two times with an adult. With a parent or caregiver, choose a wind-down time and a wake time you’ll keep every day, with enough time between them for the sleep you need.
  3. Start winding down on time. Begin at the agreed time even if you feel alert. Lower the lights, put away whatever you were working on and do something calm, and let sleep come without demanding it right away.
  4. Skip the extra task. When a wave of energy makes you want to do more, leave the new task, the extra scrolling and the caffeine alone, since each one stretches your waking while the need for sleep stays. If an idea won’t let go, write it on a card for tomorrow.
  5. Ask for help if homework crowds out sleep. If homework keeps pushing your sleep later, ask a parent or a teacher to help change the amount or when you do it. Cutting sleep shouldn’t become the way to fit it in.

How to tell it’s working. For a week, jot down each night whether a burst of alertness showed up near your wind-down, what it tempted you to add and whether you kept the plan anyway. Then count the nights you started winding down at the agreed time.

When it gets hard. The burst is convincing, and one small task feels harmless while it lasts. Keep a card and a pen by your bed, write the idea down and give the task its time tomorrow.

Good to know. A steady routine gives sleep a better chance, and it can’t promise that sleep will come quickly. If trouble sleeping keeps coming back or you feel sleepy most days, tell a parent or caregiver so they can help you get it checked by a doctor.
