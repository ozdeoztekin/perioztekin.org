Find the Route Before You Need It

Active Route Learning & Landmarks

A label like "upstairs" is not a route; finding the place yourself can attach a path to the name.

The Science

  • A location label and a usable route are different kinds of information: "upstairs" does not say which landmark comes before the turn.
  • Your brain selects what gets processed: prefrontal control helps aim perception and movement at a goal, instead of giving every detail equal priority.
  • The hippocampal memory system helps bind an experience to its place and time, giving an event a where and a when, not only a detached fact.

The Protocol

  • In an unfamiliar building, choose one place you will genuinely need and find it yourself while you have time.
  • Notice a reliable landmark at the important turn, such as the stairs before the corridor on the right, then walk that connection.
  • If a turn feels uncertain, check it against the actual layout, and keep directions available when accuracy matters.

See it in real life

Example

Four minutes from warm-up to Ring 9

Last spring, at a regional taekwondo tournament in a university field house, a volunteer told Viggo the warm-up area was downstairs. When his division was called, he took the wrong stairwell, came out on the far side of the gym and reached his ring with his chest protector half buckled while the referee called his name for the last time. He lost the first exchange before his breathing settled.

This Saturday is the state championship, in a convention center with twelve rings split between two halls. His instructor says the warm-up room is “upstairs, past the food court,” which sounds a lot like what he heard last spring.

A label like upstairs and a route you can use are different kinds of information, because upstairs doesn’t say which landmark comes before the turn or how the corridors connect. Finding a place yourself works differently. With somewhere you need to be, prefrontal control, the brain’s way of steering toward a goal, helps aim what you look at and how you move instead of giving every passing detail the same weight. The hippocampus, a memory system, helps tie the experience to where and when it happened, so the destination ends up with a path attached to its name.

Early on Saturday, two hours before his division, Viggo picks the one route he’ll need, from the warm-up room to Ring 9, and finds it himself. At each turn he picks a landmark and says the link under his breath, “right at the trophy table,” then “through the doors under the blue banner.” Two corridors look alike, so he checks that turn against the venue map on the wall and photographs the map. Then he walks the whole route again, the way he’ll go when it counts.

When the announcer calls Division 14 to Ring 9, Viggo is there four minutes later with his gear buckled, before the second call, and he bows in breathing evenly.

  1. Where it stallsTold the warm-up was downstairs, he reached his ring half buckled.
  2. What changes itHe walks the route himself early and links a landmark to each turn.
  3. What happens nextHe reaches Ring 9 in four minutes, buckled, before the second call.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

Told route, walked route

Pick a building you know a little, like your school, a library or a relative’s apartment building. A pen and the back of an envelope will do.

  1. Think of a room there that someone once told you how to find but that you’ve never walked to, and write the route from the front door, turn by turn.
  2. Pick a room in the same building that you found on your own, and write that route the same way.
  3. Underline every landmark you wrote, like a staircase, a water fountain or a poster, and circle every turn.
  4. Next time you’re in that building, walk the first route and fix whatever you got wrong.

What you’ll probably notice. The told route often stalls at a label like “by the gym” or “upstairs,” with no landmark before the turn, while the walked route tends to come with landmarks in order and a direction at each one. Walking somewhere with a goal makes you pick out useful cues, and memory ties those turns to where and when you saw them. A label on its own never carried them.

Step by step

Walk it before you need it

For a big, unfamiliar building on a day when being on time matters, like a tournament, an audition or an exam.

  1. Pick the one place you’ll need. Choose the place that matters most, like your ring, the warm-up area, the testing room or the gate, rather than trying to learn the whole building.
  2. Find it yourself, early. Go while you have time, before anything is called, and work out the way there rather than relying only on being told.
  3. Link a landmark to each turn. At every important turn, notice something that won’t move, like the stairs or a banner, and say the link, such as “right at the trophy table.”
  4. Walk the connection again. Go once more from the spot where you’ll start, in the direction you’ll be going when it counts.
  5. Check unsure turns and keep the map. Check any doubtful turn against the posted map or ask a staff member, and keep a photo of the map, since one walk doesn’t guarantee you’ll remember every turn.

How to tell it’s working. When the day comes, time the trip from where you start to where you need to be, and note whether you had to ask anyone the way. Compare that with a building where all you had was a label.

When it gets hard. Corridors and doors that look alike are where a walked route usually goes wrong. Pick a landmark that only that turn has, and if there isn’t one, keep the map photo handy.

Good to know. This is for getting to ordinary places in good time. It isn’t an emergency plan, so in an emergency, follow the exit signs and the staff.
