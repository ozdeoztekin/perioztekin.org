Did You Practice What the Test Actually Needs?

Matching Practice to the Test

Recognizing an answer and producing it from memory are different jobs, so how you practice matters.

The Science

  • Recognizing a word in a list gives you a prompt, while writing it from memory means bringing it back without one: two different demands.
  • Transfer-appropriate processing: memory can work better when retrieval calls on the processing used in learning, like attending to sound for rhymes.
  • Memory-related activity in the medial temporal lobe can accompany judging a word's meaning or letters; meaning and sound use partly different areas.

The Protocol

  • Before you study, ask what the test will make you do: recognize, explain or produce.
  • For an explanation question, close the notes and explain the idea aloud.
  • For written recall, practice writing the answer from memory, without a list in front of you.

See it in real life

Example

Rhythm with the y in the wrong place

“Rhythm,” her grandfather reads from the study list, and Solveig spells it with the y in the wrong place. It’s her fourth miss out of twenty, on words she would have sworn she knew. The county spelling bee is a week from Saturday, and the winner goes on to the regional bee.

For a month she has studied the same way, scrolling through the 450-word list every night and highlighting the hard ones. On the screen, every word looks right to her. Out loud, mischievous picks up an extra i and embarrass loses an r.

Seeing a word on a list gives her a prompt, so all she has to do is recognize it. Spelling it aloud means bringing it back with nothing to look at, which is a different demand. At the bee the word also arrives as a sound, and she has been practicing its look. Memory can work better when retrieval asks for the kind of processing that learning used, which is called transfer-appropriate processing. The brain doesn’t do identical work for every kind of learning, so a memory partly reflects the processing that formed it as well as the words on the screen.

So before she studies again, she asks what the bee will make her do. She’ll hear a word, maybe ask for the definition, and spell it out loud one letter at a time, with no page in sight. That becomes her practice. Every evening her grandfather reads words from the list in random order while her phone stays face down, and she spells each one aloud. Every miss goes into a notebook, and the next night she writes those words from memory before he reads a single new one.

At the county bee, mischievous comes up in round three, and she spells it without the extra i. She makes it to round seven, where she goes out on a word from outside the study list.

  1. Where it stallsEvery word looks right on her screen, but aloud she misses four of twenty.
  2. What changes itShe practices the way the bee works, hearing each word and spelling it aloud.
  3. What happens nextShe spells mischievous right in round three and lasts until round seven.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

Spell it, then spot it

Find one helper to read five words aloud and write down exactly what you spell.

  1. Without looking at the box, have your helper read you the five words in the first row, one at a time, and spell each one aloud while they write down what you say.
  2. Now look at the second row, where each word appears spelled two ways, and pick the right one.
  3. Score both rounds, one point per word.
  4. Look at where your spoken spellings went wrong, and notice whether the right version looked obvious in the second row.

The speller looks at the second row only, and only after round one.

What you’ll probably notice. Picking the right spelling usually feels easy, because the two choices hand you a prompt to check against. Spelling the same word from its sound, with nothing to look at, is a different job, and the doubled letters are where it tends to slip. The practice that matches a spelling bee, or any test where you have to produce the answer, is the kind that makes you produce it.

Step by step

Practice what the test asks for

Before any test, contest or tryout where you have to show what you know, start here.

  1. Ask what you’ll have to do. Before you study, find out whether you’ll recognize answers among choices, explain an idea, or produce it from memory, out loud or on paper.
  2. For recognizing, practice with choices. If the test is multiple choice, quiz yourself on picking the right answer from close wrong ones.
  3. For explaining, close the notes and talk. Explain the idea out loud to someone or to an empty room, then open the notes and check what you left out.
  4. For written recall, write it from memory. Write the answer with nothing in front of you, check it, and redo the misses the next day before anything new.
  5. Practice from sound if it comes as sound. If the question will be spoken, like a word at a bee or an oral quiz, have someone say it and answer without looking.

How to tell it’s working. A few days before the test, do one practice round in its own format and score it. Compare that score with how ready your usual studying made you feel, and give more time to whatever scored lower.

When it gets hard. Recognizing feels like knowing, so switching to producing answers can feel like you got worse overnight. That first drop is information about what the test will ask, so stay with the matching format.
