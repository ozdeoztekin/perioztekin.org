How Practice Wires Your Brain Faster

Myelination

Myelin doesn't just protect axons. It drops membrane capacitance, which speeds signal propagation and is built up by deliberate practice.

The Science

  • Myelin is wrapped around axons by oligodendrocytes in the central nervous system and Schwann cells in the peripheral nervous system. Each oligodendrocyte can myelinate up to 50 axon segments, forming the insulating sheath in concentric layers.
  • Two electrical effects: myelin (1) increases membrane resistance, so less current leaks out, and (2) decreases membrane capacitance, so less charge is needed to change the voltage. Together they let a signal spread farther and faster along the axon before it fades.
  • Bengtsson et al. (Nature Neuroscience, 2005): in brain scans of professional pianists, hours of practice in childhood, adolescence and adulthood each went with more organized white matter in different tracts, and childhood practice showed the most widespread links.
  • Fields (Trends in Neurosciences, 2008): a review of evidence that white matter changes with learning; in cell-culture studies, firing axons release chemical signals, such as ATP and adenosine, that influence the cells that make myelin.

The Protocol

  • Practice in focused blocks of 60 to 90 minutes with no interruptions, on the one skill you want to improve.
  • Sleep well on practice days; sleep helps lock in the gains from motor practice.
  • Repeat the exact movement you want to get faster; practice-driven white matter changes show up in the pathways the skill uses.

See it in real life

Example

A beat late on every chorus

Halfway through the first chorus, Rosa stops singing and turns around. Hailey’s change from G to C has landed a beat late again, the third time since they started. Rosa asked Hailey to play piano for her at the spring talent show, six weeks away, and in the chorus Hailey’s left hand jumps from G to C to D every two beats.

Hailey has been practicing, in her way: ten minutes at the piano most nights, phone propped on the music stand, a little of every song she knows. That night she sets a one-minute timer and counts clean changes from G to C. Eleven.

Every time she plays that change, the same chain of neurons fires. In cell studies, an axon that fires sends out a chemical signal telling nearby cells to wrap it in myelin, a fatty insulation. Myelin stops ions from leaking out and means less charge is needed to change the voltage, so a signal gets farther in less time. Brain scans of pianists found that the more they had practiced, especially as children, the more organized their white matter, the brain’s insulated wiring, was in specific pathways. It builds over weeks and months, on the paths that keep firing.

Hailey changes how she practices. Four evenings a week she gives it a full hour, phone in the kitchen, door shut, nothing but the chorus changes: one-minute drills of G to C, C to D and D to G, clean and slow before fast. She writes the first count of each night on a sticky note on the music stand.

The note fills up: 11, 13, 13, 17, 22. By the fifth week it says 46. At the dress rehearsal Rosa sings straight through the chorus and never turns around.

  1. Where it stallsA beat late on every change, eleven clean changes a minute
  2. What changes itAn hour, four nights a week, nothing but the chorus changes
  3. What happens nextForty-six a minute by week five, and Rosa never turns around

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

Old order, new order

Practice changes the path you use, and you can feel how specific that is. You need a table, your nondominant hand and the stopwatch here.

  1. Rest your nondominant hand on the table. Tap the old order five times through, as fast as you can without a mistake, and time it.
  2. Do four more rounds of the old order, timing each one.
  3. Now tap the new order five times through, with the same hand on the same table. Time it.
  4. Compare the new order with your fifth round of the old one.

The timer needs JavaScript. A phone stopwatch works just as well.

What you’ll probably notice. The old order usually gets faster round by round, and then the new order drags you back toward where you started. Your hand didn’t get faster in general; one sequence did. A few minutes is far too quick for myelin, which builds over weeks of repetition, but the rule is the one in The Science above: what changes is the path you keep using.

Step by step

One skill, one path, five weeks

Pick something you can count in one minute.

  1. Choose one skill you can count. One chord change, one shot, one move. You need a one-minute count, like clean changes, shots made or words typed without looking.
  2. Book a 60-minute block. Three or four times a week, give it an hour with nothing else in it: phone in another room, door shut, one skill.
  3. Repeat the exact movement. The same change or the same shot, over and over, clean and slow before fast. The path that gets wrapped is the one you keep firing.
  4. Write down one count each time. Do your one-minute count at the start of every block and write it somewhere you’ll see it, like a sticky note in the case or on the wall.
  5. Judge it in weeks. Compare week one with week five. Myelin builds over weeks and months, and a single night’s count can bounce around.

How to tell it’s working. Average your counts for the first week and for the fifth. If the number has climbed and the movement takes less thought, it’s working.

When it gets hard. An hour of one movement gets dull around minute 20. Break it into one-minute drills with short pauses, keep counting, and don’t slide into songs or moves you already know.
