Give the Aftertaste a Moment

Aftertaste & Retronasal Smell

Flavor can keep changing after you swallow, as enzymes in your mouth can free aromas that reach your nose.

The Science

  • Some foods hold aroma molecules bound to sugars in compounds called glycosides; while bound, they do not readily escape into the air as odors.
  • Enzymes in your mouth, including some from oral microbes, can split that bond, so the aroma can travel through the back of the throat to your nose.
  • Swallowing leaves small residues whose chemistry and aroma release can continue, so the aftertaste need not match your first impression.

The Protocol

  • Take one manageable bite of a familiar food, chew comfortably and swallow normally, without timing it.
  • Before a different food or drink, pause briefly and compare your first impression with what is still arriving.
  • Give the bite enough attention to notice its sequence, without expecting to become a better taster.

See it in real life

Example

Sweet on all six chalkboards

Helga handed Lior a box of chalk on Wednesday. Grape weekend at her farm stand starts Saturday, and she wants a short line above each of the six grape bins so customers know what they’re choosing. He wrote “sweet” on all six.

He did taste them. Between customers on Thursday he worked down the row, popping in each grape the moment the last one was down, with gulps of lemonade in between, until they blurred into one sugary taste. When a woman asked which kind tasted flowery, like her grandfather’s grapes, he said they were all pretty sweet. She bought apples.

The first chew of a grape isn’t its whole flavor. Some of its aroma molecules are attached to sugars, in compounds called glycosides, and while attached they don’t drift up as smells. Enzymes in the mouth, some made by microbes living there, can split that bond, and the freed aroma travels up the back of the throat to the nose. That route, retronasal smell, adds a lot to flavor beyond sweet and sour. Residues left after a swallow can keep releasing aroma, so by reaching straight for the next grape, Lior was tasting first impressions and skipping what came after.

Before the stand opens on Friday, he goes down the bins again, one grape at a time. He chews each one normally and swallows, then leaves the next grape and the lemonade alone and compares what he noticed first with what is still arriving. He doesn’t time it.

The small purple grapes start out plainly sweet, and a moment after he swallows, something floral rises behind his nose. Two of the green kinds stay crisp and sweet all the way through, as far as he can tell. He rewrites every sign. On Saturday the woman from Thursday reads “sweet, then floral after you swallow” over the purple bin, eats a sample, waits a moment and buys two boxes.

  1. Where it stallsHe rushes through six kinds of grapes and writes “sweet” on every sign.
  2. What changes itHe eats one grape at a time and pauses after each swallow.
  3. What happens nextA floral note follows the purple ones, and it goes on the sign.

Try it yourself · 2 minutes

Nose pinched, nose open

You’ll need two small pieces of a food you already eat and know is safe for you, like grapes, apple slices or jelly beans.

  1. Pinch your nose shut, put the first piece in your mouth and chew it slowly while you breathe through your mouth. Notice what you can taste.
  2. Keep chewing and let go of your nose, and notice what arrives once air can move through it again.
  3. Swallow it the way you normally would. Before you eat or drink anything else, pause and compare what you noticed at first with what is still there.
  4. Eat the second piece the ordinary way, swallow and pause again, and notice whether anything shows up after the swallow that wasn’t there at the start.

What you’ll probably notice. With your nose pinched, a lot of foods taste mostly sweet, sour or salty, and the flavor comes back when you let go. That’s retronasal smell, aroma rising from your mouth through the back of your throat to your nose, and it adds a big part of what you experience as flavor. After a swallow, small residues can keep releasing aroma, so the pause sometimes catches a note the first bite didn’t have. Sometimes nothing changes, because foods differ and so do people’s noses.

Step by step

One bite, then a pause

For an ordinary meal or snack you enjoy. It’s a way to notice more of what’s already in your mouth, not a health habit or a course in tasting.

  1. Choose a familiar food. Pick something you already eat and like, such as a piece of fruit, a slice of bread or a favorite dish, and take one manageable bite.
  2. Chew and swallow as you usually would. Chew comfortably and swallow normally. There’s nothing to hold in your mouth and nothing to count.
  3. Pause before the next food or drink. Leave a short pause before another bite, a different food or a sip. No particular number of seconds is needed, since the flavor can keep changing after an ordinary swallow.
  4. Compare the start with what’s arriving now. Ask yourself what you noticed first and what is still coming through. Some bites change and some don’t, and people differ in how easily they pick up these aromas.
  5. Repeat it when it’s enjoyable. Do it with a bite here and there, whenever you feel like it. There’s no number of bites to reach and no promise that you’ll become a better taster.

How to tell it’s working. Next time you have that food, jot down a word or two for the first impression and a word or two for the aftertaste. Do it once with a pause and once with the next bite right after the swallow, and see whether the aftertaste words differ.

When it gets hard. At a busy table your hand reaches for the next bite out of habit. Set your fork or the next piece down when you swallow, and pick it up again once you’ve compared.

Good to know. Use only foods you already eat and know are safe for you, and skip anything you’re allergic to. Never hold food in your mouth for a set amount of time.
