The Sixth Sense You Use Every Second

Proprioception

Close your eyes and you still know exactly where your hand is. That is proprioception, a constant internal body map, and your brain can stretch it to include tools you use well.

The Science

  • Penfield and Boldrey (1937), Brain: mapping the cortex revealed a body shaped homunculus in the sensory strip, with hands and face taking up outsized space.
  • Proske and Gandevia (2012), Physiological Reviews: muscle and joint receptors feed a continuous sense of limb position and movement, the proprioceptive senses.
  • Iriki, Tanaka and Iwamura (1996), NeuroReport: after monkeys learned to use a rake, parietal neurons coded the tool as an extension of the hand, evidence the body map can absorb tools.
  • Net effect: skilled tools, a racket, a controller, a car, can feel like part of you because the brain literally incorporates them into the map.

The Protocol

  • Balance drill: stand on one leg while brushing your teeth to recalibrate the body map and sharpen focus.
  • Start eyes open, then try eyes closed once you are steady, switching legs halfway.
  • Pick one skill, a sport, instrument, or game, and practice it daily so the tool gets absorbed.
  • Notice the signal, name the mechanism, and change one input before autopilot takes over.

See it in real life

Example

Breaking the puck stare

Every time Cole carries the puck out of his own end, his chin drops to his chest. His coach calls it the puck stare, and in Tuesday’s scrimmage it cost him two turnovers to a forward he never saw. The spring roster goes up next week, with one defense spot open.

In warmups he stickhandles fine, as long as he’s looking. When he forces his eyes up, his hands go stiff and the puck slides off the blade.

Proprioception is the sense of where your body is without looking. Sensors in muscles and joints report position constantly, and the brain builds a body map from them, with the hands taking an outsized share. The map can even stretch: after monkeys learned to use a rake, neurons that map the hand began treating the rake as part of it, which may be why a stick can feel like part of your hand. Cole checks with his eyes on every touch, so his map never works alone.

For a week he trains the map. Morning and night he brushes his teeth on one leg beside the sink, switching legs halfway, eyes open at first and closed once he’s steady. After school he tapes a sticky note to the garage wall at eye level and stickhandles a tennis ball for ten slow minutes, eyes on the note, looking down only when he loses it.

The first day he loses the ball every few seconds. By day six he can go a full minute without looking down, and the eyes-closed brushing barely wobbles. In Saturday’s game he carries the puck out with his head up, sees the winger cutting through the middle and hits him in stride. No turnovers all game.

  2. What changes itOne leg while brushing, ten slow minutes stickhandling with eyes up
  3. What happens nextA full minute without looking down, then a clean pass

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

The eyes-closed check

Find a clear spot next to a wall or a counter you can reach. Besides that, you only need the stopwatch here.

  1. Stand next to the wall. Close your eyes, stretch one arm out to the side, then slowly touch the tip of your nose with your index finger, three times with each hand.
  2. Open your eyes. Stand on one leg with a hand hovering near the wall, and time how long you hold it, up to 30 seconds.
  3. Same leg, eyes closed, hand still near the wall. Time it again.
  4. If you wobble, touch the wall and stop. Then try both rounds on the other leg.

The timer needs JavaScript. A phone stopwatch works just as well.

What you’ll probably notice. Eyes shut, your finger most likely still finds your nose, which is proprioception at work: your muscles and joints report where your arm is with no help from your eyes. Balance usually goes the other way. With your eyes closed, the position signals from your ankles, knees and hips have to do more of the work, and most people wobble sooner. That eyes-closed round is the part the plan trains.

Step by step

Train the body map

Two minutes at the sink, plus ten minutes with one tool.

  1. Brush your teeth on one leg. Morning and night, standing next to the counter. Switch legs halfway through.
  2. Close your eyes once you’re steady. When eyes open feels easy, close them for part of it, with one hand hovering over the counter. Stop if you feel dizzy.
  3. Pick one tool skill. A stick, a racket, a controller, a bow, a stylus: something you want to feel like part of your hand.
  4. Practice it slowly, eyes off your hands. Ten minutes a day, slow enough to stay clean. Look at a mark on the wall, the music or the screen instead of your hands, and glance down only when you lose it.
  5. Write down your seconds. Each day, note how long you balanced with your eyes closed and how long you went without looking at your hands.

How to tell it’s working. Compare day one with day seven: seconds balanced with your eyes closed, and seconds before you glanced down. If both numbers go up, your body map is getting clearer.

When it gets hard. Slow practice feels like going backward, especially if you’re used to playing fast. Keep it slow anyway; this week the goal is not looking, and speed can wait.

Good to know. Balance on a flat floor next to a counter or wall, and stop if you feel dizzy. If you notice sudden trouble with balance or with knowing where your body is, tell a parent and see a doctor.
