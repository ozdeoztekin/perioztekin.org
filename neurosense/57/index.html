The Sixth Sense You Use Every Second

Proprioception

Close your eyes and you still know exactly where your hand is. That is proprioception, a constant internal body map, and your brain can stretch it to include tools you use well.

The Science

  • Penfield and Boldrey (1937), Brain: electrically stimulating the brain surface of awake patients during surgery revealed a body shaped map, the homunculus, across the movement and touch strips, with hands and face taking up outsized space.
  • Proske and Gandevia (2012), Physiological Reviews: muscle and joint receptors feed a continuous sense of limb position and movement, the proprioceptive senses.
  • Iriki, Tanaka and Iwamura (1996), NeuroReport: in monkeys trained to use a rake, brain cells that combine touch and sight near the hand stretched their range along the rake after a few minutes of active use, but not when the monkeys only held it, evidence the body map can stretch to include tools.
  • Net effect: skilled tools, a racket, a controller, a car, can feel like part of you, and monkey research suggests the brain's body map can stretch to include them.

The Protocol

  • Balance drill: stand on one leg while brushing your teeth to practice balance and your sense of where your body is.
  • Start eyes open, then try eyes closed once you are steady, switching legs halfway.
  • Pick one skill, a sport, instrument, or game, and practice it daily so handling the tool becomes second nature.
  • Notice the signal, name the mechanism, and change one input before autopilot takes over.

See it in real life

Example

Dribbling with his head up

The second steal happened so fast that Cole never saw who took the ball. One moment he was bringing it up the court, eyes glued to the floor as usual, and the next a guard was gone the other way for a layup. His coach calls it the ball stare, and with one guard spot open on the spring roster, Cole knows it could keep him off the list.

In warmups he’s fine, as long as he can watch the ball. When he tries to keep his head up, his hand stiffens and the ball bounces off his foot.

His body can already sense what his eyes keep checking. It’s called proprioception, and it’s how you can touch your nose with your eyes closed. Sensors in your muscles and joints keep reporting where everything is, and your brain turns those reports into a running map of your body. With practice the map can even stretch to include things you use. Monkeys trained to use a rake had brain cells that treated it as part of their hand while they were using it, and a ball you handle every day may work in a similar way. The map just never gets a chance when your eyes check every bounce.

So for a week he practices not looking. He brushes his teeth standing on one leg, morning and night, and once he’s steady he closes his eyes. After school he tapes a note to the garage door at eye level and dribbles slowly for ten minutes with his eyes on the note, glancing down only when he loses the ball, which at first is every few seconds.

By Friday he can go a whole minute without looking. On Saturday he brings the ball up with his head up, sees a teammate cutting to the basket, and gets it to him in stride for an easy layup. It’s his first game all season without a turnover.

  1. Where it stallsHe watches the ball so closely that he never sees the guard coming.
  2. What changes itHe spends a week balancing on one leg and dribbling with his eyes on a note.
  3. What happens nextHe brings the ball up with his head up and sets up an easy layup.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

The eyes-closed check

All you need is a spot next to a wall or a kitchen counter, and the stopwatch below.

  1. Stand next to the wall, close your eyes and stretch one arm out to the side. Then slowly bring your finger in and touch the tip of your nose. Do it three times with each hand.
  2. Open your eyes, stand on one leg with a hand hovering near the wall, and time how long you can hold it, up to 30 seconds.
  3. Now try the same leg with your eyes closed, hand still near the wall, and time it again.
  4. If you start to wobble, touch the wall and stop. Then try both on the other leg.

The timer needs JavaScript. A phone stopwatch works just as well.

What you’ll probably notice. You probably found your nose every time, even with your eyes shut. That’s proprioception doing its job, with your muscles and joints telling your brain where your arm is. Balance is usually a different story. With your eyes closed, your muscles, joints and inner ear have to do the reporting, and most people start to wobble a lot sooner. That eyes-closed balance is exactly what the plan below trains.

Step by step

Train your body map

It takes two minutes at the sink and ten minutes with one skill.

  1. Brush your teeth on one leg. Do it morning and night, standing next to the counter, and switch legs halfway through.
  2. Close your eyes once you feel steady. When balancing with your eyes open starts to feel easy, close them for part of the time, with one hand hovering over the counter. Stop if you feel dizzy.
  3. Pick one skill that uses something in your hands. Choose something you’d like to feel like part of your hand, such as a ball, a racket, a game controller or a pencil.
  4. Practice it slowly without watching your hands. Give it ten minutes a day, slow enough that you stay in control. Keep your eyes on a spot on the wall, on the music or on the screen, and look down only when you lose it.
  5. Write down your seconds. Each day, note how long you balanced with your eyes closed and how long you went without looking at your hands.

How to tell it’s working. On day seven, compare both numbers with day one. If you can balance longer with your eyes closed and go longer without glancing down, your body map is getting sharper.

When it gets hard. Going slowly can feel like going backward, especially if you’re used to playing fast. Stick with it for the week. Right now the goal is not looking, and the speed will come back.

Good to know. Do the balance work on a flat floor next to a counter or a wall, and stop if you feel dizzy. If your balance or your sense of where your body is suddenly gets worse, tell a parent and see a doctor.
