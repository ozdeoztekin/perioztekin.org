The Calculator Can Be Right About the Wrong Input

Number Sense & Calculator Checks

A calculator computes exactly what you entered; a rough prediction made first can flag a wrong entry.

The Science

  • A calculator performs the operation you entered, so a wrong number still produces a precise answer, and its decimal places will not warn you.
  • Knowing a formula stores a rule; working through problems makes you use it and notice where your answer fails. Those are different learning demands.
  • With numbers, active practice can build a sense of scale, roughly how large a result should be, an expectation formed apart from the calculator.

The Protocol

  • Predict the rough size first: a fifteen percent tip on a bill near fifty dollars should come to around seven or eight dollars.
  • Then calculate exactly; if the screen says hundreds of dollars, inspect the entry, the decimal placement or the operation.
  • Check the intended unit too, and give important work its detailed checks, since a plausible estimate cannot prove correctness.

See it in real life

Example

Three bowls of pho at $186.30 each

Radek’s friends always hand him the check, because his calculator is open before anyone else’s. Last month at the bowling alley he read “two forty-three each” off his phone, and everyone paid it without checking, because it came with cents. A few minutes later the server came back with the folder. He had split the tip three ways and left out the bill.

Tonight it’s pho with Janek and Josefa after their last shift at the pool snack bar. The bill is $48.60, they want to leave fifteen percent, and Janek is already sliding the check across the table.

A calculator does exactly the operation you enter, so a wrong number or a wrong step still produces a precise answer, and its decimal places won’t warn you. Knowing a formula stores a rule, while working through problems makes you use it and notice when an answer fails. With numbers, that practice can build a sense of scale, roughly how large a result should be, an expectation formed apart from the calculator. Last month Radek had nothing to hold the screen up against.

This time, before he touches the phone, he sizes it up. Ten percent of about fifty is five dollars, and half of that again puts the tip around seven or eight, so each of them owes a little under twenty. Then he calculates, and the screen says $186.30 each. The mismatch tells him where to look, and he finds the decimal point in the bill typed one place too late. Entered again, it comes to $18.63. A sensible size doesn’t prove an answer right, so he checks it the long way too, multiplying his share back by three and comparing it with the bill plus the tip.

The folder goes back with the right amount in it. At the register the server counts it, looks over and gives them a thumbs-up on their way out.

  1. Where it stallsA share with cents on it fooled everyone, Radek included.
  2. What changes itHe estimates each share before he touches the calculator.
  3. What happens nextHe spots the slipped decimal, and the folder goes back right.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

Guess the size first

Use a calculator or a phone and something to write on. One entry below gets slipped on purpose, so you can see what a wrong answer looks like.

  1. Before you calculate anything, write a rough size for three things, a fifteen percent tip on $48.60, twelve notebooks at $3.85 each and a $64 jacket at 20 percent off.
  2. Calculate each one exactly, but for the notebooks, type the price as 385, with no decimal point.
  3. Put each screen next to your rough size and circle any answer that’s far off.
  4. Fix the circled entry and calculate it again.

What you’ll probably notice. A slipped entry still gives a confident, exact answer, $4,620 for twelve notebooks, and nothing on the screen says it’s wrong. Your rough size, around $46, is what catches it. An answer that lands near your estimate is only sensible in size, though, since a wrong price or a slip that happens to land close can still get through.

Step by step

Size it up, then press equals

For any calculator answer you’re about to trust with money, a grade or a recipe.

  1. Predict the rough size first. Before you touch the calculator, ask whether the answer should be bigger or smaller than what you started with, and about how big. For a fifteen percent tip on a bill near fifty dollars, ten percent is about five, and half that again puts the tip around seven or eight.
  2. Then calculate exactly. Let the calculator do the precise part, which is the job it’s good at.
  3. Compare the screen with your guess. If they’re far apart, like hundreds of dollars where you expected eight, check the entry, the decimal point and the operation before you accept anything.
  4. Check the unit. Make sure the answer is in the unit you meant, like dollars rather than cents or feet rather than inches, since a conversion slip can survive correct arithmetic.
  5. Give important work its full check. An answer close to your estimate is only sensible in size. For anything that matters, check it in detail too, for example by working it backward.

How to tell it’s working. For a week, write your rough size next to each calculator answer you use. Count how often the two disagreed and what you found when you looked, whether it was a slipped decimal, a wrong operation or an estimate that needed work.

When it gets hard. Estimating can feel slow with the calculator right there. Keep it rough, one or two digits, since its job is to catch answers that are wildly off, not to replace the calculation.
