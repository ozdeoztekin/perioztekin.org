Why Your Eyes Make Your Brain Tired

Screen Eye Strain

When words start swimming on a screen, pushing harder can make your whole brain feel tired.

The Science

  • Sustained near focus asks your eyes to hold alignment and focus for a long time.
  • Screens can reduce blinking and add blur, dryness, or eye strain.
  • That discomfort can make the work feel harder, and it may cut into how much people get done.

The Protocol

  • Look away from the screen before the blur turns into a battle.
  • Focus on a far object and let your face soften for several breaths.
  • Return by reading one small section instead of the whole page.

See it in real life

Example

Pickle’s ear on page 6

The line along the dog’s back has started to move. Willa has inked it three times on her tablet, and every stroke seems to double and slide.

Her best friend, Juno, is entering a picture book in a young writers’ contest that closes on Sunday, and Willa promised the pictures, twelve pages inked and colored by Friday. She has been at a table by the library’s second-floor window since right after school, with her eyes hot and dry and her forehead scrunched, leaning closer to the screen, which makes it worse. She’s starting to wonder whether she’s bad at this.

Two hours of drawing a foot from her face means her eyes have held their focus and alignment on one close point the whole time. People tend to blink less when they look at screens, too, so the eyes dry out and the lines blur. That discomfort makes the drawing harder to stay with, and from the inside it feels like losing her touch.

She stops before the blur turns into a fight. Past the parking lot, a water tower stands on a hill, the farthest thing she can see, and she keeps her eyes on it for several slow breaths, letting her forehead, her jaw and her eyelids relax. Then she comes back to one small piece instead of the whole page, the single line along the dog’s back.

It goes on clean in one stroke. When she zooms out on page 6, she catches something she had missed twice, which is that Pickle’s black spot is on his left ear on page 1 and his right ear here. She takes the same far look at the end of every page, and by closing time she has inked through page 9. On Friday afternoon she sends all twelve, with “fixed Pickle’s ear” at the top.

  1. Where it stallsShe inks the same line three times with hot, dry eyes.
  2. What changes itShe looks at the water tower for several breaths and redoes one line.
  3. What happens nextShe catches Pickle’s spot switching ears and inks through page 9.

Try it yourself · 2 minutes

Near, then far

You’ll need a pen, a window or a long hallway and the screen you usually read on.

  1. Hold the pen at reading distance and keep its tip sharp for 30 seconds, and notice the effort around your eyes.
  2. Look at the farthest thing you can see, out a window or down a hallway, for five slow breaths, and let your forehead and jaw go loose.
  3. Read something on your screen for one minute, and notice how often you blink.
  4. Look far again for a few breaths, and notice what changes.

What you’ll probably notice. Holding focus up close is a small, steady effort you usually don’t feel until you let it go, and looking far away is how you let it go. Many people also catch themselves barely blinking while they read on a screen. Steady close focus plus fewer blinks is how eye strain builds, which is the reason the reset uses distance instead of more effort.

Step by step

The far-look reset

Use it for long stretches of reading or drawing on a screen.

  1. Catch the first blur. When you notice blurring, doubling or rereading the same line, stop there, before it turns into a fight.
  2. Find the farthest thing you can see. Look at a tree line, a roof or a light down the street, and keep your eyes on it for several slow breaths.
  3. Let your face relax. Loosen your forehead, your jaw and the skin around your eyes, and blink a few times on purpose.
  4. Come back to one small section. Go back to a paragraph, a single scene or one part of a drawing instead of the whole page.
  5. Reset at every natural break. The end of a scene, a section or a problem set is the moment to look far before you go on.

How to tell it’s working. Count how many times you reread the same line during half an hour of screen reading with no resets, and then during half an hour with a far look at every break. Fewer rereads, or eyes that feel less dry at the end, mean the far looks are helping.

When it gets hard. When you’re on a roll, stopping to look at a tree feels like quitting. Tie the reset to breaks that are already there, so it costs you a few breaths and nothing else.

Good to know. If pain, blurry vision or headaches keep coming back, get your eyes checked.
