Teach Your Brain When It Is Time to Sleep

Sleep Cues and Conditioning

Sleep can get harder when bed becomes the place for scrolling, worrying, and negotiating.

The Science

  • Your nervous system learns the meaning of a place from what repeatedly happens there.
  • Cues can teach your body what state is expected next.
  • If the cue mix is noisy, your body may not know if bed means rest or alertness.

The Protocol

  • Lower the light at the same point each night when you can.
  • Move the device away before you get under the covers.
  • Repeat one quiet action, like reading two pages or stretching gently.

See it in real life

Example

The bed that learned to stay awake

For three weeks after he broke his ankle, Marcus did everything in bed. He did schoolwork on a laptop propped on a pillow, ate dinner off a tray, played games until midnight and watched every video anyone sent him.

Now he’s back at school in a walking boot, tired all day and wide awake at night. He gets into bed before eleven and is still up after one, with the phone on his chest, scrolling, worrying about the chemistry he missed and doing sleep math, like how if he falls asleep now he’ll get five hours and forty minutes. He has missed the bus twice this week.

The nervous system learns what a place means from what keeps happening there, and for three weeks this one meant screens, talking and being wide awake. Now the signals are mixed, because bed is supposed to mean sleep, but most of what happens there still says stay up. Promising himself an early night doesn’t change that, but a new pattern can.

So he builds three cues and runs them every night. When he finishes brushing his teeth, the ceiling light goes off and only the desk lamp stays on. Before he gets under the covers, the phone and the controller go on the dresser by the door, which he can limp to now. Then he reads two pages of the paperback his cousin left and turns off the lamp. The first few nights he’s still awake past midnight, so when sleep won’t come, he gets up, reads in the desk chair until he’s sleepy and goes back to bed, without the phone or the sleep math.

On the ninth night he wakes up at two with the lamp still on and the book open on his chest, partway down the second page. By the end of the second week he’s usually yawning when the ceiling light goes off, and he makes the bus five mornings in a row.

  1. Where it stallsAfter three weeks of doing everything in bed, he’s awake past one.
  2. What changes itHe switches to the desk lamp, puts the phone on the dresser and reads two pages.
  3. What happens nextBy night nine he falls asleep with the book on his chest.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

What your bed has learned

This has two parts, one you can feel right now and one you write down. You’ll need your phone, a pen and paper.

  1. Turn your phone’s volume down low and play your morning alarm sound for two seconds, and notice what your body does before you think anything.
  2. Write down the last ten things you did in bed besides sleeping, like scrolling, homework, calls, games, shows or lying there worrying.
  3. Mark each one R if it’s restful and A if it keeps you alert, and count the A’s.
  4. Circle one A you could move somewhere else starting tonight.

What you’ll probably notice. Two seconds of your alarm sound can be enough for a small jolt or a groan, even in the middle of the afternoon. Nobody decided that. The sound has been paired with waking up so many times that your body answers on its own, a kind of learning called conditioning. Your bed learns the same way, from whatever happens in it, so a list full of A’s means it’s getting mixed signals.

Step by step

Teach your bed one meaning

Give it more than a few nights, since the cues work through repetition.

  1. Lower the lights at the same point. Tie it to something you already do every night, like brushing your teeth, and switch off the bright lights and turn on one low lamp at about the same time when you can.
  2. Put devices away before the covers. Put the phone, tablet and controller somewhere you’d have to get up to reach. If your phone is your alarm, a cheap alarm clock solves that.
  3. Repeat one quiet action. Read two pages of the same book or do a few slow stretches, with the same action in the same order every night.
  4. Do the awake stuff somewhere else. Do homework at a desk or table, and games and calls in a chair or another room, so the bed stops collecting reasons to stay up.
  5. Don’t turn it into a battle. If you’re lying there wide awake, get up, do something calm in dim light until you’re sleepy and go back to bed, with no phone and no sleep math, since the pattern matters more than any one night.

How to tell it’s working. Each morning, jot down roughly when you got into bed and when you think you fell asleep. Compare your first three nights with your last three, and notice whether you start yawning around your lights-down moment.

When it gets hard. The first few nights may feel exactly the same, and that’s when the phone starts to look reasonable. Leave it on the dresser anyway, because the cues only teach anything if they happen the same way on the nights it isn’t working yet.

Good to know. New bed cues help with everyday trouble falling asleep. If you lie awake for a long time most nights, snore loudly or feel worn out even after full nights, talk to a parent and a doctor.
