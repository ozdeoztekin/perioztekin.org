Why Letting Anger Out Doesn't Drain It

Catharsis & Lingering Anger

Punching a pillow can keep anger going, not drain it. Angry states tend to outlast whatever set them off.

The Science

  • Catharsis is the idea that acting aggressively drains anger into harmless channels, but acting aggressively can keep anger going instead.
  • Emotional states can outlast their trigger. Hunger can ease when you eat, but after a fight you can stay riled up long after it is over.
  • Anger has two parts you can work on: your body's revved-up arousal and the meaning you give the event that set it off.

The Protocol

  • To bring the arousal down, count to ten before you respond, or put on calming music.
  • To reframe, consider a different reading, such as whether someone brushing past with a heavy box simply had full hands.
  • Or try an activity that pulls you toward a different response, like watching a comedy or helping someone.

See it in real life

Example

Seed packets all over the floor

Ingrid spent the first hour of her Saturday shift at the garden center building a seed display, every packet sorted by color. At noon her coworker Ambrose swung past with a bag of potting soil on his shoulder, clipped the rack and kept walking, and packets slid across the floor.

“Get it out of your system,” her mom says, so Ingrid yells about Ambrose the whole drive home, types a furious text to him and punches her pillow until her arms ache. An hour later her jaw is still clenched. At six she goes next door to babysit the twins, and when they scream over a toy dump truck, she snaps so loudly that the younger one cries.

Her mom’s advice is the idea behind catharsis, that acting aggressively drains anger into harmless channels, but acting aggressively can keep anger going instead. Hunger can ease when you eat, but anger may not end when you let it out. It’s a state that can outlast its trigger and spill over onto whoever comes next. Anger has two parts she can work on, her body’s revved-up arousal and the meaning she gave the moment.

Ingrid counts to ten on her fingers before she says anything else and puts on the slow piano playlist the twins’ mom plays at nap time. Then she tries another reading of noon. Ambrose had a heavy bag on his shoulder and may never have seen the rack, so maybe his hands were full. She doesn’t know, but the version where he meant it is the one keeping her shaking.

Last, she puts on something that pulls her the other way, the cartoon where a dog keeps falling into a pool. By the third splash all three of them are laughing on the rug. When the twins’ mom gets home at nine, Ingrid deletes the text to Ambrose without sending it.

  1. Where it stallsShe yells and punches her pillow, and an hour later she’s still clenched.
  2. What changes itShe counts to ten, puts on slow music and tries another reading.
  3. What happens nextShe and the twins laugh at a cartoon, and the angry text gets deleted.

Try it yourself · 2 minutes

The ringing lid

You’ll need a metal pot lid, not glass, and a wooden spoon. It’s a model of the body side of anger, not of a person, but it shows how a state can outlast what set it off.

  1. Hold the lid up by its knob and tap the edge once with the spoon. Count the seconds the sound keeps going after the spoon has left.
  2. Tap it again, and while it’s still ringing, hit it a little harder to try to make the ringing stop.
  3. Tap it once more, then press your palm flat against the lid and listen.
  4. Last, tap it and let it ring out on its own while you count.

What you’ll probably notice. The ring keeps going after the tap, the way an angry state can keep running after its trigger is over. Hitting the lid harder to stop the sound starts a bigger ring instead, a lot like trying to get anger out by acting aggressively. Your palm quiets it fastest, which is the lid’s version of bringing the body’s arousal down. The lid can’t show the other part of anger, the meaning you give what happened, so that part lives in the plan.

Step by step

End the state instead of feeding it

Save it for when you’re furious and the thing that set you off is already over.

  1. Skip the venting. Punching a pillow, slamming doors or yelling in the car can keep anger going. Notice the urge and let it pass without acting it out.
  2. Count to ten before you respond. Count on your fingers or under your breath before you say or send anything, so your body’s revved-up state has a chance to come down.
  3. Put on calming music. Pick something slow that you already know, and let it play while the worst of it passes.
  4. Try another reading. Ask what else could explain what happened, like full hands, a heavy box or someone who didn’t see you. You don’t have to be sure, only open to it.
  5. Do something that pulls you the other way. Watch something funny or help someone with a small job. A different response can help interrupt the angry state.

How to tell it’s working. The next few times you get angry, note how many minutes pass before you feel settled and whether it spilled onto someone else. Compare the times you vented with the times you used these steps.

When it gets hard. When you’re hot, ten seconds can feel like forever. Count out loud on your fingers, and if someone is waiting for an answer, say “Give me a minute.”

Good to know. If anger often ends with you hurting someone or breaking things, or it’s hard to come down most days, ask a parent or a counselor at school for help, and see your doctor. If someone is threatening you, get somewhere safe and tell an adult.
