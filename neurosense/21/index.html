Why Your Brain NEEDS You to Fail

LTP & Plasticity

You're not bad at it. Your brain is rewiring, and the misses are part of the practice.

The Science

  • Bliss & Lømo (Journal of Physiology, 1973): in anesthetized rabbits, bursts of high-frequency stimulation made a hippocampal pathway respond more strongly for hours, the first description of long-term potentiation (LTP). Later work showed that at many synapses LTP depends on NMDA receptors, which let calcium in only when both sides of the synapse are active, and that it adds AMPA receptors that strengthen the connection.
  • Hebb (1949) proposed that when one neuron repeatedly helps fire another, the connection between them strengthens, an idea later summed up as 'neurons that fire together wire together.' LTP is one way synapses carry out this rule.
  • Yu & Dayan (Neuron, 2005) proposed, in a computational model, that acetylcholine signals expected uncertainty and norepinephrine signals unexpected surprises, helping the brain decide when to update what it has learned. Separately, studies of learning find that making an error and then getting the right answer can help it stick (Metcalfe, 2017).
  • Walker et al. (Neuron, 2002): after a night of sleep, people who had practiced a finger-tapping sequence were about 20% faster with no loss of accuracy, while the same hours awake brought no gain.

The Protocol

  • Pick ONE skill: a song, a problem type, a movement.
  • Set a 15-minute timer and practice at the edge of what you can do, where you still make mistakes.
  • When you miss, check what went wrong and try again; errors followed by the right answer are part of how practice works.
  • Sleep that night, properly; sleep after practice helps a new skill stick.

See it in real life

Example

Forty-three drops in the driveway

The ball bounces off Quinn’s knee and rolls under her mom’s car, the third one in a minute. On Saturday she has a busking spot at the farmers market next to the bread stand, and the trick she wants in the middle of her act is a single throw under her leg without breaking the three-ball pattern.

She can keep the plain pattern going for a full minute. But every practice this week has gone the same way. She tries the under-the-leg throw twice, drops it twice and goes back to the plain pattern, because that one never falls apart.

A move like that gets built out of connections between brain cells. When the cells on both sides of a connection are active together again and again, the connection gets stronger and stays stronger, which is called long-term potentiation. So the connections behind the new throw only get worked when she throws it, whether she drops it or not. Studies of learning also suggest that making a mistake and then getting it right can help something stick, and sleep after practice helps a new skill settle in.

On Wednesday after school she sets a timer for fifteen minutes and makes one rule, which is that every fourth throw goes under the leg. She drops, chases the ball and starts again, over and over. Around minute nine, with a ball in the hedge for the fifth time, the plain pattern starts to look very good, but she fishes the ball out and throws the next one under anyway. When the timer rings she stops, and that night she’s asleep by ten.

She dropped the ball 43 times on Wednesday. On Thursday before school she tries ten under-the-leg throws, and six come back clean. On Saturday, next to the bread stand, she lands three in a row, and a little kid in a stroller claps and yells for another one.

  1. Where it stallsShe tries the new throw twice, drops it twice and goes back to the safe pattern.
  2. What changes itFor fifteen minutes, every fourth throw goes under her leg, and she’s asleep by ten.
  3. What happens nextShe lands six of ten on Thursday and three in a row on Saturday.

Try it yourself · 10 minutes

The elbow coin catch

This is a small move with plenty of misses built in. You’ll need ten coins of the same size and a rug or carpet, so dropped coins don’t roll far.

  1. Bend one arm so your hand is beside your ear and your forearm lies flat, palm up, like a shelf. Set one coin on the flat part near your elbow.
  2. Swing your hand forward and down fast and try to catch the coin in that same hand. Add a coin each time you catch the whole stack, until you miss twice in a row. That number is your edge.
  3. Do ten tries at your edge and count the catches.
  4. Practice at your edge for five minutes, expecting to miss most of the time. When you’ve missed several in a row, take the next try anyway.
  5. Do ten more tries and count again. If you can, do ten more tomorrow before you practice.

What you’ll probably notice. The second count is usually higher, even though the five minutes in between were mostly misses. The connections behind the move got used again and again, and each miss showed you something to change. If you test again tomorrow, the number often holds or climbs, since sleep after practice helps a new skill settle in.

Step by step

Fifteen minutes at the edge

Start with the move you keep backing away from because it goes wrong.

  1. Pick one move. Choose one thing, like an under-the-leg throw, a chord change, one type of problem or the four bars of a song that keep falling apart. Everything else can wait for another session.
  2. Set a timer for 15 minutes. Ten minutes works on a busy day. When it rings, stop, even in the middle of a good streak.
  3. Work on the version you still miss. Do the move the way it has to work when it counts, at full speed, and expect to miss a lot of the early tries. Take the next one right when the safe version starts looking good, and if you stop missing, make it a little harder.
  4. Sleep that night. Go to bed on time and get a full night, since sleep after practice helps a new skill settle in.
  5. Test it before you warm up. Next session, do ten tries cold and count the clean ones. That number tells you more than how the end of yesterday felt.

How to tell it’s working. Write down your clean tries out of ten at the start of each session. If that number climbs over a week, even after sessions that felt like nothing but misses, the misses are doing their job.

When it gets hard. After three or four misses in a row, the safe version starts to look very reasonable. Instead of switching, shrink the move and practice one piece of it, like the under-the-leg throw with a single ball or only the first two bars, then put it back together.

Good to know. Missing is the point here, but pain isn’t. Stop for anything that hurts, and practice moves that involve falls, water or heights only where a coach or another adult normally supervises them.
