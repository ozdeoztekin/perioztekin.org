Sweat Changes the Signal

Sodium, Electrolytes & Hydration

Hydration is not only water; your nerves also depend on electrolytes moving correctly.

The Science

  • Sodium helps cells fire signals, hold fluid balance, and respond during hard effort.
  • Heavy sweating loses water and salt at the same time.
  • Needs vary by body, heat, training, diet, and blood pressure, so context matters.

The Protocol

  • For long hot practice, plan water plus electrolytes, food, and smart breaks.
  • Do not overload salt or copy someone else's bottle.
  • Stop and get support if you feel dizzy, confused, or unusually weak.

See it in real life

Example

A step late at four o’clock

By Wednesday of preseason two-a-days, Ian’s navy practice shirt dries stiff, with white lines across the chest. Practice runs at 7:30 in the morning and again at four, and he’s competing with a senior to start at left guard.

He drinks all day, topping off a gallon jug of plain water at every break. But at four o’clock he’s a step late off the ball on the same inside run he blocked cleanly that morning, and the line coach makes him run it twice more. At the water tent, Darnell holds out his own bottle, bright orange from three packets of powder. “This is what I do.”

The white lines are salt. Heavy sweating pulls out water and salt together, and sodium helps cells fire signals, hold fluid and respond during hard effort. Gallons of plain water may not match what left. A triple-strength mix made for Darnell isn’t the fix either, since needs vary with the body, the heat, the training, diet and blood pressure.

Between practices Ian shows the athletic trainer his shirt, and she helps him plan the long hot days. A real breakfast, and a full lunch between sessions. Water at every break, plus one sports drink at lunch and one in the afternoon. Pretzels in his bag. Breaks under the sideline tent, off the turf. And if he ever feels dizzy, confused or unusually weak, he stops and tells her, whatever drill they’re in.

In the last quarter of Friday’s scrimmage, at the hottest point of the afternoon, Ian is off the ball on every snap, and the line coach sends him in with the first group for the final series. On Monday, Darnell asks the trainer for a plan of his own.

  1. Where it stallsA gallon jug of plain water, and a step late at four
  2. What changes itReal meals, water plus sports drinks, pretzels, breaks under the tent
  3. What happens nextOff the ball on every snap, then in with the first group

Try it yourself · one evening

Salt stays behind

A model, with nothing to taste. You need table salt, warm water, two cups, a cotton swab and a sheet of black or dark blue paper.

  1. Stir a teaspoon of salt into three spoonfuls of warm water until you can’t see it anymore. Pour plain water into the other cup.
  2. With the swab, paint a thick stripe of each across the paper, going over each one two or three times, and label which is which.
  3. Leave the paper somewhere warm, like a sunny windowsill, until both stripes are completely dry. That can take an hour or more.
  4. Tilt the paper toward the light and compare the two stripes.

What you’ll probably notice. The plain water vanishes without a trace. The salt water leaves a white line, the same kind that shows up on a dark shirt after a long, hot practice. Sweat works the same way, only much less salty, which is why a shirt takes hours to show it: the water leaves into the air, and the salt that came out with it stays on the fabric. Water and salt leave together, so a heavy-sweat day can need more than plain water, while on an ordinary cool day, regular meals often cover it.

Step by step

Plan for the long hot days

For practices, games or shifts that run long in the heat. A cool study day doesn’t need any of this.

  1. Eat real meals around it. Breakfast before and a full lunch on long days. Real food often covers more than fancy powders do.
  2. Bring water plus electrolytes. Water at every break, and for long sweaty stretches something with electrolytes too, like a sports drink or a salty snack. Follow the label and don’t overload.
  3. Don’t copy someone else’s bottle. A friend’s mix fits their body, their sweat and their day. Start from the label, or ask your coach or athletic trainer.
  4. Take breaks in the shade. Sit down out of the sun when you can, instead of standing on hot ground.
  5. Dizzy, confused or weak? Stop. Stop right away and tell a coach, trainer or adult. Those can be signs of a real problem, not something to push through.

How to tell it’s working. On a few long hot days, write down how you feel in the last hour and whether your timing or form holds up. Compare water-only days with planned days. A dark shirt after practice also shows if you’re a salty sweater.

When it gets hard. Sports drinks can turn into all-day sipping, even on cool days at a desk. Keep them for long hot sessions, and let water and meals handle the rest.

Good to know. Heat illness can come on fast. If you or a teammate feels dizzy, confused or unusually weak in the heat, stop, get to shade and tell an adult right away. If you have a health condition or take medication, ask a doctor before changing how much salt or water you have.
