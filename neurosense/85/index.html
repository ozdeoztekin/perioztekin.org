Why Brain Explanations Need Care

Neuro-Law & Responsibility

When someone says their brain made them do it, slow down. Brain science can explain why self-control was harder.

The Science

  • Sapolsky (2017), Behave: brain science can explain why self-control was harder, but it does not automatically erase harm or responsibility.
  • The prefrontal cortex helps pause impulses and weigh consequences, and damage or development can weaken that control.
  • A brain scan cannot tell the whole moral story by itself.
  • The claim deserves a slow-down, not a free pass.

The Protocol

  • Separate explanation from accountability: name the brain factor.
  • Name the harm honestly.
  • Name the protection or repair needed next.
  • Notice the signal, name the mechanism, and change one input before autopilot.

See it in real life

Example

Ninety words about her own brain

Mariam’s apology to Hana is 140 words long, and 90 of them are about Mariam’s brain.

On Sunday, Hana sent her a video of herself practicing the choir solo she’s auditioning for on Friday, voice cracking on the high note, laughing at herself. “Don’t show anyone.” The group chat was joking about bad singing right then, and two seconds later Mariam had posted it there. Eleven people saw it before she deleted it, and Jayden saved it. On Monday, Hana walked past her locker without stopping.

Every draft leans on the same line: “My prefrontal cortex is literally still developing.”

There’s some truth in it. The prefrontal cortex helps pause impulses and weigh consequences, and development can weaken that control. But explaining why self-control was harder doesn’t erase the harm, and even a brain scan couldn’t tell the whole moral story. The useful question is what made the choice harder, and what repair still matters.

Her fourth draft has three parts. The brain factor gets one sentence: she posted it in two seconds because it was funny, without stopping to think. The harm, said plainly: “Eleven people saw your practice video right before your audition, and you trusted me with it.” The repair: Jayden has deleted his copy and sent her proof, she promises never to post anything Hana sends her, and she asks, “What else would help?” It comes to about fifty words, most of them about Hana.

Hana doesn’t answer until Tuesday night: “ok. thank you for not making it about your brain.” On Friday she asks Mariam to wait outside the choir room and hold her water bottle.

  1. Where it stallsAn apology draft with 90 of its 140 words about her brain
  2. What changes itThree parts: the brain factor, the harm, the repair
  3. What happens nextA reply Tuesday night, and a water bottle to hold on Friday

Try it yourself · 10 minutes

The word count test

Pick a small mistake of your own from the last month, like a forgotten favor or a sharp comment. You need paper and a pen.

  1. Write the apology you’d send right now, fast, without editing.
  2. Underline every word about you: your reasons, your brain, how tired or stressed you were. Circle every word about them: what happened to them and what you’ll do about it.
  3. Count the underlined words and the circled ones.
  4. Rewrite it in three sentences: the factor in one, the harm in one, the repair in one. Count again.

What you’ll probably notice. First drafts are often mostly underlined, because the explanation tends to come out first and take up the most room. The three-sentence version usually flips the count. The explanation still gets its sentence; it just stops standing in front of the harm, because a reason for a mistake doesn’t erase what it did.

Step by step

Explain it, then repair it

For the next time you’ve hurt someone and the first thing you want to say is why.

  1. Name the brain factor once. One sentence on what made the choice harder, like acting in two seconds because a crowd was laughing. Then stop explaining.
  2. Name the harm plainly. Say what happened to the other person in their terms, including who saw it and what trust broke. Don’t soften it.
  3. Name the repair and one protection. Say what you’ve already done to fix it, and one rule so it doesn’t happen again. Then ask what else would help.
  4. Deliver it without the excuse. Send it, or better, say it in person. If you catch yourself adding “but my brain,” cut it.
  5. Keep the protection. Put the rule where you’ll see it this week, like a note on your lock screen, and keep it whether or not they’ve forgiven you.

How to tell it’s working. Before you send an apology, count the words about you and the words about them. Afterward, notice whether the other person responds to the repair or argues with the excuse.

When it gets hard. If they aren’t ready to talk, that’s part of the harm, and pushing for a quick “it’s fine” makes it about you again. Do the repair anyway and let them choose when.

Good to know. If someone got hurt, or something serious or illegal happened, bring in a parent or another adult you trust. Some repairs are too big to handle alone.
