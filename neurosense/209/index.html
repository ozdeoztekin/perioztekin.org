Why Trying to Fall Asleep Keeps You Awake

Sleep Effort & Arousal

Forcing sleep does not work well; turning down arousal can matter more than trying harder.

The Science

  • All day, sleep pressure builds, quieting your brain's wake system and boosting its sleep system.
  • The wake side, your arousal system, can overrule sleep so you can respond to danger; when one system switches on, the other usually switches off.
  • Clock checking makes it worse: each glance can trigger anxiety, such as seeing it is 2:45 when you are up at six.

The Protocol

  • Hide the clock, including the time on your phone, so you are not checking it through the night.
  • Give your mind a calm job: picture a familiar walk in detail, from your front door to the steps, a tree and a crossing.
  • If trouble sleeping hits three or more nights a week for three months and your days suffer, get professional help.

See it in real life

Example

Hiding the clock before the road test

Two hours after lights out, Yasmin is wider awake than when she lay down. Her road test is at 8:15 tomorrow, she’s up at six for a practice loop with her mom, and she went to bed early for a full night.

She has tried lying still and relaxing on purpose, and every few minutes she taps her phone to see the time. Each glance comes with the same math, six hours if she falls asleep now, then five and a half, and her heart speeds up a little more each time.

All day, sleep pressure builds, turning down the brain’s wake system and turning up its sleep system. But the wake side, the arousal system, can overrule sleep so you can respond to danger, and the two hold each other down, so when one switches on, the other usually switches off. Forcing sleep doesn’t work well, and turning arousal down can help more. Each glance at the clock can set off a jolt of anxiety, which feeds the side she’s trying to quiet.

So she checks the alarm, puts the phone face down across the room and turns the clock radio to the wall. Then she gives her mind a calm job, her morning walk with her dog, Pretzel, in as much detail as she can, so her attention has somewhere to go besides the test. She leans on the sticky front door, goes down the three porch steps, past the crack Pretzel always sniffs and the magnolia on the corner, to the crossing by the bakery, where she looks left twice. When the three-point turn barges in, she walks back to the last thing she pictured.

She never finds out when she fell asleep, because she never checked. The last thing she remembers is the bakery’s striped awning, and the next is the alarm. At a quarter past eight she pulls out of the testing lot with the examiner beside her.

  1. Where it stallsThe night before her road test, every check of the time wakes her more.
  2. What changes itShe hides the clock and takes a slow mental walk with her dog.
  3. What happens nextShe never learns when she fell asleep, and the alarm wakes her at six.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

Force it or walk it

Find a chair where you can sit still for a couple of minutes, and use the counter below.

  1. Rest your eyes on one spot for one minute and try as hard as you can to make your mind go completely blank. Tap the counter every time a thought shows up.
  2. Write down the number and reset the counter.
  3. For another minute, take a slow walk in your head along a route you know well, starting at your front door, in as much detail as you can.
  4. Tap the counter each time your mind slips off the route, and go back to the last thing you pictured.
  5. Compare the two numbers.

The timer needs JavaScript. A phone stopwatch works just as well.

What you’ll probably notice. Working hard at a blank mind can keep you busy, a small taste of what trying hard to sleep is like. The walk often goes differently, maybe because it gives your attention a calm job and somewhere to go other than worries. Whatever your numbers, practicing the walk on an ordinary day makes it easier to find on a night when you need it.

Step by step

A quieter night before a big day

Use it the night before something that matters, like a test, a tryout or an early flight.

  1. Hide every clock, phone included. Set your alarm, put the phone face down across the room and turn any clock to the wall, so there’s nothing to check.
  2. Stop trying to make sleep happen. Sleep can’t be forced, so aim to let your alertness settle rather than to be asleep by a certain time.
  3. Give your mind a calm job. Take a slow walk in your head along a route you know well, past your front door, the steps, a tree and a crossing, in as much detail as you can.
  4. Walk back when your mind wanders. When tomorrow pops into your head, go back to the last thing you pictured on the route and keep walking.
  5. Know when it’s more than a rough night. Trouble sleeping three or more nights a week for three months, with your days suffering, is a reason to get professional help.

How to tell it’s working. The next morning, note whether you checked the time once it was hidden and roughly how the night went. After a few big nights, compare the ones with the clock hidden and the ones without.

When it gets hard. The urge to check the time is strongest right after you hide the clock. Put the phone far enough away that checking means getting up, and if you do look, turn it back over and pick up the walk where you left off.

Good to know. This is for an ordinary restless night before something big. If trouble sleeping happens three or more nights a week for three months and your days suffer, ask a parent to help you get it checked by a doctor, since a short tip like this isn’t a treatment.
