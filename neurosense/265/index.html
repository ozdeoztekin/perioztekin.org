The Help You Need May Not Feel Like a Burden

Asking for Help & Burden Predictions

Before asking for help, you may count the interruption and miss the other person's own reason to help.

The Science

  • From your side, a request costs someone time or effort; from theirs, it can also be a chance to do something useful for another person.
  • People seeking help can underestimate both how willing others are to help and how positive helping will feel to them.
  • Pressure can still matter, but it is not the whole story: a person can value their time and still value using some of it to help.

The Protocol

  • When you genuinely need help, make the request specific and manageable, so the other person knows what agreeing involves.
  • Ask in a way that leaves a real opening for no, such as "Could you help me carry this to the door, if you have a moment?"
  • If they say no, accept it without repeating the request; if they help, thank them and notice the actual exchange.

See it in real life

Example

One photo with all six of them

Florian has taken more than four hundred photos this week, and he isn’t in any of them. It’s the last evening of the family’s week at the coast, everyone is out on the pier for the sunset, and his grandmother flies home on Tuesday. There isn’t a single picture of all six of them together.

Twice he half raises his phone toward a stranger and lowers it again. Everyone came out for their own sunset, and he can already picture the sigh and the blurry shot handed back.

From his side, the favor costs someone a minute of their evening. From theirs, it can also be a chance to do something useful for another person. People who need help can underestimate both how willing others are and how good helping will feel to them. Pressure is part of the picture, but not all of it, since someone can value their time and still value using a minute of it to help.

So he makes the ask small and clear. He opens the camera, picks a spot by the railing with the lighthouse behind it and asks a man carrying a toddler on his shoulders, “Could you take one photo of the six of us, if you have a moment?” The man has both hands on his son’s ankles and says, “Sorry, not right now.” Florian thanks him and leaves it there. The next person, a woman in a rain jacket, says, “Of course,” and kneels on the wet boards to fit the lighthouse in.

She takes five and says she likes being asked. In the third one everyone’s eyes are open and his grandmother is laughing at something his sister said, and by morning it’s the lock screen on his grandmother’s phone.

  1. Where it stallsAfter four hundred photos, there isn’t one with all six of them.
  2. What changes itHe asks one stranger for one photo and leaves a real opening for no.
  3. What happens nextAfter one no and one yes, the photo ends up on his grandmother’s phone.

Try it yourself · 2 minutes

The other side of a favor

You’ll compare both sides of a small favor. Have somewhere to write two numbers.

  1. Think of the last time someone asked you for a small, clear favor, like taking a photo, giving directions or helping carry something.
  2. From 0 to 10, rate how helping felt to you then, where 0 means it was a burden and 10 means you were glad to do it.
  3. Now think of a small favor you’ve been putting off asking someone for, and rate how you think helping you would feel to them, on the same scale.
  4. Put the two numbers next to each other.

What you’ll probably notice. If your guess about them came out lower than your own experience of helping, that fits a pattern in people who need help. They count the interruption and can miss the helper’s side, where a small favor may also feel useful or even good. Your number for them is still only a guess, and asking with a real opening for no is the only way to get their answer.

Step by step

Ask clearly, and let them choose

For ordinary favors you need, like a photo, a ride from a friend’s parent, a hand carrying something or a quick look at your work.

  1. Start from a real need. Pick something you need help with that one person could do in a few minutes.
  2. Make it specific and manageable. Say exactly what you’re asking and roughly how long it will take, so the other person knows what agreeing involves.
  3. Leave a real opening for no. Ask in a way that leaves the choice to them, like “Could you help me carry this to the door, if you have a moment?”
  4. Take a no as the answer. Thank them and stop there. Asking again until saying no gets uncomfortable defeats the purpose, and their time is theirs.
  5. After a yes, thank them and notice. Notice how the exchange went. A yes doesn’t prove they enjoyed it, and you don’t need to invent resentment they never showed.

How to tell it’s working. Before each ask, write your guess, yes or no, and how they’ll seem. Afterward, write what happened. After a few asks, look at how often your guess was gloomier than the answer.

When it gets hard. One no can feel like proof that you were a burden. It’s an answer about that person’s moment, so thank them, and if you still need help, ask someone else.

Good to know. This is for everyday favors, not a treatment for social anxiety. If worry about asking often keeps you from help you need, bring it up with your family or a doctor, or with a counselor at school.
