Your Palms Are Heat Exits

Glabrous Skin Cooling & Performance

When a workout gets hard, your muscles may not be lazy; they may be hot.

The Science

  • Heat can interfere with the enzymes and signals that let muscle fibers keep contracting.
  • Your palms, soles, and face have special blood vessels built for fast heat exchange.
  • Cooling those surfaces lets the system keep going with less thermal pressure, if the water is cool rather than painfully cold.

The Protocol

  • Between sets, hold a cool bottle or run cool water over your palms.
  • Use cooling as support in hot gyms or fields, not as punishment.
  • Stop if you feel dizzy, chilled, numb, or unsafe.

See it in real life

Example

The fourth run of the closer

“Trombones, you’re late to fourteen,” the director says over the speaker on his tower, and Ines knows it’s mostly her. It’s the second week of band camp. By two o’clock the practice lot is soft at the edges, and the first halftime show is nine days away.

Around the fourth run of the closer, her legs stop answering on time. She’s two counts late to set 14, and her trombone keeps sagging below every other bell in the row. At each water break she sits on her case with her head down, waiting to feel ready. She never does.

Her legs probably aren’t lazy. They’re hot. Heat can interfere with the enzymes and signals that let muscle fibers keep contracting, so each run costs more than the last. Her palms, the soles of her feet and her face have special blood vessels built for fast heat exchange. Cool those, and heat has a quick way out, so her body can keep going with less heat pressing on it. Cool, not icy: very cold water can make those vessels clamp down.

At the next break she watches Dev, the senior who leads the trombones, and does what he does. She takes a bottle from the cooler in the shade, cool but not ice-cold, and holds it in both palms for the whole break. On the long break she runs her palms under the fountain by the band room, drinks, and refills. If her hands started to ache or go numb, she’d stop. They don’t.

On the fifth run she hits set 14 on the count with her horn up. The sixth run, the same. When the speaker crackles after the last chord, it’s for the clarinets.

  1. Where it stallsLate to set 14 every run, horn sagging below the row
  2. What changes itA cool bottle held in both palms, every break, never icy
  3. What happens nextOn the count to set 14, two runs in a row, horn up

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

Palms or backs

You need a sink with cool tap water and a towel. Cool means comfortable to keep your hands in, never icy.

  1. March in place, or walk up and down a flight of stairs at an easy pace, for two minutes, until you feel warm. This isn’t a race.
  2. Hold the backs of your hands and wrists under cool running water for 30 seconds, palms facing down. Notice your face, neck and chest.
  3. Dry off and move for two more easy minutes.
  4. Now turn your hands over and hold only your palms under the same water for 30 seconds, or wrap them around a cool bottle.
  5. Compare the two rounds. Which one cooled more of you, and how fast?

The timer needs JavaScript. A phone stopwatch works just as well.

What you’ll probably notice. Same water, same 30 seconds, and the palm round tends to feel stronger. That fits The Science above: palms have special blood vessels built for fast heat exchange, which makes them one of the quickest exits for heat. On a hot field after hard work there’s far more heat to move, and that’s when the difference matters. If the water ever makes your hands ache, it’s too cold.

Step by step

Cool palms for hot practices

For practices and games in hot gyms, on fields or on blacktop.

  1. Pack a cool bottle, not an icy one. Fill a bottle with cool water and keep it in the shade or in an insulated bag. A second bottle for drinking keeps this one cool longer.
  2. Hold it in both palms between sets. Wrap both hands around it for the whole rest between sets or drills. Running cool water from a fountain over your palms works too.
  3. Keep it cool, never painful. If your hands ache or go numb, the water is too cold, so let it warm up. Stop cooling if you feel dizzy, chilled or unsafe.
  4. Keep drinking at every break. Cool palms help heat leave. They don’t replace water, shade or rest.
  5. Watch the last runs. After your next few hot practices, write down how the last runs or drills went: on time or late, legs answering or heavy.

How to tell it’s working. Look at the last runs or drills of your hot practices, when heat usually wins. If more of them land on time once you start cooling your palms, or your legs keep answering longer, it’s working. Give it a few hot days, since one practice can go either way.

When it gets hard. By the second hour, a bottle sitting in the sun is as warm as the air. Keep it in the shade or an insulated bag, and refill it from a fountain when it warms up.

Good to know. Cool palms help heat leave, but they don’t make extreme heat safe. If you feel dizzy, confused or sick to your stomach in the heat, or get a headache that won’t let up, stop, get into shade, drink water and tell a coach, director or trainer right away.
