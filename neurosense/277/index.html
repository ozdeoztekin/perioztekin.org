Practice the Move You Need to Stop

Stopping Actions & Mental Rehearsal

Starting an action and canceling it are different jobs, so practice can include the stop, not just the go.

The Science

  • A go cue begins a response; a stop cue asks your system to interrupt that response before it is complete.
  • When a stop signal comes early, there is more chance to withhold the press; when it comes later, the movement may finish before stopping succeeds.
  • In a trained button task, physical plus imagined practice can improve stopping; it does not show better self-control everywhere or lasting gains.

The Protocol

  • In an arrow-key task, press the key matching each arrow, cancel if STOP appears afterward, and keep both kinds of trials.
  • After learning the rules by doing, rehearse the same cues without pressing: imagine responding, and withholding when STOP appears.
  • Back on real trials, check ordinary speed and accuracy as well as successful stops.

See it in real life

Example

Three stops out of twenty on the fridge

The leaderboard on the fridge has three columns, arrows hit, average speed and stops held, and Beckett leads in two of them. His sister Irene built the game for the science fair. An arrow appears and you hit the matching arrow key right away, but on some turns a red STOP flashes a moment later, and then you press nothing. Beckett has held 3 stops out of 20, and Irene, who is twelve, has held 14.

He knows the rule. By the time STOP shows up, his finger is usually already on its way down. When he tries waiting a beat on every arrow, his stops go up, but the game marks half his arrows too slow, and Irene says waiting doesn’t count.

Starting an action and canceling it are different jobs. The arrow is a go cue that begins a response, and STOP asks his system to interrupt that response before it’s finished. When the stop comes early there’s more chance to hold back, and when it comes late the press may finish first. Waiting on every arrow avoids canceling, but it gives up the quick responses the arrows need. In a trained button task like this one, real practice combined with rehearsing the same cues in your head can improve stopping on that task.

So each evening he plays one round for real to keep the rules in his hands, then runs a round with his hands in his lap. When an arrow appears he pictures pressing it, and when STOP flashes he pictures his finger staying still. Then he plays another real round and writes down three numbers, stops held, arrows right and average speed, so he can tell stopping from slowing down.

By Friday he’s holding 9 stops out of 20, with his arrows as accurate and as quick as they were on Monday. Irene copies his Monday and Friday numbers onto her poster, next to her own and their dad’s.

  1. Where it stallsHis finger is already pressing when the red STOP flashes.
  2. What changes itHe rehearses the cues with his hands in his lap, then plays.
  3. What happens nextHis stops climb from 3 to 9 while his speed holds.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

Left, right, stop

This one takes a friend to call the cues and a table to tap on. You’ll tap with your left hand for “left” and with your right hand for “right.”

  1. Your friend calls “left” or “right” in a random order, and you tap the table with that hand, quickly. Do ten to get the feel.
  2. Now your friend sometimes adds “stop” after the direction, either right away or after a short beat. When you hear it, don’t tap, and if your hand is already moving, try to pull it back.
  3. Do twenty calls with a stop on some of them but not most, while your friend keeps two tallies, stops you held after a quick “stop” and stops you held after a late one.
  4. Swap roles, then compare your tallies.

What you’ll probably notice. Quick stops are usually easier to hold than late ones, because by the time a late “stop” arrives, the tap is often already on its way. Starting an action and canceling it are separate jobs, and the stop has to catch a response that’s already developing. If you started waiting before every tap, your go taps got slower, which avoids canceling but gives up the quick answer the go calls need.

Step by step

Practice the go and the stop

It takes a friend, three index cards and a keyboard with arrow keys, and it’s practice for this task.

  1. Make the cues. Draw a left arrow, a right arrow and a red STOP on three cards. Your friend holds up an arrow and you press that arrow key, and on some turns, not most, they show STOP a moment later and you don’t press.
  2. Keep both kinds of turns. Don’t wait on every arrow. Waiting avoids canceling, but it gives up the quick presses the arrow turns need.
  3. Learn the rules by doing. Start each session with a round of real presses, so the task is in your hands before you rehearse it.
  4. Rehearse the cues without pressing. With your hands in your lap, go through a round of the same cards, picturing your press on each arrow and your finger staying still when STOP appears.
  5. Go back to real presses and count. Play for real again while your friend notes stops held, right keys on arrow turns and how long ten arrow turns take, so you can tell stopping from slowing down.

How to tell it’s working. Over a few days, compare those three numbers from your first session and your latest. If stops held go up while right keys and arrow time stay about the same, your stopping in this task has improved.

When it gets hard. The easy way to raise the stop count is to wait before every press, which slows everything else down. If your arrow time creeps up, go back to full speed and let some stops fail.

Good to know. This is practice for stopping in this kind of task, over a few days. It doesn’t show better self-control in daily life or in a sport, or a change that lasts, and the numbers say nothing about your character.
