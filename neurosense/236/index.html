A Bad Mood Can Build a Convincing Case

Mood-Congruent Memory & Judgment

A sad mood makes sad memories easier to reach, so a real but skewed selection can look like your whole story.

The Science

  • Mood-congruent memory: the mood you were in when a memory formed becomes one of its cues, so today's mood calls up memories that match it.
  • During depressive rumination this can feed itself: a sad memory can bring its mood back, making another easier to reach.
  • Mood can also skip memory: a rainy-day mood can color a judgment about your whole life if you mistake today's feeling for evidence.

The Protocol

  • When a low mood makes your whole life look bad, name a possible cause of the mood, like today's rain.
  • Then ask how a friend who was there would describe that same period.
  • Treat what comes up as details you may have overlooked, not as proof of a more accurate memory.

See it in real life

Example

Two columns after three days of rain

Juniper’s family moved across the country for her mom’s job last August. The lease is up in July, and on Friday her parents asked how this year has gone for her before they decide whether to stay. She promised an answer at Sunday dinner.

It has rained since Friday. On Sunday she opens her notes app, and the list comes fast. She ate lunch alone most of September, got lost on the first day and didn’t make the volleyball team. Then her old friends post a photo from the lake back home, and she types at the top, “Worst year of my life.”

Every memory on her list is real, but the selection is skewed, which is called mood-congruent memory. The mood you were in when a memory formed becomes one of its cues, so today’s mood calls up memories that match it. What comes to mind easily feels common, and the sad ones start to look like the whole story. A mood can also skip memory and color a judgment about your whole life, if you mistake today’s feeling for evidence about everything.

Before dinner she runs a check. She names a possible cause of the mood, three days of rain and a lake photo she isn’t in. Then she asks how a friend who was there would describe this year, and texts Mirela, who has sat with her at lunch since October. Mirela answers with the snow day when the bus broke down and they walked to the taco place, the robotics bake sale where Juniper sold every brownie and the night they stayed on the phone through a season finale.

Juniper doesn’t take that as the true version either, only as moments her mood had skipped. She starts a second column and fills it with eleven lines. At dinner she reads both columns out loud, starting with September, and her dad wants to know which taco place.

  1. Where it stallsOn the third rainy day, her list says the move ruined her year.
  2. What changes itShe names the rain and asks a friend who was there to describe it.
  3. What happens nextHer list gets a second column, and she reads both at dinner.

Try it yourself · 10 minutes

Your version, their version

Grab a notebook and set a two-minute timer for each list. Pick an ordinary stretch of time, like last month or last summer, rather than something painful.

  1. Write how you feel right now in a few words, and one thing that might be coloring that mood, like the weather, sleep, hunger or a message.
  2. For two minutes, list moments from that stretch of time as fast as they come.
  3. Pick a friend or family member who was there for most of it. For two more minutes, list the same stretch of time the way they would tell it.
  4. Underline everything on the second list that never made the first.
  5. If you like, text that person and ask for their top three moments from that time.

What you’ll probably notice. The first list tends to lean toward whatever mood you were in when you wrote it, because today’s mood helps decide which memories come up first. The second list usually turns up moments you skipped. That doesn’t make their version the true one. A different viewpoint can cue details you overlooked without guaranteeing a more accurate memory, and the useful part is seeing both lists side by side.

Step by step

Check the mood before the verdict

Turn to it when a low mood makes your whole year, team or life look bad.

  1. Catch the big verdict. Watch for sentences that cover everything, like “this whole year was bad” or “nothing ever works out for me.”
  2. Name a possible cause of the mood. Look for something about today that could be coloring it, like rain, a bad night’s sleep, hunger or a photo you saw. Naming it can help keep today’s feeling from passing as evidence about everything.
  3. Ask how a friend who was there would tell it. Picture a friend or family member who was there, or ask them, and write that stretch of time the way they would describe it.
  4. Treat it as overlooked details, not proof. What comes up may be things your mood skipped. It isn’t proof of a more accurate memory, only a wider selection.
  5. Put both versions side by side. Before you decide or say anything big, read your version and theirs together.

How to tell it’s working. Each time you try it, count the details in the friend’s version that weren’t in yours, and notice whether your big sentence changes once both lists are in front of you.

When it gets hard. When you’re low, the friend’s version can sound fake or too cheerful. You don’t have to believe it, only write it down next to yours.

Good to know. A rainy day is a small cause, and this check is meant for ordinary low moods. If a low mood lasts for weeks or you feel hopeless, tell someone, starting with a parent, your doctor or the counselor at school.
