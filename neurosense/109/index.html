Why Your Brain Drops the First Thought

Working Memory & Attention

A thought can vanish while you are still using it. You were not trying to forget. The mental workbench got crowded. Working memory is the few pieces you can hold while you use them.

The Science

  • Working memory holds a small batch of information only long enough to use it.
  • Working memory and attention are tightly linked, because the held items guide the next action.
  • Working memory tends to work best at a middle level of dopamine, a chemical messenger in the brain, and too little or too much can hurt it.

The Protocol

  • Shrink the batch before you try harder.
  • Externalize one step on paper or a checklist.
  • Close extra input until the current step is finished.

See it in real life

Example

Day four on the espresso bar

If Saturday goes well, the Sunday morning bar shift is Eden’s. It’s her fourth day at the café by the train station, and by nine the line is out the door. Tasha, the shift lead, calls drinks from the register three at a time, an iced oat latte, a large mocha with no whipped cream and a cortado.

Eden keeps all three in her head so her hands never stop. Then a man at the pastry case asks whether the oat milk is sweetened, Tasha calls a fourth order, and the milk under the steam wand starts to shriek. She hands over a hot oat latte, and the woman says, “I asked for iced.” Nobody has made the cortado, and by 9:30 she has remade three drinks.

Working memory is like a small workbench. It holds a few pieces only long enough to use them, and it’s tied to attention, because what’s on the bench steers your next move. Too little alertness makes it dim and too much pressure makes it shaky, and Eden’s bench is crowded and shaky at the same time. Every order she forces onto it makes it easier for another one to slide off.

After the third remake, she hands the holding over to the cups. As each order is called, she writes it on a cup in marker and lines the cups up from left to right. She works only on the cup on the left, saying the order under her breath first, like “Iced oat latte.” When the next question comes, she says, “One second,” finishes the pour and then answers.

By ten the line is back inside the door. Her next 23 drinks go out without a remake, and at closing time the schedule taped to the back door has her name on Sunday’s bar shift.

  1. Where it stallsShe holds three orders in her head and hands over an iced latte hot.
  2. What changes itShe writes each order on its cup and works on one cup at a time.
  3. What happens nextTwenty-three drinks go out with no remakes, and Sunday’s bar shift is hers.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

Plus one

This shows how much your workbench holds, and what happens when you shrink the batch. You’ll need a pen, scrap paper and something to cover the rows.

  1. Read row 1 once, then cover it and say each digit plus one, in order and out loud, so that 9 becomes 0. Then uncover it and check.
  2. Do the same with row 2, and notice where it starts to slip.
  3. For row 3, shrink the batch. Read only the first three digits, cover them, say the answers and write them down, and then do the last three the same way.
  4. Compare how many you got right on rows 2 and 3.
Row 15 2 8 3
Row 27 4 1 9 6 2
Row 33 8 6 / 1 5 9

For example, 4 7 9 becomes 5 8 0.

What you’ll probably notice. Four digits usually go fine. With six, a lot of people lose the first ones while they’re working on the later ones, which is how a thought vanishes while you’re still using it. Splitting the row into two batches and writing down the first answers tends to fix it without any extra effort, since your workbench didn’t get bigger and the batch got smaller.

Step by step

Clear the workbench

Use it whenever you feel a thought slip in the middle of a task.

  1. Shrink the batch first. Before you try harder, cut the job down to the two or three pieces that matter right now, and let the rest wait outside your head.
  2. Put one step on paper. Write the current step in a few words where you can see it, like on a sticky note, the side of a cup or the top of the page.
  3. Close the extra input. Turn notifications off, close extra tabs and save side conversations for later, until this step is finished.
  4. Say the next move out loud. Say it quietly in a few words, like “Iced latte, then the mocha” or “First, question four.”
  5. Work in short sets. Spend a few minutes where the only job is one small batch, and finish it before you let the next one in.

How to tell it’s working. Count how often you lose your place in one session the old way, then in one session with a step on paper and the extras closed. If the second count is lower, it’s working.

When it gets hard. Under pressure, holding everything at once feels like being in control. That’s the moment to cut the batch, so pick the next two things, write them down and let the rest wait.
