Why the Second Important Thing Can Disappear

Attentional Blink & Information Design

Catching the first item in a fast sequence can make you miss the next, even though each is easy to see alone.

The Science

  • In an attentional blink, your eyelids need not close and the second item need not be dim or blurry; either is easy to recognize alone.
  • Attention selects information for limited processing, and inputs compete for it, so identifying one thing can affect what else you can report.
  • One way to see it: the first target takes priority while the stream keeps moving, so the next may be replaced before it gets attention.

The Protocol

  • When presenting two essential items, like a room number and a start time, keep both visible or let viewers finish one first.
  • When receiving a fast sequence, pause or replay it if you can, and return to a stable note instead of guessing the missed item.
  • Then check that you actually have both pieces, not only the one you remember catching.

See it in real life

Example

The start time nobody caught

Gaia cut the film club’s trailer to a drum track, and the ending is her favorite part. After a run of scenes from their short film, “ROOM 114” punches onto the screen for half a second, and “FRIDAY 3:30” replaces it on the next beat. It has played on the hallway screens all week, ahead of the club’s first screening.

By Wednesday six people have asked her in the hall what time it starts, and each of them knew the room. Her friend Malin watched it twice on her phone and still couldn’t say. The letters fill the screen, white on black, and either is easy to read alone.

What happened to the time has a name, the attentional blink, though nobody’s eyelids closed. Attention picks out only some of what comes in for fuller processing, and everything else competes for that selection, so catching one thing can affect what else you can report. One way to see it is that the room number takes priority while the trailer keeps moving, and by the time the start time needs attention, the club’s logo has replaced it. People walking between classes, often mid-conversation, had to catch two things back to back.

So Gaia reworks the ending. She keeps the fast cuts, but the last four seconds become one still card with the room and the time side by side, so nobody has to catch them one after the other. She pins the same card at the top of the club’s group chat, where anyone can look again. Then she checks, playing the new version at lunch for five people at the next table and asking what it said.

All five give her both the room and the time. After Thursday nobody stops her in the hall to ask, and on Friday the lights go down at half past three on a room so full that people sit cross-legged by the door.

  1. Where it stallsSix people ask her the start time, and all six knew the room.
  2. What changes itShe ends the trailer on one still card with the room and the time.
  3. What happens nextFive viewers out of five tell her both, and the questions stop.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

R and Z in the stream

This works best with a friend to flip the cards while you watch. Get sixteen index cards or squares of scrap paper and a marker.

  1. On fourteen cards, write single digits from 2 to 9, in any mix. Write an R on one more card and a Z on the last one.
  2. For the first round, your friend leaves out the R, shuffles the Z into the middle of the digits and flips the cards face up onto a pile, as fast as they can while each one stays readable. Say whether you saw the Z.
  3. For the second round, they put the R near the top and the Z near the bottom, with about six digits between them, and flip at the same speed. Name both letters.
  4. For the third round, they put the Z right after the R, with no digit in between. Name both letters again.
  5. Last, spread the whole stack across the table and find both letters.

What you’ll probably notice. The Z alone is usually easy, and so are both letters when they’re far apart. When the Z comes right behind the R, people often name the R and miss the Z, or only guess at it, even though it was every bit as clear as before. Attention was still busy with the R while the stack kept moving, which is the attentional blink. Spread out on the table, both letters stay put, and you can find them as slowly as you like.

Step by step

Keep both pieces in view

Use it when you’re the one sharing two things people need, like a place and a time, and when you’re the one trying to catch them. It’s a sensible way to design around how attention works, not a tested formula.

  1. Show the two essentials together. On a slide, a poster, a video or a message, put the place and the time where they can be seen at once, and leave them up long enough to look twice.
  2. Or let people finish one first. If they have to come one after the other, give people time to read the first before the second replaces it. Nobody has found a number of seconds that works for every screen, so lean toward longer.
  3. Pause or replay when you’re watching. If something important flashes past, hold your thumb on it, scroll back or play it again rather than filling the gap with a guess.
  4. Go back to something that stays put. Find the version that doesn’t move, like a pinned message, a printed schedule or a screenshot, and read the missing piece from there.
  5. Check that you have both. Say both pieces back to yourself. Remembering the room shows you caught the room, and it says nothing about whether you caught the time.

How to tell it’s working. If you’re sharing, ask a few people afterward to tell you both pieces, and keep a tally of who gets both. If you’re watching, notice how often going back to a stable note corrects a guess you would have made.

When it gets hard. Fast cuts and swaps can look great, and you don’t have to give them up. Use them for the parts nobody needs to remember, and let the essentials sit still.
