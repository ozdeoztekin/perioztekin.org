Why Smart Brains Still Get Stuck

Cognitive Flexibility

Stuck is not always a lack of intelligence. It often means an old rule is still dominating. Cognitive flexibility is the executive function that helps your brain hold the goal, inhibit the old frame, and test a new angle.

The Science

  • Diamond (Annual Review of Psychology, 2013): cognitive flexibility is one of three core executive functions, alongside inhibition and working memory. It is the ability to shift between rules, perspectives, or task demands when conditions change.
  • Miyake et al. (Cognitive Psychology, 2000): the foundational 'unity and diversity' framework showed that shifting (rule/set switching) is statistically separable from inhibition and updating. It is related to the other two but separable from them.
  • Dajani & Uddin (Trends in Neurosciences, 2015): cognitive flexibility depends on coordinated activity in the lateral prefrontal cortex, the frontoparietal control network, and the cingulo-opercular network.
  • Important distinction: flexibility is not multitasking. Multitasking splits attention. Flexibility holds one goal and changes the strategy used to reach it.

The Protocol

  • Name the stuck problem.
  • Set a 2-minute timer.
  • Generate THREE intentionally wrong, sideways, or ridiculous approaches.
  • Try role prompts: backward thinker, coach, game designer.
  • Return to the real problem and choose one useful angle from the search.

See it in real life

Example

Milo puts the babysitter to bed

Bedtime at the Okafors’ used to take twenty minutes, with a bath, two books and lights out at eight. Bianca has babysat Milo every Friday since last summer, and that rule always worked.

Then Milo turned six. Now every step is a negotiation, and three Fridays in a row he was still awake when his parents got home at ten. His mom asked, kindly, whether nine might be possible. Tonight at 8:40 he wants a fourth book, and Bianca is on her third “this is the last one,” each one firmer than the one before.

Bianca isn’t short on brains or patience. Her rule stopped working, and she keeps applying it harder. Cognitive flexibility is the brain’s ability to switch strategies without losing sight of the goal. It’s one of the brain’s core control skills, along with holding back an automatic response and keeping information in mind, and it depends on networks in the front and sides of the brain working together. The idea behind this episode’s plan is that coming up with alternatives on purpose, even bad ones, loosens the grip of the first answer.

“Two minutes,” she tells him, and she steps into the hallway with a timer running. In her notes she writes three wrong answers on purpose, one for each of three roles. As a coach, she’d turn bedtime into a drill against the clock. As a game designer, she’d make lights out the boss level. As a backward thinker, she’d have Milo put her to bed. The last one is ridiculous, but there’s something real in it, because what Milo wants is to be in charge.

So tonight Milo runs bedtime. He tucks her into the beanbag chair, whispers a story from the pictures in his dinosaur book and turns off the lamp himself. He’s asleep at 8:58, and she texts his parents the time. The next Friday he asks to do his job again, and he’s out by 8:35.

  1. Where it stallsShe enforces the old two-book rule harder, and Milo is still awake at ten.
  2. What changes itShe spends two minutes in the hallway writing three wrong answers.
  3. What happens nextMilo runs bedtime himself and is asleep before nine.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

The hidden rule

This is a card game for two. You’ll need a deck of playing cards and one other person.

  1. Your partner picks one rule from the box and keeps it to themselves.
  2. Turn over cards one at a time and put each one on a left pile or a right pile, while your partner says only “yes” or “no.”
  3. When you get six right in a row, your partner silently switches to a different rule.
  4. Keep going while your partner counts how many cards it takes you to catch the switch and get six right again.
  5. Swap roles and play it once more.
Rule ARed left, black right
Rule BUnder 7 left, 7 and up right
Rule CPicture cards left, numbers right
Rule DHearts or spades left, others right

Aces count as 1. Picture cards are jacks, queens and kings.

What you’ll probably notice. After your partner changes the rule, the old one usually hangs on for several cards, even while the “no”s pile up. A rule that worked doesn’t switch off just because the answers changed. Catching the switch means keeping the goal of six in a row, holding back the old rule and testing new ones, and keeping the goal while changing the strategy is what cognitive flexibility means.

Step by step

Three wrong angles

It’s for any problem you keep attacking the same way, from an argument to a creative block.

  1. Name the stuck problem. Write one sentence with the goal in it, like “Milo asleep by nine” or “win the second half.” The goal is what you hold on to while the strategy changes.
  2. Set a two-minute timer. Keep it short on purpose, so the search doesn’t turn into another round of worrying.
  3. Write three wrong answers on purpose. Make them wrong, sideways or ridiculous. If one makes you laugh, it’s doing its job.
  4. Borrow three points of view. Ask what a backward thinker would do, starting from the end. Then ask what a coach would do, and then a game designer.
  5. Go back and pick one angle. Return to the real problem, take the most useful piece from your list and try it the next time the problem shows up.

How to tell it’s working. Keep a short log of the problem, your three wrong answers and the angle you tried. After a few weeks, count how many angles led somewhere new, and notice whether you switch strategies sooner when something stops working.

When it gets hard. The two minutes can feel silly when you’re frustrated and want the real answer now. Write the wrong answers anyway, and fast, because the useful idea is often hiding inside the silliest one.
