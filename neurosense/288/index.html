Why Screen Time Can Leave Your Eyes Dry

Blinking & the Tear Film

You tend to blink less while reading or on screens, and blinking helps keep the surface of your eyes coated.

The Science

  • Tears are not just salty water: the lacrimal glands supply the watery part, while glands in the eyelids contribute oils to the tear film.
  • Blinking helps spread the tear film and express its parts, including oils. During reading or screen work, you tend to blink less.
  • So screen discomfort is not necessarily only about the muscles that focus up close; upkeep of the eye's exposed surface can change too.

The Protocol

  • At a natural pause, like the end of a paragraph, let your eyelids close and reopen comfortably through a few complete blinks.
  • Keep the blinks gentle and comfortable, then return to ordinary viewing, allowing normal blinking as you continue.
  • If dryness keeps coming back, do not brush it off or expect this small step to solve it.

See it in real life

Example

A full read for every writer

Zora promised every writer a full read. She’s one of five student editors of the public library’s teen literary magazine, this round brought in thirty-eight stories and poems, and each editor scores all of them on a laptop before Friday’s meeting. On Wednesday night she still has twelve to go.

An hour in, her eyes sting and feel gritty, and every few minutes she stops to rub them and has to find her place again. When one long paragraph ends, she notices she can’t remember the last time she blinked.

Tears are more than salty water. The lacrimal glands make the watery part, and glands in the eyelids add oils that layer over it and help keep it from evaporating. Each blink spreads that film across the eye and squeezes out more of it, oils included. Reading, on paper or a screen, tends to bring blinking down, so the spreading and squeezing happen less, and the eye’s surface isn’t kept up as well. The sting doesn’t have to come only from the muscles that focus up close, and Zora has been holding her eyes open through page after page.

The pauses she needs are already in the reading. Each time a paragraph ends, before her eyes move to the next one, she closes them slowly and opens them again a few times, all the way, without squeezing. Then she reads on and blinks the ordinary way, without forcing a stare. She doesn’t count anything.

By ten her eyes are tired anyway, and she stops for the night with three pieces left. But she has rubbed them only twice in the last hour, and every piece she read has a score and a sentence of notes. She finishes the last three on Thursday and brings all thirty-eight to Friday’s meeting.

  1. Where it stallsHer eyes sting, and she keeps stopping to rub them and losing her place.
  2. What changes itEach time a paragraph ends, she takes a few slow, complete blinks.
  3. What happens nextShe rubs her eyes twice in the last hour, and every piece gets scored.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

Talking blinks, reading blinks

Ask a friend or someone at home to count for you, and have something to read on a screen. If you’re on your own, record your face with a phone’s front camera and count the blinks when you play it back.

  1. Chat with your friend for one minute about anything while they tap the counter below each time you blink. Write down the number.
  2. Reset the counter. Read something on a screen for one minute, the way you usually would, while your friend counts again.
  3. Compare the two numbers and see which minute got more blinks.
  4. Keep reading, and when the next paragraph ends, let your eyelids close and open slowly a few times, all the way, without squeezing. Then read on and notice how the surface of your eyes feels.

The timer needs JavaScript. A phone stopwatch works just as well.

What you’ll probably notice. The reading minute usually gets fewer blinks than the talking minute, since people tend to blink less while they read or look at a screen. Each blink spreads the tear film over the eye and squeezes out more of it, including oils that slow evaporation, so a long stare skips some of that upkeep. A few slow, complete blinks at a natural pause put that step back, with no number to reach.

Step by step

Blink where the work pauses

For long stretches of reading, drawing or gaming on a screen. It’s a small step based on how the tear film works, not a treatment.

  1. Pick the pauses you already have. Use breaks built into the task, like the end of a paragraph, the end of a page or a finished level.
  2. Blink fully a few times. At each pause, let your eyelids close and reopen comfortably through a few complete blinks, all the way down and up, without squeezing.
  3. Go back to ordinary viewing. Return to the task and let your blinking happen on its own. A long, wide-eyed stare isn’t automatically better focus.
  4. Notice how your eyes feel. Compare how dry or gritty your eyes feel after a session with the pauses and after a similar one without them.
  5. Take dryness that keeps coming back seriously. Fewer blinks are only one possible cause, and the amount and makeup of tears vary from person to person, so this small step can’t be expected to fix it.

How to tell it’s working. After an hour of screen time with blinks at the pauses, rate how dry your eyes feel from 0 to 10 and count how many times you rubbed them. Do the same after a similar hour without the pauses, and compare.

When it gets hard. Once you’re absorbed, you’ll sail right past the pauses. Tie the blinks to something you already do at each break, like saving your work or scrolling to a new page.

Good to know. If your eyes keep feeling dry, sore or gritty, or your vision blurs or changes, let a parent know and see an eye doctor. Blinking more isn’t a treatment, and it isn’t a reason to put off getting checked.
