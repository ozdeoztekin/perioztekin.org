Creative Incubation: Step Away to Solve It

Creative Incubation

Stuck on a problem and the answer will not come? The smartest move can be to quit for ten minutes. Stepping away can help your brain make the connection that grinding could not.

The Science

  • Sio and Ormerod (2009), Psychological Bulletin: a meta analysis, which pooled results from many experiments, found that incubation, a break from a problem, on average improves later solving, especially after a longer first effort.
  • Baird et al. (2012), Psychological Science: in one study, an easy task during a break, the kind that lets the mind wander, helped people think of more unusual uses for objects they had already worked on than rest or a hard task did, though a larger repeat study, Murray et al. (2024), found no advantage for an easy task over a hard one.
  • Beaty et al. (2016), Trends in Cognitive Sciences: creative thinking draws on the default mode network for idea generation working with executive control to refine them.
  • Net effect: low demand breaks may loosen fixation and let spontaneous thought link ideas, while scrolling fills the break with new input and probably does not help in the same way.

The Protocol

  • Work hard on the problem first, because breaks tend to help more after real preparation.
  • When stuck, set a 10 minute timer and walk away.
  • Pick a boring, low demand task: a walk, a shower, the dishes. No phone, no new content.
  • Let your mind wander without forcing the answer, then come back and capture new connections.

See it in real life

Example

Wings that won’t fit through the door

Ruby’s wings are four feet across, and the door to the convention stage isn’t. Neither is the bus door. The cosplay contest is on Saturday at two, and for two evenings she has been trying to make the wings fold.

Cardboard with a hinge in the middle sagged, and foam board snapped at the crease. Tonight she’s trying coat-hanger wire, and the left wing has flopped off the shoulder straps for the third time. Every version swaps the material but keeps the same idea, two flat, stiff panels joined by a hinge.

The trap she’s in has a name, fixation, which is when the first obvious answer blocks the others and forcing it keeps attention on the same wrong path. Taking a break tends to help most after real effort, especially a break doing something easy, like a walk, a shower or the dishes, that leaves the mind free to wander. Creative thinking seems to draw on the brain’s wandering mode for new ideas and its control networks for sorting them out. Scrolling probably doesn’t work as well, because it fills the time with new things to take in.

At 9:15 she sets the oven timer for ten minutes, leaves her phone on her worktable and washes the dinner dishes without any music. She doesn’t try to think about wings. Somewhere around the frying pan she finds herself thinking about her grandmother on the porch in July, snapping a paper fan open and shut.

When the timer beeps, she dries her hands and sketches before the idea slips away, with pleats instead of panels and ribs spreading out from one pin at the shoulder. By midnight a paper test wing folds to the width of her hand and opens with one pull of a string. On Saturday she walks through the stage door with the wings folded flat and opens them under the lights.

  1. Where it stallsHer third set of stiff wings still won’t fold.
  2. What changes itShe washes the dinner dishes for ten minutes with no phone and no music.
  3. What happens nextShe sketches pleated wings that fold flat and open with one pull.

Try it yourself · 10 minutes

Uses for one sock

This is a small version of the ten-minute break. You’ll need paper, a pen, one ordinary sock or a paper clip, and the timer below.

  1. Start the timer and list every use you can think of for the sock for two minutes. Silly ideas count.
  2. When it rings, count your list, and notice how many are versions of the obvious one, something that goes on a foot.
  3. Spend about five minutes on something boring with your hands, like folding laundry, washing a few dishes or walking around the house. Skip the phone and music with lyrics, and don’t try to think about socks.
  4. Come back, start the timer again and keep adding to the same list for two more minutes, counting only the new ones.

The timer needs JavaScript. A phone stopwatch works just as well.

What you’ll probably notice. Most first lists stall after the obvious uses, and a lot of people come back from the break with a few new ones that seem to come out of nowhere. The stall is functional fixedness. The restart may happen because an easy break loosens the grip of the first answer and gives your mind room to wander toward other connections. Breaks help most after real effort, so the hard round has to come first.

Step by step

Step away on purpose

It’s for a problem you’ve already worked hard on and still can’t crack.

  1. Load the problem first. Work on it for real, then write the problem in one sentence, so your brain has something specific to hold.
  2. If you hit a wall, set a timer and leave. Give it ten minutes, or up to twenty, and walk away from the desk, the instrument or the workbench.
  3. Pick something boring. Take a walk or a shower, wash dishes or fold laundry. Skip the phone, the feed and music with lyrics, so there’s nothing new to take in.
  4. Let your mind wander. Don’t chase the answer, and if your thoughts drift somewhere unrelated, let them drift.
  5. Capture it fast, then test it. Keep paper nearby. If an idea shows up, write or sketch it in a few words right away, then go back and test it with focused work.

How to tell it’s working. Keep a short log of stuck moments, with what kind of break you took, phone or boring, and whether a new idea showed up within the next half hour. After a few tries, compare the two kinds.

When it gets hard. The urge to keep grinding is strongest right when you should leave, and the next urge is to grab your phone on the way out. Leave the phone with the problem.
