Why Bad Habits Snap Back

LTD & Unlearning

Knowing the better move and still doing the old one is not proof you failed. The old pathway is still easy for your brain to run, and weakening it is a separate process from building the new one.

The Science

  • Malenka & Bear (Neuron, 2004): plasticity is bidirectional. Long-term potentiation (LTP) strengthens synapses; long-term depression (LTD) weakens them. Both are required for flexible learning. The system has an add button and a turn-down button.
  • Collingridge, Peineau, Howland & Wang (Nature Reviews Neuroscience, 2010): in many circuits, LTD involves removal of AMPA-type glutamate receptors from the postsynaptic membrane, so the same input arrives but produces a smaller response. The connection becomes quieter, not erased.
  • Massey & Bashir (Trends in Neurosciences, 2007): LTD-like processes have been linked, mostly in animal studies, to motor learning in the cerebellum, to behavioral flexibility and to refining connections, so changing an old pattern seems to involve weakening as well as adding.
  • In habit research, replacement tends to work best when the trigger is noticed, the old response is interrupted, and the new response is practiced in the same situation, many times.

The Protocol

  • Pick ONE ordinary habit, skill error, or reaction pattern.
  • Name the EXACT trigger (sitting down, opening an app, missing a shot, hearing a certain tone in someone's voice).
  • When the trigger hits, pause for 1 clean second.
  • Run the replacement for 5 seconds while the trigger is still fresh.
  • Repeat in the same context so the old path weakens and the new path strengthens.

See it in real life

Example

The kick that kept coming back

Over the winter, Wes’s taekwondo instructor rebuilt his side kick for breaking: knee high, hips turned over, heel driving through the board instead of at it. In class it works. When it counts, it disappears.

The moment a first attempt bounces off, the old kick comes back without asking: hips square, a flat foot, a careful push that thuds against the board and stops. At last month’s tryout for the demonstration team, it came back after every miss, and he didn’t make the team. He knows exactly what he’s supposed to do. His leg does the other thing.

Learning has an add button and a turn-down system. Long-term depression is the turn-down side. It weakens connections when a pattern should fade, in many circuits by leaving fewer signal-catching receptors, so the old connection gets quieter without being erased. That’s why it snaps back under pressure. The old path is still easy to run, and knowing better doesn’t weaken it. The trigger has to meet a different response while the old one is interrupted.

He names the exact trigger: the dull thud of a board that doesn’t break. At Thursday’s class he holds back on purpose against a plastic practice board, to make that thud happen. After each one he takes one clean second, feet set, one breath out. Then five seconds: one slow shadow kick of the new motion, knee high, hips over, before the real kick. Forty times.

At Saturday’s second tryout there are three boards. The old push sneaks back once, on the second board, and he feels it happen; that one takes three attempts. At the third, his first kick thuds off, and this time he takes the clean second, the breath and the slow shadow kick. The board splits in two.

  1. Where it stallsA board that won’t break, then the old flat-footed push
  2. What changes itOne clean second, a breath, then a slow shadow kick
  3. What happens nextOne slip on the second board, then the routine on the third

Try it yourself · 2 minutes

The arm-cross test

All you need is your arms, plus someone to say “go” at a random moment if anyone is around.

  1. Cross your arms without thinking. Notice which forearm ends up on top.
  2. Uncross them and cross them the other way. Notice how clumsy it feels.
  3. Cross them the new way ten times, slowly, paying attention each time.
  4. Drop your arms. Have someone say “go” at a random moment, or count to a random number in your head, and on “go” cross your arms as fast as you can. Which way did they go?
  5. Try once more, but pause for one second before you cross, and choose the new way.

What you’ll probably notice. Even after ten slow tries, a lot of people’s arms go the old way when they rush. Ten tries are a start, and the old pattern hasn’t gone anywhere, so speed brings it back. The one-second pause in step 5 gives the new pattern room to run. The plan below uses the same pause, repeated in the same situation many times, so the old path can keep getting quieter.

Step by step

Interrupt, then replace

Pick one pattern you’d like to change: a habit, a skill error or a reaction.

  1. Pick one pattern. One at a time: the rushed kick, the phone grab when you sit down, the automatic comeback. An ordinary one is fine.
  2. Name the exact trigger. Something you could point to: sitting down at the desk, opening an app, missing a shot, a certain tone in someone’s voice.
  3. Pause for one clean second. When the trigger hits, stop for one second. A breath, feet set, hands flat on the desk.
  4. Run the replacement for five seconds. Right away, while the trigger is fresh: the slow correct kick, hands on the desk instead of on the phone, one calm sentence instead of the comeback.
  5. Repeat it in the same place. Same situation, many times. If you can, make the trigger happen on purpose in practice, like holding back a kick so the board thuds, so you get more repetitions.

How to tell it’s working. Count how often the old pattern shows up in a week: flat kicks per class, phone grabs per hour, comebacks per day. Expect it to fall slowly and to spike under pressure. A count that trends down over a few weeks means it’s working.

When it gets hard. The old pattern will snap back on a stressful day, and it can feel like starting over, but it isn’t: the old path got quieter, and it can still be loud for a moment. Run the pause and the replacement on the very next trigger.
