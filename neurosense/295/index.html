Practice the Lowering When the Whole Pull-Up Breaks Down

Eccentric Contractions & Pull-Up Practice

A muscle can work while it lengthens, so you can practice controlling a pull-up's lowering on its own.

The Science

  • A muscle can stay active while a load makes it lengthen. That is an eccentric contraction, and neural signals still activate the muscle.
  • Because lowering is active work, you can split a complicated movement and practice controlling the lowering without first completing the lift.
  • If you finish a pull-up by leaning on your arms while struggling to coordinate your back muscles, forcing it again may just repeat that workaround.

The Protocol

  • Use this only if you can already support yourself safely at the top: step up from a stable box to get there.
  • Lower your body under control, keeping it manageable rather than dropping and trying to catch yourself.
  • Use the box to return to the top; as the lowering gets manageable, build the full skill with an appropriate training plan.

See it in real life

Example

Two pull-ups, mostly arms

Vida, nineteen, trains in the adult group at her climbing gym, where the coach, Stellan, films everyone’s pull-ups whenever a new training block begins. She can do two, and they look the way they did last fall, with her shoulders up by her ears and her chin craned over the bar while her arms do nearly all of it.

Since then she has ended every session at the bar fighting for a third, kicking and jerking until her chin gets near it. The new clips look like the old ones, only messier.

A muscle doesn’t have to shorten to be working. On the way down from the bar, the muscles that pulled her up stay switched on by nerve signals while her weight makes them lengthen, which is called an eccentric contraction. So the lowering is real work she can practice by itself, without first solving the whole climb. Each forced pull-up gives her another round of the same workaround, with her arms doing the job her back should share, while practicing one phase changes the problem she’s solving.

Stellan’s first question is whether she can hold herself safely at the top. He slides a sturdy box under the bar by the hangboards, and from it she takes the bar with her chin above it, lifts her feet and holds, shoulders down. She can. Then she lowers herself under control until her arms are straight and her toes find the floor, with no number of seconds to hit, and steps back onto the box to start again. Each session starts with a handful, and she stops when one gets away from her.

In the first week the top half of each lowering is smooth and the bottom third drops fast. A month later her clip shows one even line from the top to straight arms, shoulders down the whole way, and Stellan writes her next step into her notebook.

  2. What changes itShe steps up from a box and practices only the lowering.
  3. What happens nextA month later she lowers in one smooth line, shoulders down.

Try it yourself · 2 minutes

Lean into the wall

Find a clear stretch of wall and wear shoes or socks that won’t slip. There’s no bar, no hanging and nothing heavy here.

  1. Stand about an arm’s length from the wall and put one palm flat on it at shoulder height. Rest the fingers of your other hand on the back of that upper arm.
  2. Keeping your feet where they are, lean slowly toward the wall by bending that elbow, and feel whether the muscle under your fingers stays firm as it lengthens.
  3. Push yourself back until your arm is straight, and feel the same muscle work as it shortens.
  4. Let the arm hang at your side and feel how soft that spot is when it isn’t working.

What you’ll probably notice. The muscle stays firm while you lean in, even though it’s getting longer, because nerve signals keep it switched on while it resists your weight. That’s an eccentric contraction, the same kind of work the back and arms do on the way down from a pull-up. Since lowering is real work, it can be practiced as its own part of a movement.

Step by step

Practice the way down

This is a coaching drill for adults who train with a coach and can already hold themselves safely at the top of a pull-up.

  1. Check that you can hold the top. If you’re an adult training with a coach, first make sure you can support yourself safely with your chin over the bar. If you can’t yet, this version isn’t for you, and your coach can suggest another way in.
  2. Step up from a stable box. Set a sturdy box under the bar so you can reach the top position without pulling. The box only replaces the climb, so you still hold yourself at the top.
  3. Lower yourself under control. Keep your shoulders down and move smoothly to straight arms. There’s no magic number of seconds, and dropping and catching yourself defeats the purpose.
  4. Use the box to start again. Step back up to the top rather than pulling, so every attempt is about the lowering. Stop when a lowering stops being controlled, and let your coach decide how many and how often.
  5. Build the rest with your coach. As the lowering gets manageable, work toward the full pull-up through your coach’s plan. The drill by itself doesn’t guarantee one.

How to tell it’s working. Every week or two, film one lowering from the side and watch one thing, whether your shoulders stay down and the speed stays even all the way to straight arms. Compare clips taken from the same angle.

When it gets hard. The usual pattern is a controlled top half and then a drop through the bottom. When that happens, the set is over, and the bottom is the part to own next time.

Good to know. This isn’t for anyone under 18, anyone new to pull-ups or bar training, who should learn first with a qualified coach, or anyone who can’t hold the top position safely. If a shoulder, elbow or wrist is injured, or you have a medical condition, check with a doctor first.
