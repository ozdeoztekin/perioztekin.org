The Move That Separates A-Students From Everyone Else

Growth Mindset

After a low score, the brain often turns someone else's success into a trait story. NeuroSense turns it into a process map.

The Science

  • Dweck (Mindset, 2006) describes the difference between a fixed and a growth mindset as how people respond to failure: looking for a better strategy, or pulling back to protect their self-image.
  • Mangels et al. (Social Cognitive and Affective Neuroscience, 2006): in an EEG study of college students, those who believed intelligence can grow paid more sustained attention to the correct answers after their mistakes and fixed more of those errors on a surprise retest; those with a fixed view reacted more strongly to the 'wrong' signal itself.
  • Yeager et al. (Psychological Science, 2022): in a national study of about 9,000 ninth graders, a short growth-mindset program raised math grades for students whose teachers held growth mindsets, but not for students whose teachers held fixed mindsets; the setting matters.

The Protocol

  • After the next quiz, find one person who improved or scored higher.
  • Ask: What did you do first? What did you do when you got stuck? What did you change from last time?
  • Test one specific answer on one assignment this week.
  • Track whether the change moved your score; that is the evidence the mindset feeds on.

See it in real life

Example

The test folded in quarters

Sky’s physics test has been folded in quarters at the bottom of her backpack since Tuesday. It says 58 on the front, and she hasn’t looked past that. It’s a fifth of her semester grade.

At lunch she hears that Julian, who got a 71 on the last quiz, got a 94. “He’s just a physics person,” she tells Bex, and she means it as the end of the conversation.

That sentence is doing a job. After a setback, the brain can go looking for a better strategy, or it can shut the search down to protect how she feels about herself. Calling Julian a physics person shuts it down: if it’s a trait, there’s nothing to ask him. Brain recordings suggest that people who expect to improve pay closer attention to the right answer after a mistake, and fix more of their errors later. And the belief does the most when there’s support around you and a better strategy to try.

On Thursday she catches Julian at the door after class, too fast to back out, and asks what he did first. “Drew the picture. Every problem, an arrow for every force, before any numbers.” Then what he did when he got stuck. “Wrote down what it gave me and what it wanted.” Then what he changed since the quiz. “Stopped rereading my notes. Redid the homework on blank paper.” That night she unfolds the test. Seven of her misses are force problems, and she didn’t draw a thing for any of them.

She tests one answer. This week’s problem set has twelve problems, and every one starts with a sketch. She gets ten right. On Friday’s quiz she gets four of five, and she tapes Julian’s three answers inside her binder.

  1. Where it stallsA 58 folded in her backpack, and a trait story about Julian
  2. What changes itThree questions for Julian, then a picture first on every problem
  3. What happens nextTen of twelve on the problem set, four of five on Friday

Try it yourself · 10 minutes

Ask for the recipe

Find someone who’s good at something you’d like to be better at: a sibling’s free throws, a friend’s drawings, a parent’s cooking. You need paper and about ten minutes of their time.

  1. Before you talk to them, write one sentence on why they’re good at it. Use the first explanation that comes to mind.
  2. Ask them three things: what they did first, what they did when they got stuck, and what they changed from last time.
  3. Write down every answer that’s something you could actually do, like “drew the picture first” or “practiced the hard part slowly.”
  4. Count the doable steps in your first sentence, then the doable steps in their answers.

What you’ll probably notice. First explanations are often a trait, like “she’s just artistic,” and a trait gives you nothing to try. That’s the search shutting down before it starts. The three questions usually turn up two or three concrete moves, which is what a growth mindset needs to work with: a strategy you can test this week.

Step by step

After the next low score

It works for anything with a score: a quiz, a race time, a tryout ranking.

  1. Label every miss before you file it. Next to each wrong answer, write one word: didn’t know, careless, ran out of time or set it up wrong. The pattern tells you what to ask about.
  2. Find one person who improved. Someone who scored higher, or who went up since last time. A classmate, a teammate or an older sibling who took the same class all count.
  3. Ask the three questions. What did you do first, what did you do when you got stuck, and what did you change from last time? Write the answers down while they talk.
  4. Test one answer this week. Pick the answer that fits your pattern of misses and use it on one assignment from start to finish. Just one, so you can tell whether it worked.
  5. Compare the new score with the old. That comparison is your evidence. It tells you whether to keep the strategy or test the next answer.

How to tell it’s working. Count the same kind of miss before and after: how many you had on the old test, and how many on the new assignment. If that kind of miss shows up less, keep the strategy; if not, test a different answer next week.

When it gets hard. Some people just say they studied. Ask for one detail instead, like what they did the night before or which problems they did first. A teacher can answer the three questions too.
