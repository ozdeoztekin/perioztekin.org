The Move That Separates A-Students From Everyone Else

Growth Mindset

After a low score, the brain often turns someone else's success into a trait story. NeuroSense turns it into a process map.

The Science

  • Dweck (Mindset, 2006) describes the difference between a fixed and a growth mindset as how people respond to failure: looking for a better strategy, or pulling back to protect their self-image.
  • Mangels et al. (Social Cognitive and Affective Neuroscience, 2006): in an EEG study of college students, those who believed intelligence can grow paid more sustained attention to the correct answers after their mistakes and fixed more of those errors on a surprise retest; those with a fixed view reacted more strongly to the 'wrong' signal itself.
  • Yeager et al. (Psychological Science, 2022): in a national study of about 9,000 ninth graders, a short growth-mindset program raised math grades for students whose teachers held growth mindsets, but not for students whose teachers held fixed mindsets; the setting matters.

The Protocol

  • After the next quiz, find one person who improved or scored higher.
  • Ask: What did you do first? What did you do when you got stuck? What did you change from last time?
  • Test one specific answer on one assignment this week.
  • Track whether the change moved your score; that is the evidence the mindset feeds on.

See it in real life

Example

The test folded in quarters

Sky’s physics test has been folded in quarters at the bottom of her backpack since Tuesday. The front says 58, and she hasn’t looked past that. It counts for a fifth of her grade.

At lunch she hears that Julian, who got a 71 on the last quiz, got a 94 this time. “He’s just a physics person,” she tells Bex, and she means it to end the conversation.

That sentence is doing a job for her. After a setback, people can go looking for a better strategy, or they can stop looking to protect how they feel about themselves. Calling Julian a physics person stops the search, because if it’s a trait, there’s nothing to ask him. In one study that recorded students’ brain activity, the ones who believed ability can grow paid closer attention to the right answers after their mistakes, and they fixed more of those errors on a surprise retest. A large study of ninth graders found that this kind of belief helps most when the people around you support it and there’s a better strategy to try.

On Thursday she catches Julian at the door after class, too quickly to back out, and asks what he did first. He says he drew the picture for every problem, with an arrow for every force, before any numbers. When she asks what he did when he got stuck, he says he wrote down what the problem gave him and what it wanted. After the quiz, he stopped rereading his notes and redid the homework on blank paper. That night Sky unfolds the test. Seven of her misses are force problems, and she didn’t draw anything for any of them.

So she tests one of his answers. This week’s problem set has twelve problems, and she starts every one with a sketch. She gets ten right, and on Friday’s quiz she gets four out of five. She tapes Julian’s three answers inside her binder.

  1. Where it stallsShe hides a 58 in her backpack and decides Julian is a physics person.
  2. What changes itShe asks Julian three questions and starts every problem with a sketch.
  3. What happens nextShe gets ten of twelve on the problem set and four of five on Friday.

Try it yourself · 10 minutes

Ask for the recipe

Find someone who’s good at something you’d like to get better at, like a sibling’s free throws, a friend’s drawings or a parent’s cooking. You’ll need paper and about ten minutes of their time.

  1. Before you talk to them, write one sentence about why they’re good at it, using the first explanation that comes to mind.
  2. Ask them what they did first, what they did when they got stuck and what they changed from last time.
  3. Write down every answer that’s something you could do yourself, like “drew the picture first” or “practiced the hard part slowly.”
  4. Count the doable steps in your sentence, then the doable steps in their answers.

What you’ll probably notice. First explanations are often a trait, like “she’s just artistic,” and a trait gives you nothing to try. That’s the search shutting down before it starts. The three questions usually turn up two or three moves you could make, and that’s what a growth mindset needs to work with, a strategy you can test this week.

Step by step

After the next low score

It works for anything with a score, like a quiz, a race time or a tryout ranking.

  1. Label every miss before you file it. Next to each wrong answer, write a word or two, such as didn’t know, careless, ran out of time or set it up wrong. The pattern tells you what to ask about.
  2. Find one person who improved. Look for someone who scored higher or who went up since last time. A classmate, a teammate or an older sibling who took the same class all count.
  3. Ask the three questions. Ask what they did first, what they did when they got stuck and what they changed from last time, and write the answers down while they talk.
  4. Test one answer this week. Pick the answer that fits your pattern of misses and use it on one assignment from start to finish. Stick to one, so you can tell whether it worked.
  5. Compare the new score with the old. That comparison is your evidence, and it tells you whether to keep the strategy or test the next answer.

How to tell it’s working. Count the same kind of miss before and after, on the old test and on the new assignment. If that kind of miss shows up less, keep the strategy, and if it doesn’t, test a different answer next week.

When it gets hard. Some people will only say they studied. Ask for one detail, like what they did the night before or which problems they did first. A teacher can answer the three questions too.
