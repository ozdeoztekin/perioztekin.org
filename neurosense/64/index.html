How Your Brain Turns the Volume Down

GABA: The Mute Button

When noise, pressure, or a group chat makes your mind feel loud, trying harder can backfire. When the system is already loud, the useful move is often a brake, not another push.

The Science

  • Farrant and Nusser (2005), Nature Reviews Neuroscience: GABA is the brain's main inhibitory transmitter, making some neurons less likely to fire.
  • Petroff (2002), The Neuroscientist: activity in the cortex depends on a balance between excitation and inhibition, so inhibition shapes brain activity rather than shutting it down.
  • Zaccaro et al. (2018), Frontiers in Human Neuroscience: slow breathing shifts arousal through body-brain pathways, a route you can actually use.
  • When the system is already loud, a brake helps more than another push.

The Protocol

  • Aim for arousal downshifting, not forcing silence.
  • Breathe with a longer exhale: about four seconds in, six seconds out, for one minute.
  • Drop the shoulders, unclench the jaw, then choose one small next action.
  • Notice the signal, name the mechanism, and change one input before autopilot.

See it in real life

Example

Four falls on problem 5

Problem 5 has one bad move, a reach from a pinch to a sloper with Ayumi’s feet barely on the wall. She has fallen off it four times in six minutes, and the round has nine minutes left.

The gym is loud. Speakers thump in the corner, the crowd groans every time someone comes off, and the clock over the wall counts down in red. On each attempt she pulls harder and moves faster, and each one ends sooner. Her forearms are pumped, her jaw is clamped and her shoulders are up by her ears.

Her system is already firing hard, and another push only adds more. The brain’s main brake is GABA, a chemical messenger that makes some neurons less likely to fire. That doesn’t mean shutting down. Holding some signals back is part of how the brain shapes movement and attention into something clean, like the precise reach a sloper needs. Slow breathing, with the breath out longer than the breath in, is one route she can use to bring her whole body down a notch.

So she sits on the mat with her back to the crowd and spends one quiet minute, timed on the clock, breathing in through her nose for four seconds and out for six. She drops her shoulders, unclenches her jaw and shakes out her hands. Then she picks one small next step, which is to chalk up, climb only as far as the pinch and look at the sloper.

From the pinch she sees it. Her feet can go higher than she has been putting them all round. With three minutes left she sets them there and reaches slowly instead of lunging, and the sloper holds. She tops out on her fifth attempt, and the crowd is exactly as loud as before.

  1. Where it stallsShe falls four times on one move while the clock and the crowd get louder.
  2. What changes itShe sits with her back to the crowd and breathes four in, six out.
  3. What happens nextShe climbs to the pinch, looks, and tops out on the fifth try.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

Squeeze, then brake

This is a quick way to feel the difference between a push and a brake. You’ll need a pen, paper and the pacer below.

  1. Make a firm fist with the hand you don’t write with and clench your jaw, tight but not painful. Keep both that way while you write your full name as small and neat as you can, three times.
  2. Let go and start the pacer, breathing in through your nose for four and out for six, for six rounds.
  3. Drop your shoulders, let your jaw hang loose and rest your other hand open on the table.
  4. Write your name small three more times.
  5. Compare the two sets, and how your writing hand felt each time.

The timer needs JavaScript. A phone stopwatch works just as well.

What you’ll probably notice. Side by side, the first set often looks shakier, and your writing hand may have felt more cramped, as if the squeeze leaked into the pen. Clean movement depends on some signals getting quieter while others work, which is part of what the brain’s braking system does, so more effort everywhere rarely helps. The second set also had practice on its side, so for a fair test, try the order the other way around tomorrow.

Step by step

Brake first, then one small thing

It’s for when things get loud and you start rushing.

  1. Notice when it gets loud. When noise, pressure or a busy chat has you speeding up and making more mistakes, that’s the signal to brake instead of pushing harder.
  2. Take your foot off the gas. Stop the thing you’re forcing. Take your hands off it, and mute the chat or turn the phone face down for a set time.
  3. Breathe four in, six out. Breathe in through your nose for about four seconds and out for six, for one quiet minute.
  4. Drop your shoulders and unclench your jaw. Let your shoulders fall and your teeth come apart, and check your hands too.
  5. Choose one small next action. Pick the smallest piece that still counts, like one move, one paragraph, one email or one bar of music. Do that, then pick the next.

How to tell it’s working. Count your do-overs, like falls, deleted lines or missed notes, in the ten minutes before a brake and the ten minutes after. If the after number is lower more often than not, the brake is working for you.

When it gets hard. Stopping feels like losing time when you’re already behind, so you’ll want to skip it exactly when you need it. Set the rule ahead of time, so three mistakes in a row means one minute of brake.

Good to know. If your mind feels loud most days, or the alarm keeps coming back or ever feels unsafe, tell a parent or a coach you trust, and talk with a doctor.
