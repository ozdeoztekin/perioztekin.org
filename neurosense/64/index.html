How Your Brain Turns the Volume Down

GABA: The Mute Button

When noise, pressure, or a group chat makes your mind feel loud, trying harder can backfire. When the system is already loud, the useful move is often a brake, not another push.

The Science

  • Farrant and Nusser (2005), Nature Reviews Neuroscience: GABA is the brain's main inhibitory transmitter, making some neurons less likely to fire.
  • Petroff (2002), The Neuroscientist: inhibition is not shutdown; it shapes sleep, movement, attention, and stress by balancing excitation.
  • Zaccaro et al. (2018), Frontiers in Human Neuroscience: slow breathing shifts arousal through body-brain pathways, a route you can actually use.
  • When the system is already loud, a brake helps more than another push.

The Protocol

  • Aim for arousal downshifting, not forcing silence.
  • Breathe with a longer exhale: about four seconds in, six seconds out, for one minute.
  • Drop the shoulders, unclench the jaw, then choose one small next action.
  • Notice the signal, name the mechanism, and change one input before autopilot.

See it in real life

Example

Four falls on problem 5

Problem 5 has one bad move, a reach from a pinch to a sloper with Ayumi’s feet barely on. She has fallen off it four times in six minutes, and the round has nine minutes left.

The gym is loud. Speakers thump from the corner, the crowd groans every time someone comes off, and the clock over the wall counts down in red. Each attempt she pulls harder and moves faster, and each one ends sooner. Her forearms are pumped, her jaw is clamped, and her shoulders are up by her ears.

Her system is already firing hard, and another push adds more. The brain’s main brake is GABA, a messenger that makes some neurons less likely to fire. That isn’t weakness or shutting down: inhibition is how the brain shapes movement and attention into a clean signal, the kind of precise reach a sloper needs. Feeling this wired is a whole-body state with many causes, so the useful move is a downshift, and slow breathing is a route in.

She sits on the mat with her back to the crowd. One quiet minute, counted on the clock: four seconds in through her nose, six seconds out. She drops her shoulders, unclenches her jaw and shakes out her hands. Then she picks one small next action: chalk up and climb only to the pinch, then stop and look at the sloper.

From the pinch she sees it. Her feet can go higher than she’s been putting them all round. With three minutes left she sets them there, reaches slowly instead of lunging, and the sloper holds. She tops out on her fifth attempt, and the crowd is exactly as loud as before.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

Squeeze, then brake

A quick feel for a push versus a brake. You need a pen, paper and the pacer here.

  1. Make a firm fist with the hand you don’t write with and clench your jaw, tight but not painful. Keep both that way while you write your full name as small and neat as you can, three times.
  2. Let go. Start the pacer: in through your nose for four, out for six, six rounds.
  3. Drop your shoulders, let your jaw hang loose and rest the other hand open on the table.
  4. Write your name small three more times.
  5. Compare the two sets, and how your writing hand felt each time.

The timer needs JavaScript. A phone stopwatch works just as well.

What you’ll probably notice. Side by side, the first set often looks shakier, and the writing hand may have felt more cramped, as if the squeeze leaked into the pen. Clean movement depends on some signals getting quieter while others work, which is part of what inhibition does, so more effort everywhere rarely helps. The second set also had practice on its side, so for a fair test, try the order the other way around tomorrow.

Step by step

Brake first, then one small thing

  1. Notice when it gets loud. Noise, pressure or a chat going off, and you speeding up and making more mistakes. That’s the signal to brake, not to push harder.
  2. Take your foot off the gas. Stop the thing you’re forcing. Hands off, and the chat muted or the phone face down for a set time.
  3. Breathe four in, six out. About four seconds in through your nose and six seconds out, for one quiet minute.
  4. Drop the shoulders, unclench the jaw. Let your shoulders fall and your teeth come apart. Check your hands too.
  5. Choose one small next action. The smallest piece that still counts: one move, one paragraph, one email, one bar of music. Do that, then pick the next.

How to tell it’s working. Count do-overs (falls, deleted lines, missed notes) in the ten minutes before a brake and the ten minutes after. If the after number is lower more often than not, the brake is working for you.

When it gets hard. Stopping feels like losing time when you’re already behind, so you’ll want to skip it exactly when you need it. Set the rule ahead of time: three mistakes in a row means one minute of brake.

Good to know. If your mind feels loud most days, or the alarm keeps coming back or ever feels unsafe, tell a parent or a coach you trust, and talk with a doctor.
