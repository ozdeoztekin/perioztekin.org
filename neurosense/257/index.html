Give Your Attention Something Specific to Find

Goal-Directed Attention & Search Cues

Your brain cannot fully process everything at once; a feature like red clothing tells attention what to favor.

The Science

  • More information reaches your senses than your brain can fully process at once, so attention helps select what gets priority.
  • Something sudden can grab attention, but a goal can also guide it, like a friend telling you, "I am wearing red."
  • This goal-directed attention changes what your limited processing is aimed at; it does not increase the capacity of the whole system.

The Protocol

  • Before searching, pick a reliable, visible feature that sets the target apart: clothing color, location or a particular shape.
  • Use the feature to find candidates, then check the details: a red jacket narrows the search but does not prove it is your friend.
  • If the cue turns out to be wrong, switch to a different one rather than repeating the same filter.

See it in real life

Example

A cello case above the crowd

A few hundred people come up the stairs from the platforms, and Eamon is supposed to find one of them. His cousin Astrid is taking the train in alone for the first time, and his job is to meet her in the main hall and get them both onto the 5:40 bus home. The next one isn’t for an hour.

He stands under the departures board scanning face after face. Ten minutes later his eyes ache, Astrid has texted “im here where r u,” and every time a suitcase clatters or an announcement starts, his eyes jump to the noise. He keeps telling himself to look harder.

Looking harder isn’t a very specific instruction. More information reaches the senses than the brain can fully process at once, so attention has to choose what gets priority. Something sudden, like a crash or a loud voice, can grab it, and a goal can also guide it. If a friend says she’s wearing red, not everyone in the crowd is an equally likely candidate anymore. That’s goal-directed attention. It changes what limited processing is aimed at without making the system any bigger.

He texts back, “What are you wearing?” and she answers, “yellow raincoat.” Now he looks only for yellow, and there are three coats, two on grown-ups and one on a girl with a long braid by the coffee stand. A yellow coat narrows the search but doesn’t prove anything, so he checks her face before waving, and it isn’t Astrid. Then a new text arrives, “took the coat off, too hot.” The cue is wrong now, so he drops yellow and switches to a shape, since she’s bringing her cello.

A black case is sticking up above the crowd near the ticket machines. Under it is Astrid, her raincoat over her arm. They reach the bus bay with four minutes to spare and ride home with the cello wedged between their knees.

  1. Where it stallsIn a crowd of hundreds, he looks harder and still can’t find her.
  2. What changes itHe asks what she’s wearing, checks each yellow coat, then switches cues.
  3. What happens nextHe spots the cello case, and they make the bus with minutes left.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

Find it by the spine

You need a friend and a crowded bookshelf, or any shelf packed with labeled things, like spice jars or board games.

  1. While you look away, your friend picks one book and tells you only its title. Start the first round, find the book, then stop the clock.
  2. For the second round, your friend picks a different book and tells you its title and the color of its spine. Find it the same way.
  3. In that second round, notice how many books of that color you checked before the title matched.
  4. For a bonus round, your friend can name the wrong color on purpose. Notice how long you keep searching that color before you give up on it and read titles instead.

The timer needs JavaScript. A phone stopwatch works just as well.

What you’ll probably notice. The color round is often much faster, since you stop treating every book as an equally likely candidate and check only the few that match. That’s goal-directed attention, a known feature steering limited processing toward what matters, and it doesn’t make your attention any bigger. The color only gave you candidates, and reading the title was what settled it.

Step by step

Give your search a feature

For finding someone or something in a crowded place, like a station, a stadium or a school event.

  1. Pick a feature before you look. Ask what will set the target apart and stay visible, like the color of a coat, a spot to meet or a shape, such as an instrument case.
  2. Use it to find candidates. Let your eyes pass over everything without the feature and stop only on what has it.
  3. Check the details before you wave. A red jacket narrows the search but doesn’t prove it’s your friend, so check the face, the voice or the name.
  4. Switch cues when one fails. If the feature is wrong or gone, like a coat taken off, change to another one, such as a place or a shape, rather than searching harder with the same filter.

How to tell it’s working. Next time you meet someone in a crowd, note roughly how long the search takes with a feature agreed in advance, compared with a time you scanned every face. Count how many wrong candidates you checked, too.

When it gets hard. It’s easy to keep trusting a cue after it stops fitting, like hunting for a red coat that’s now in a bag. If a couple of minutes go by with no match, ask for a new feature or agree on a place to meet.

Good to know. A good cue helps with this kind of search, and it doesn’t train your attention for everything else. If you can’t find someone you’re meeting and you start to worry, stay where you said you’d be, call them and ask staff for help.
