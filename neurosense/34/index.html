Pick the Wrong Music and You Sabotage Your Studying

Music & Neural Entrainment

The music you play isn't just background noise. It can help or hurt your studying.

The Science

  • Thaut, McIntosh & Hoemberg (Frontiers in Psychology, 2015): a review of how rhythm entrains the motor system: movements such as walking steps lock onto a steady beat, which therapists use to help patients walk more evenly.
  • Will & Berg (Neuroscience Letters, 2007): in EEG recordings, steady, repeating drumbeats increased the synchronization of brain waves, a sign that the brain can lock onto a rhythm.
  • Tierney & Kraus (Brain and Language, 2013): teenagers who were better at tapping along to a beat tended to score higher on reading and attention measures, a link rather than proof that rhythm training helps.
  • Perham & Currie (Applied Cognitive Psychology, 2014): students scored lower on a reading test while hearing music with lyrics, whether they liked it or not, than in quiet or with instrumental music.

The Protocol

  • Build two playlists: a wordless one for studying and a slow one for winding down.
  • Start the study playlist a few minutes before you begin, so it becomes a cue that it's time to work.
  • Match volume to task: lower for deep cognitive work, louder for physical or repetitive work.

See it in real life

Example

Two playlists and a no-skip rule

Three nights in a row, the first half hour of Gianna’s studying has gone to the music. She sits down with her precalculus review, and by the time a song feels right, problem one is still blank. The final is on Friday.

Her study mix is two hundred songs on shuffle, so a hype song comes on, then a slow one, then something she has to skip. Every change pulls her hand to the phone, and every skip turns into a search for something better. On Monday she finished seven problems.

Sound with a steady beat can become a timing signal for the nervous system. Movements like walking tend to lock onto a steady beat, and brain waves can fall into step with it too, which is called neural entrainment. It isn’t remote control, but rhythm gives the body a pattern to follow. In one study, though, students did worse on a reading test while hearing songs with lyrics, whether they liked the songs or not. Her shuffle changes the pattern every three minutes, half the songs have lyrics, and each one gives her a reason to touch the phone.

So she builds two playlists and stops editing them. One is for getting going, steady, wordless and not too loud, and the other is for afterward, slow and simple. On Wednesday she starts the first one on her laptop eight minutes before she means to begin, with the volume low and a rule against skipping. While it plays, she clears the desk and lays out the practice test. When she reaches the word problems and catches herself listening instead of working, she turns it down almost to nothing.

She writes the first line of problem one three minutes after sitting down. Before the hour-long playlist starts over, she has finished all nineteen problems in the trig section. Then she puts on the slow playlist, stretches on the floor for a song and a half and packs her bag.

  1. Where it stallsShe spends half an hour skipping songs before she starts problem one.
  2. What changes itShe starts a steady, wordless playlist eight minutes early and never skips.
  3. What happens nextShe finishes nineteen problems before the playlist starts over.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

Sort to the beat

You’ll need a deck of cards, the stopwatch below and two wordless tracks you can play quietly, one fast and steady and one slow.

  1. Shuffle the deck and sort it into a red pile and a black pile in silence, timing yourself.
  2. Shuffle again. Start the fast, steady track at low volume, let it play for half a minute, then sort and time it.
  3. Shuffle once more and do the same with the slow track.
  4. Watch your hands as well as the clock, and notice whether they start moving with the beat.

The timer needs JavaScript. A phone stopwatch works just as well.

What you’ll probably notice. With music on, your hands may fall into the beat without you deciding to, faster with the fast track and slower with the slow one, though not everyone’s time changes. That’s entrainment in a small way, with rhythm giving movement a pattern to follow. It’s also why a steady track can make starting easier and one that keeps changing can make a task feel messy.

Step by step

Two playlists, started early

Build them once, then leave them alone.

  1. Make a playlist for getting going. Make it steady, wordless and not too loud, and long enough for a whole session. It’s for the first minutes of focus, a workout or a cleaning sprint.
  2. Make one for winding down. Make this one slow, simple and predictable, for stretching, recovering or getting ready for sleep.
  3. Start it five to ten minutes early. Put it on while you set up, clearing the desk, filling your water and opening the work. Over time, the playlist becomes a cue that it’s time to work.
  4. Keep it low for hard thinking. Play it quieter for problems, reading or writing, and louder for physical or repetitive work. Once it starts, don’t skip.
  5. Go silent if it steals your focus. If you catch yourself listening instead of working, switch to something simpler or to silence.

How to tell it’s working. For a week, write down when you sat down, when you started and how much you finished. Compare nights with the getting-going playlist to nights with your usual music or none, and look mostly at the gap between sitting down and starting.

When it gets hard. Building the perfect playlist can turn into a new way to put off starting. Give it one sitting of twenty minutes at most, then stop editing it for the week.
