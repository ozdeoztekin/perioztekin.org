The Hidden Side Effect of Testing Yourself

Testing Effect & Retrieval-Induced Forgetting

Quizzing yourself boosts what you recall, but it can make related answers you skipped harder to reach.

The Science

  • Memory is not a shelf: each successful retrieval can make that memory easier to bring back again. That is the testing effect.
  • When similar town names compete and you recall the right one, the losing names can be inhibited, pushed down so they are harder to recall later.
  • That is retrieval-induced forgetting. The boost to what you retrieved is usually large, and the cost to related material is often small.

The Protocol

  • Keep quizzing yourself with flashcards or practice questions; the side effect is no reason to stop.
  • After each session, list the related items you never got asked.
  • Start your next session with those skipped items before moving on to the rest.

See it in real life

Example

The skipped pile in his apron

The cup Bashir slides across the counter is pale green and creamy, and the customer looks at it and says, “I ordered the slush.” She’s right. It’s his third week at the bubble tea shop, and his hands made the honeydew milk tea, the drink he gets quizzed on most.

Every shift starts with a quiz. Before the doors open, Ms. Liang, the owner, calls out drinks and he says each recipe back, and at home he runs through index cards. He’s fast on the eight best sellers now, but the quizzes never reach the slushes, and when the honeydew slush came in, the milk tea showed up first and the slush wouldn’t come.

The quizzing is doing its job. Each time he pulls up a recipe, it can get easier to pull up again, which is called the testing effect. But memory isn’t a shelf where things go back unchanged. Related memories compete, and when one keeps winning, the losers can be pushed down so they’re harder to recall later. That’s retrieval-induced forgetting. The gain for what he practices is usually large and the cost often small, but the drinks next to the milk teas that never get asked are the ones that can lose ground.

He keeps the quizzes and changes the order. After each one he writes the related drinks that never came up, like the slushes and the seasonal ones, on cards of their own and rubber-bands them into a skipped pile. The next session starts with that pile before anything else, and on Thursday he asks Ms. Liang to open her quiz with the slushes. She raises an eyebrow and does.

On Saturday the line reaches the door by two. A kid in a soccer jersey orders a honeydew slush, and Bashir makes it with the recipe binder closed on its shelf. By closing time, the skipped pile in his apron is down to four cards.

  1. Where it stallsHe makes the milk tea he’s quizzed on daily, not the slush she ordered.
  2. What changes itHe starts every quiz with the related drinks that never came up.
  3. What happens nextOn Saturday he makes the honeydew slush with the binder closed.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

Practice half the fruit

Get a pen and some scrap paper, and keep one hand free to cover the box.

  1. Read both lists in the box for 30 seconds, then cover them.
  2. Look only at the practice row and say the full word for each start out loud, like orange for “or.” Go through the row three times, with a short pause between rounds.
  3. Count backward from 50 by threes, out loud, for about half a minute.
  4. Write down every word you can from both lists, the tools first and then the fruit.
  5. Compare three groups, the two fruits you practiced, the two fruits you skipped and the tools.
Fruitorange, banana, cherry, mango
Toolshammer, saw, drill, wrench
PracticeFruit, or… Fruit, ba…

Cover the first two rows before you practice.

What you’ll probably notice. Orange and banana usually come back first and fastest, which is the testing effect at work. Cherry and mango, the fruits you skipped, may come back a little slower or a little less often than the tools, which nobody practiced, because practicing their neighbors can push them down. In one short round that cost may be too small to see, while the gain for the practiced pair is easy to see, so the answer is to keep quizzing and change what comes first.

Step by step

Quiz, then rotate the skipped pile

It works with flashcards, practice questions or anyone willing to quiz you.

  1. Keep quizzing yourself. Keep using flashcards or practice questions, out loud or on paper. The gain for what you recall is usually large, so the side effect is no reason to stop.
  2. List what never came up. After each session, write down the related items you never got asked, like the other drinks in the same family or the other dates in the same chapter.
  3. Keep them in a skipped pile. Put them on cards of their own or at the top of your notes, somewhere you’ll see them first.
  4. Start the next session with that pile. Go through the skipped items before anything else, and then carry on with the rest of the deck.
  5. Make a fresh pile every time. Whatever goes unasked in the new session becomes the next pile, so the gain keeps moving around.

How to tell it’s working. The rotation hasn’t been tested as a schedule, so watch your own numbers. Before each session, count the cards in the skipped pile and how many you get right on the first try, and see over a week whether the pile shrinks and that score climbs.

When it gets hard. The skipped pile is full of the items you’d rather avoid, so it tends to drift back to the bottom. Keep it on top of the deck with a rubber band around it, or ask whoever quizzes you to start there.
