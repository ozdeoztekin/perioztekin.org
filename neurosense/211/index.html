Give Your Memory Somewhere to Go

Memory Palace & Ordered Cues

A familiar route can give a loose list an order, with each stop along the way cueing one item.

The Science

  • A list of unrelated words gives memory little to follow. A memory palace gives each item an address at a familiar spot you revisit in your mind.
  • The hippocampus helps form new memories and holds place cells, tuned to familiar locations; they may also help represent a familiar room you imagine.
  • A strange image, like a banana wearing a boot on your couch, stands out, and the route gives you ordered cues for where to look next.

The Protocol

  • Pick 20 spots you know well in their natural order, such as your front walk, the doorbell and the couch.
  • Have a friend read you 20 words, about one every five seconds, and place each one at its spot as a strange image.
  • Walk the route in your mind, then repeat it at your own pace, aiming for a clear cue at each spot rather than speed.

See it in real life

Example

A tuba ringing the doorbell

Andile’s biggest speech in the community theater’s spring comedy is a list. He plays a grandfather who won’t clean out the attic and names 20 things he refuses to throw away, in a set order, with no props. Every few items, Delia, playing his granddaughter, answers with a line about the last one.

At Tuesday’s rehearsal he put the tuba before the waffle iron and skipped the moose head, so Delia’s moose joke landed on a sewing machine, and the director stopped the scene again. Opening night is in a week, and the list he rereads on the bus still blurs after six items.

Twenty unrelated things give memory little to follow. A memory palace gives each item an address at a familiar spot you revisit in your mind. Places may work so well because the hippocampus, which helps form new memories, also holds place cells, neurons that fire for a particular familiar location, and they may help represent a familiar room you only picture. His own home already has its stops in order, and a strange image at each one stands out.

On Wednesday he lists 20 spots in the order he passes them coming home, from the front walk to his bunk bed, and his sister reads him the play’s list, about one item every five seconds. He parks each item at its spot doing something strange. The tuba rings the doorbell, and the moose head lies on the couch in his dad’s reading glasses. Walking the route in his mind, he finds two spots empty, so he goes through again at his own pace and makes them louder, with a waffle iron flipping pancakes in the fish tank.

On Saturday he walks that route in his head while he talks, one spot at a time. The moose head comes seventh, Delia’s joke gets a laugh from the stage crew, and the director doesn’t stop the scene once.

  1. Where it stallsHe swaps the tuba and the waffle iron and skips the moose head.
  2. What changes itHe parks each of the twenty items at a spot on his way home.
  3. What happens nextThe moose head comes seventh, and the scene runs to the end.

Try it yourself · 10 minutes

Ten words, ten stops

Find a friend to read aloud, and grab a pen and some paper. Pick a route you could walk with your eyes closed, like the way from your front door to your bed.

  1. Write down ten spots along that route in the order you pass them, like the doormat, the coat hooks and the couch.
  2. Have your friend read list A at about one word every five seconds while you listen. Then write down every word you can, in order.
  3. For list B, at the same pace, put each word at the next spot on your route doing something strange, like a candle melting all over the doormat.
  4. Walk the route in your mind and write down what you find at each spot.
  5. Score both lists with one point for each word in its right place.
List Asaddle, peach, hammer, kite, mitten, toaster, owl, bench, shovel, harp
List Bbarrel, cactus, whistle, pumpkin, scarf, robot, feather, ladle, teapot, zebra

Only the reader looks at these lists.

What you’ll probably notice. List A tends to come back as a few words with the middle scrambled, because ten unrelated words give memory little to follow. List B usually comes back in better order, since each spot hands you the next prompt and you only have to look in one place at a time. If a spot came up empty, its picture was probably too ordinary, so make that one stranger.

Step by step

Build a route, then fill it

The 20 spots and the five seconds come from a classic practice exercise, not a magic formula, so treat them as a starting point and then use the route for any list where order matters.

  1. Pick 20 spots in their natural order. Walk through your home, or picture it, and list 20 spots you know well in the order you reach them, like the front walk, the doorbell and the couch.
  2. Have a friend read you 20 words. Ask for about one word every five seconds, which gives you time to place each one before the next arrives.
  3. Make each word strange at its spot. Picture the item doing something odd right there, bigger, louder or sillier than real life, since a weird image stands out.
  4. Walk the route in your mind. Start at the first spot, look for the image, say the word and move on to the next one. Write down what you find, in order.
  5. Repeat it at your own pace. Go through again slowly and fix any spot that came up empty or fuzzy. A clear cue at each spot matters more than speed.

How to tell it’s working. Score each run with one point for every word in its right place, and compare it with a plain list read at the same pace. If the route brings back more words in order, keep it for the lists where order matters.

When it gets hard. If two spots blur together, swap one for a spot that looks different, like a red door instead of a second plain wall. If an image keeps fading, have the item do something to the spot, since a tuba ringing the doorbell is harder to lose than a tuba standing next to it.
