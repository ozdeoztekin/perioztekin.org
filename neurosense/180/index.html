Delay the Impulse

self-control and delay

An impulse feels powerful because the reward is close enough to glow.

The Science

  • Fast reward signals can dominate before long-term value fully enters the decision.
  • A short pause may give control systems a chance to change what the option is worth.
  • Even a brief delay may help turn a reflex into a choice.

The Protocol

  • Set a tiny timer before sending, buying, eating, or replying.
  • Ask what this choice gives now and what it costs later.
  • Choose after the timer, when the glow has less of a monopoly.

See it in real life

Example

Ninety seconds by the store window

In the fitting room mirror the jacket looks like it was made for Wren, and Dani says so out loud. It costs $95, and Wren is already halfway to the register.

In her phone case is $45 from babysitting the Henderson twins last night. With the $130 in the envelope at home, that’s enough for the $160 used sewing machine the repair shop on Main Street is holding for her until Saturday. For a year she has been taking in thrift-store jeans by hand, one stitch at a time, and the jacket would leave her $80 short.

The jacket is right here, with the mirror and Dani’s face attached, and Saturday is four days away. Fast reward signals can run a decision before long-term value fully gets a say. A short pause may give the brain’s control systems a chance to change what an option is worth, and even a brief delay can help turn a reflex into a choice.

“Give me ninety seconds,” she tells Dani, and starts a timer on her phone. She carries the jacket to the front window and asks herself two things. What it gives her now is Monday at school and that feeling from the mirror. What it costs her later is the machine on Saturday and another year of sewing by hand.

When the timer rings, she still likes the jacket, but she likes the machine more. She hangs the jacket back on the rack and splits a pretzel with Dani on the way out. On Saturday she carries the sewing machine home in a laundry basket, and by Sunday night the first pair of jeans is taken in, with one seam only a little crooked.

  1. Where it stallsThe jacket costs $95, and she’s already heading for the register.
  2. What changes itShe sets a ninety-second timer by the window and asks two questions.
  3. What happens nextThe jacket goes back on the rack, and the sewing machine comes home Saturday.

Try it yourself · one day

The ninety-second test

Save it for the next pull you feel today to buy, send, post or reply. All you need is the timer below.

  1. When you notice the pull, stop before you act and start the timer.
  2. While it runs, ask yourself, out loud or in your head, what this gives you now and what it costs you later.
  3. Keep your hands off the button, the cart or the send arrow until it rings.
  4. When it rings, choose, and either answer is allowed. Notice whether you still want it as much as you did.
  5. If ninety seconds feels impossible, do ten seconds, and then ten more.

The timer needs JavaScript. A phone stopwatch works just as well.

What you’ll probably notice. Often the pull is a little weaker when the timer rings, and the cost-later answer is easier to hear. Sometimes you still want it, and that’s fine, because now it’s a choice instead of a reflex. A short pause may give long-term value time to catch up with a fast reward signal.

Step by step

Ninety seconds before you act

Use it for buying, sending, posting and replying, the places where impulses usually win.

  1. Know your impulse spots. Name the one or two places it usually happens to you, like the checkout, the group chat, late-night shopping or a reply to someone who annoyed you.
  2. Set a short timer first. Before you buy, send or reply, start a ninety-second timer, and keep your hands off until it rings.
  3. Ask what it gives now and costs later. Ask what this gives you right now and what it costs later, in money, time or how someone sees you.
  4. Choose after the timer. When it rings, decide. Either answer is fine, as long as it’s a decision and not a reflex.
  5. Park it if you’re unsure. Leave it in the cart, save the draft or walk away, and look again tomorrow, since still wanting it then tells you something.

How to tell it’s working. Over the next week, count the timers you set and how many times you changed your mind when they rang. If you change your mind some of the time, or end up with fewer purchases and messages you regret, the timer is worth the ninety seconds.

When it gets hard. Ninety seconds can feel endless with friends waiting or a sale countdown ticking. Start with ten seconds and do it twice, and say what you’re doing, like “give me a minute,” so nobody rushes you.
