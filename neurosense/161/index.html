Why Boredom Is a Door to New Ideas

Boredom and Mind Wandering

Boredom can feel like a problem, and the phone offers an exit in half a second.

The Science

  • In two small studies, people were more creative after a dull writing or reading task.
  • One study tied the gain to a wandering mind, but a later test found no such link.
  • In one study, a dull, easy task during a break helped more than resting did.

The Protocol

  • Leave the phone untouched for three minutes before a creative task.
  • Do something dull and easy, like folding laundry, with no clips or songs.
  • Write down one weird, useful, or unfinished idea when the timer ends.

See it in real life

Example

A résumé for Moose

Moose has lived at the county animal shelter for 214 days, longer than any other dog there, and he spends most of them asleep in the one patch of sun in the yard. Tessa walks dogs there on Saturdays, and she offered to make the poster for next week’s adoption day, due Thursday.

Four evenings in, the poster board is blank. Every time she sits down with a marker, the quiet lasts about two seconds before she’s on her phone, scrolling through other shelters’ posters. They all look alike, with sad eyes, a paw print and “Adopt, don’t shop,” and so do her sketches.

Each search fills the gap with someone else’s finished idea. In a few small studies, people came up with more creative ideas after a stretch of something dull and easy, and one idea is that the mind wanders and puts old pieces together in new ways, though that part is still debated. Tessa hasn’t given her mind a single dull minute. Writing something down afterward keeps a stray idea from floating off.

On Wednesday she leaves her phone on the kitchen counter, sets the egg timer for three minutes and takes it out to the back steps with a basket of socks to match and nothing in her ears. The first minute itches, and twice her hand goes to her empty pocket. Then her mind drifts to Moose in his patch of sun, the “Now Hiring” sign at the pizza place and her brother groaning over his first résumé. When the timer rings, she writes two words on an envelope, “Moose’s résumé.”

By bedtime the poster is a job application. It lists Moose’s age as 9, his experience as 214 days of professional napping, his skills as leaning on legs, snoring and waiting, and the job he’s looking for as one couch, sunny side. On Saturday two people quote it at the front desk, and one of them asks to meet him.

  1. Where it stallsAfter four evenings of scrolling, her poster board is still blank.
  2. What changes itShe leaves her phone inside and matches socks on the steps for three minutes.
  3. What happens nextShe makes Moose a résumé poster, and someone asks to meet him.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

The empty three minutes

Pick one small thing you need an idea for, like a caption, a gift, a title or something to draw. You’ll need paper, a pen and a timer that rings.

  1. Write the question at the top of the page in a few words, like “title for the video” or “what to draw next.”
  2. Set a three-minute timer and put your phone out of reach, even if it’s the timer.
  3. Do something dull and easy, like copying numbers from a receipt or doodling circles, with nothing playing, and let your mind go wherever it goes.
  4. When you feel the itch to grab something, notice it and stay put for one more breath.
  5. When the timer rings, write down one weird, useful or unfinished idea. Half an idea counts.

What you’ll probably notice. The first minute usually feels the longest, and a lot of people catch themselves reaching for something at least once. After that, thoughts tend to drift to odd places, like an old conversation or a sign you walked past, and now and then two of them snap together into something new. Your mind may be doing useful work in the background while it drifts, and writing things down keeps them from floating off.

Step by step

Give the idea a room first

Try it before the next creative thing you’re stuck on.

  1. Name the task first. Write what you’re about to make in a few words, like the poster, the caption, the drawing or the costume.
  2. Leave the phone alone for three minutes. Set a timer that rings, and leave the phone out of reach until it does. Face down across the room is fine.
  3. Feed it nothing new. Skip clips, songs and podcasts, do something dull and easy, like folding laundry, and let your mind drift wherever it goes.
  4. Stay one more breath. The urge to escape usually shows up in the first minute, so notice it, don’t follow it and stay put one breath longer.
  5. Write one idea when it rings. Write one weird, useful or unfinished idea on paper, and then start the task from it, even if it’s only half there.

How to tell it’s working. Keep every idea on one page with the date. After a week, count how many you used, and compare the sessions that started with three empty minutes with the ones that started with a scroll.

When it gets hard. Some days the three minutes feel endless and nothing shows up. Write down whatever is there anyway, even a question or half a sentence, since unfinished ideas count and some of them finish themselves later.
