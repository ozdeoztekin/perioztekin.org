When Thinking Through Every Move Gets in the Way

Procedural Skill & Stepwise Control

Close attention helps while you learn a move; once it is well learned, controlling every piece can disrupt it.

The Science

  • Early in learning, thinking through the pieces, like where to place a foot or what comes next, can help organize an unfamiliar action.
  • With practice, parts of a skill can become procedural: the action runs without a separate conscious command for each part as it unfolds.
  • Under pressure, detailed checking can return, and controlling familiar pieces one by one can interfere with carrying out the movement.

The Protocol

  • Ask about the specific movement: is it already reliable, or are you still learning it?
  • For a safe, well-learned action, use a practice attempt to work deliberately on the detail that actually needs attention.
  • Then perform the familiar move, like a short putt, without new instructions for each joint, keeping attention on task and safety.

See it in real life

Example

Seven turns without a checklist

Zubair has been doing flip turns since he was nine, and for years they ran without a thought, with his eyes on the black T painted near the wall, then a tuck and a push. Two weeks ago, during turn drills, his coach told him his push-off was too shallow, right up at the surface.

Since then he has talked himself through every turn, chin down, knees in, feet planted at the right depth, push. At Saturday’s time trial before the conference meet, with that list running, his feet barely caught the wall on two turns of the 200 freestyle, and he finished four seconds off his best.

Thinking through the pieces helps early on, because spelling out where a foot goes or what comes next organizes a movement you don’t know yet. With practice, parts of a skill can become procedural, running without a separate conscious command for each part. Under pressure, detailed checking can come back, and steering familiar pieces one by one can get in the way. Close attention still has its place, since beginners often need it, and so does a part of a skill you haven’t practiced much.

So he asks which part of his turn is reliable and which he’s still learning. The flip is reliable, and the deeper push-off is new. At Tuesday’s practice he gives the new part its own attempts, ten turns from the flags at easy speed, thinking only about pushing off a hand’s width deeper while his coach watches. In the race-pace sets he swims each turn as one move, eyes on the T and the wall instead of a list, and he goes over it with his coach after each set.

At the conference meet he swims the 200 freestyle without a missed wall. He touches under his best time, and his coach, watching from the deck, counts five deep push-offs out of seven and adds turn drills to Thursday’s plan.

  1. Where it stallsHe talks himself through every turn, and his feet miss the wall.
  2. What changes itHe drills the push-off on its own, then swims whole turns without a list.
  3. What happens nextHe swims seven clean turns and touches under his best time.

Try it yourself · 2 minutes

Your signature, two ways

Grab a pen, some scrap paper and the stopwatch below. Your signature is a safe, well-learned movement, which is the only kind to try this with.

  1. For round 1, sign your name the way you always do, at your usual speed, and time it.
  2. For round 2, sign it again while telling your hand what to do with every stroke as you go, like “down, loop, across,” and time that too.
  3. Look at the two signatures side by side.
  4. Now sign once with your other hand, slowly, thinking through each stroke, and notice whether the step-by-step attention feels useful this time.

The timer needs JavaScript. A phone stopwatch works just as well.

What you’ll probably notice. Directing every stroke usually makes the signature slower, and it often comes out stiffer or a little off, even though nothing about your hand changed. A well-learned movement can run without a separate command for each part, and taking over every stroke gets in its way. The other hand is a different case, since that version is still being learned, and thinking it through is what organizes it.

Step by step

Fix it in practice, let it run

For a safe move you already do well that falls apart when you think through every part of it.

  1. Ask about this one move. Is this exact movement reliable for you, or are you still learning it? Being experienced overall doesn’t make every part of a skill equally automatic.
  2. Give the fix its own attempts. Set aside practice attempts to work on the one detail that needs attention, deliberately and at an easy pace, with your coach watching if you can.
  3. Then do the whole move without a checklist. Perform the familiar move without new instructions for each joint, with your attention on the task and on safety, like the wall, the ball or the edge of the mat.
  4. Review it afterward. Watch a video or ask your coach after the attempt, not during it. If the problem is still there, go back to focused practice.
  5. Keep taking your coach’s corrections. Letting a move run never means ignoring a correction you need. It means working on that correction in practice, where it has room.

How to tell it’s working. Count something you can see on video or your coach can tell you, like how many of ten turns are clean and how often the corrected detail shows up. Compare a session where you ran a checklist during the move with one where the fix had its own attempts, in practice and under pressure.

When it gets hard. Under pressure the checklist tends to come back by itself. When you catch yourself listing joints, put your attention back on the task, like the wall coming up, and save the review for afterward.

Good to know. Use this only with safe moves you already do well, and never distract yourself on purpose during anything risky. Not every mistake under pressure comes from overthinking, so if something keeps going wrong, work it out with your coach.
