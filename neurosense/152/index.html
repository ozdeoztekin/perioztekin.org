Why a Quick Check Costs You Ten Minutes

Attention Residue After Interruptions

When one quick check turns into ten foggy minutes, the message is not the whole problem.

The Science

  • Your attention does not switch like a light, it carries residue from the last task.
  • An unfinished thread, like an unanswered message, can keep pulling at your attention.
  • That leftover pull competes with the sentence or problem right in front of you.

The Protocol

  • Before you check anything, write the exact next action to return to.
  • Set the phone face down and out of reach once the check is done.
  • Restart with the written action, not with a brand new decision.

See it in real life

Example

Row 41 of Yiayia’s hat

Chris was on stitch 23 of row 41 when his phone buzzed. The hat is for his grandmother’s birthday on Sunday, in plum, her favorite color, made from rows of ribbing that he’ll sew into a tube. Yiayia taught him his first chain stitch, and she has no idea this is coming.

The message was from Jasper, asking, “did you say something to Coach about me?” Chris read it in ten seconds and put the phone down without answering. When he picked the hook back up, he couldn’t tell whether he was on stitch 23 or 29, so he ripped out the row. Twenty-five minutes later he has started row 41 three times, lost count every time and written four replies to Jasper in his head.

Reading the message took ten seconds, but attention doesn’t switch like a light. It carries leftovers from the last thing, and a message he hasn’t answered keeps asking what it means and what he should say. That leftover pull is competing with the one row in his hands.

Before he touches the phone again, he writes the exact next step on a sticky note and presses it to the lamp, “Row 41 from the start, back loops only, count every stitch.” Then he answers Jasper, “no, why? call you after dinner,” and sets the phone face down on the dresser across the room. The message is still replaying, so he adds one line to the note, “after dinner, ask what happened.” Then he does what the note says.

Row 41 comes out right on the first try, and by the time he calls Jasper he has finished row 47. On Saturday night he sews the seam and gathers the top, and on Sunday, before she puts it on, Yiayia runs a finger down the ribs, counting.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

The open-loop test

You’ll need your phone, any book, a pen and a sticky note. It works best with a thread that’s still open, like a question you haven’t answered or plans that are still being decided.

  1. Open your messages and read one thread that’s still open, all the way through, without replying.
  2. Put the phone down and copy five lines of the book by hand, tapping the counter below each time your mind goes back to the thread.
  3. Write one sentence about the thread on the sticky note, like “answer Jade about Saturday at 8,” and under it write the first words of the next line you’ll copy.
  4. Set the phone face down out of reach, reset the counter and copy the next five lines, starting from the note, and then compare the two counts.

The timer needs JavaScript. A phone stopwatch works just as well.

What you’ll probably notice. The first count is often higher than people expect, even though you chose not to reply. Part of your attention stays behind with the message, since an open thread keeps asking what it means and what you’ll say. The note doesn’t close the thread, but it gives your attention a place to land. Part of any drop is time passing, so try it again the next time a message arrives in the middle of something real.

Step by step

Protect the return

Use it the next time a check lands in the middle of work, practice or a project.

  1. Write the next step first. Before you open anything, write the exact next action on a sticky note, like “row 41 from the start, 36 stitches,” “problem 4, part b” or “bars 33 to 40, slow.” It should be specific enough to start without thinking.
  2. Check, then put the phone face down. Read what you need, and reply if it takes one line. Then set the phone face down and out of reach.
  3. Park the replay in one sentence. If the message keeps replaying, write one line about it on the note, like “after dinner, ask what happened,” and leave it there until then.
  4. Restart with the note. Read the note and do exactly that step. The deciding already happened before you checked, so there’s no “what now?” to answer.

How to tell it’s working. Do one block where you check without a note and one where you write it first. After each check, time how long it takes to finish one real piece, like a row, a problem or a clean run-through. If the gap gets shorter with the note, make the note a habit.

When it gets hard. Vague notes don’t work. “Keep going on the hat” gives your attention nowhere to land, while “row 41 from the start, back loops only” does, so if your note could mean three different things, add the first thing your hands will do.
