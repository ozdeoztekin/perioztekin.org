Your Group Can Agree and Still Miss the Answer

Common Knowledge Effect & Hidden Profiles

A group can feel productive and still choose badly when a crucial fact, known to one person, stays buried.

The Science

  • The common knowledge effect: discussion tends to circle facts two or more people hold, while a crucial fact known to only one or two can stay buried.
  • In a hidden profile, the best answer is scattered: shared facts point to Option A, while each fact favoring the better Option B sits with one member.
  • The group then likely spends most of its time on A's strengths and never discovers A's drawbacks, so the better option can lose.

The Protocol

  • Before a group decision, run a quick round where each person names one fact the others may lack.
  • In the same round, have each person name one drawback of the current favorite.
  • Examine every side openly, looking for missing facts and allowing for the favorite to fail, before you choose.

See it in real life

Example

Three hands up for the trampoline park

Paz turns sixteen on Saturday, and at lunch on Tuesday her three closest friends plan the surprise. It goes fast and feels great. Everyone remembers how much she loved the trampoline park a few years ago, it’s close to her house and it’s cheap. Every hand goes up, and Dov says he’ll book it after practice tomorrow.

Ivo’s hand went up with the others, but on the bus home it bothers him. His cousin works the front desk there, and last weekend she mentioned a private party that has the whole park on Saturday afternoon. He never brought it up. Everyone was agreeing, the talk kept moving, and he wasn’t sure it was the whole afternoon.

A group’s advantage is that everyone can put what they know on the table, but discussion tends to circle the facts two or more people already share, while a fact only one person holds can stay buried. This is called the common knowledge effect. In a hidden profile, the shared facts point to one option, and each fact favoring a better one sits with a different person, so the group can spend its time on the favorite’s strengths and never find its drawbacks. If Ivo was sitting on a fact, the others might be too.

At lunch the next day, before Dov books anything, Ivo asks for two minutes. Each of them names one thing the others might not know and one problem with the trampoline park. Ivo goes first with the private party. Thora says Paz told her last month that trampolines are for twelve-year-olds now. Dov says Paz keeps sending him videos from a pottery-painting studio that she wants to try.

Nobody had heard all three. Thora calls the studio and books four seats for Saturday afternoon. On Saturday, Paz walks in, sees her three friends behind a row of blank mugs and sits down without taking off her jacket.

  1. Where it stallsThree friends vote fast, and Ivo never mentions the private party.
  2. What changes itEach friend names one fact the others lack and one problem with the plan.
  3. What happens nextThe party moves to a pottery studio, and Paz sits right down.

Try it yourself · 10 minutes

The hidden fact game

You need two other people, like friends or family at dinner. Copy each card below onto its own scrap of paper, and give everyone the shared card plus one private card, so nobody sees anyone else’s private card.

  1. Everyone reads the shared card and their own private card without showing anyone.
  2. Talk it over for two minutes and decide where the team party should go.
  3. Now run a round. Each person names one fact the others might not have and one drawback of the place you picked.
  4. Decide again, then lay all the cards on the table.
The choicePizza for the team party on Friday night, at Place A or Place B.
Shared cardPlace A is across from school, a two-minute walk. It has a big back room. The team had a great time there last year.
Private card 1Place A’s back room is already booked that Friday.
Private card 2Three people on the team can’t eat gluten, and only Place B has a gluten-free crust.
Private card 3Place B, on Main Street, lends its party room for free on Fridays.

What you’ll probably notice. In the free talk, groups usually spend most of the time on what everyone already knows, so Place A tends to win. The round brings out the scattered facts, and once they’re all on the table, Place B usually looks better. Nobody hid anything, since the talk went where the shared facts were.

Step by step

One fact, one flaw

Run it before any group decision where everyone seems to agree fast.

  1. Call a quick round before you choose. Before anyone books, pays or votes, ask for two minutes. It works in person or in a group chat.
  2. Everyone names one fact others may lack. Each person says one thing the rest might not know, like a booking conflict, a price, a schedule or something the person you’re planning for said.
  3. Everyone names one drawback of the favorite. Even if you like the favorite, find one thing that could go wrong with it.
  4. Look at every side openly. Put the missing facts next to the favorite’s strengths, and allow for the favorite to fail before you settle on anything.
  5. Then choose. Decide with everything on the table. The favorite may still win, and that’s fine.

How to tell it’s working. After a few group decisions, count how many new facts each round turned up and whether any of them changed the choice.

When it gets hard. The round can feel pointless when everyone already agrees. Keep it to one sentence each, since fast agreement is when a buried fact is easiest to miss.
