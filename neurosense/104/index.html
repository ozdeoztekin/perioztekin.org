Why the Fix Is Often a Workaround, Not a Cure

Compensation & Rehabilitation Strategy

When people recover from a brain injury, we assume the damage simply healed. Part of it does. But a lot of recovery is quieter: learning to get around the problem.

The Science

  • Cicerone et al. (2011), Archives of Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation: a review of 112 studies of people with brain injury or stroke supported teaching workarounds, such as memory notebooks, and found that some training may also improve the underlying skills.
  • Clinical neuropsychology distinguishes restoration of function from compensation, where external supports carry a load the brain no longer handles alone.
  • Kleim and Jones (2008), Journal of Speech, Language, and Hearing Research: plasticity, the brain's ability to change with experience, is believed to underlie relearning after brain damage, though some workarounds can get in its way.

The Protocol

  • Offload memory onto a calendar, checklists, and reminders.
  • Reduce the demand: one task at a time, fewer inputs.
  • Shape the environment so the right action is the easy action.
  • Treat external supports as skill, not as failure.

See it in real life

Example

The ticket rail by the stove

“I ran a grill for thirty-one years,” Grandpa says, loudly enough that Emerson hears it from the front door. Her dad wants to pull the knobs off the stove.

Since his stroke in March, Grandpa has lived with them, and he makes his own lunch while everyone is out. Twice this month Emerson has come home to a burner glowing on low under nothing, with the pan already washed. With the radio going, he loses track of things halfway through them.

Some of what a stroke takes comes back as tissue and connections repair. But a lot of recovery is quieter. Doctors and therapists separate restoring an ability from working around it, using outside supports like a calendar, a checklist or a rearranged room to carry part of a load the brain no longer handles alone. Rehabilitation leans on those supports, and they help decide how much of what comes back works in daily life. Grandpa can make lunch without a perfect memory if the kitchen holds part of it for him.

Emerson’s answer is a ticket rail like the one over his grill at the diner, a strip of magnetic tape on the backsplash with a stack of index cards. Every lunch gets a ticket with three lines, burner on, timer set and burner off. A kitchen timer clips to his shirt pocket whenever a burner is lit, and the radio stays off until the pan is in the sink. Grandpa calls it ridiculous, then writes GRILLED CHEESE across the first ticket.

Two weeks later there are fourteen tickets on the rail with every “burner off” checked, and the knobs are still on the stove. On Saturday he makes pancakes for all five of them with the timer clipped to his apron.

  1. Where it stallsGrandpa leaves a burner on twice, and Dad talks about pulling the knobs.
  2. What changes itEmerson sets up a ticket rail by the stove, a clip-on timer and a quiet kitchen.
  3. What happens nextFourteen lunches later every burner is checked off, and he makes pancakes.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

The five-step job

You’ll need one other person, an index card and a pen. Having a TV, radio or music playing helps for the first round.

  1. Ask the other person to make up a five-step job around the room and say it once, like putting a spoon on the table, folding one towel, filling a glass halfway, setting a pen by the door and switching off one light.
  2. With the TV or music on, do the job from memory while they count how many steps you get right, in the right order.
  3. Have them make up a different five-step job. This time, write it on the card as they say it, turn off the TV or music, and do the job with the card in your hand.
  4. Compare the two scores, and how hard each round felt.

What you’ll probably notice. Five steps sounds easy, yet the first round often ends with a step missed or swapped, and the second round usually feels lighter. Nothing in your brain changed between rounds. The card held the order and the quiet removed the competition, so your attention had one job. That’s what a lot of rehabilitation does after a brain injury, with supports carrying part of the load so the person can still do the thing.

Step by step

Build a scaffold that holds

Supports are a skill, not a failure, and you can build one for your own brain too.

  1. Name the task that keeps slipping. Pick one task and say it plainly, like the burner, the practice bag, the reply you owe or the four o’clock call.
  2. Move it outside your head. Put it on a checklist, a calendar or an alarm, in the words of the person who will use it.
  3. Cut the inputs around it. Do one task at a time, with the radio off, extra tabs closed and questions saved until it’s done.
  4. Make the right move the easy one. Put the reminder where the task happens, like the card by the stove, the bag on the doorknob or the timer where the hand already goes.
  5. Reuse it until it runs itself. Use the same system in the same place every day, and change the part that doesn’t work instead of dropping the whole thing.

How to tell it’s working. For one week, mark each time the task gets missed. Then run a week with the scaffold in place and mark again, and if there are fewer misses, the scaffold is earning its place.

When it gets hard. Pride can get in the way, since a checklist can feel like an insult, especially to someone who used to do it all from memory. Build it with them, in their words and their system, and let them be the one who checks the boxes.

Good to know. These are everyday supports, not treatment. After a stroke or other brain injury, recovery should be guided by the person’s doctors and rehabilitation team, who can suggest supports that fit.
