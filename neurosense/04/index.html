Why ADHD Brains Can't Start (But Can Hyperfocus)

ADHD: Tonic Versus Phasic Dopamine

ADHD brains don't lack attention. They have a different dopamine fuel pattern: too low at baseline to start boring tasks, but flooded by anything stimulating.

The Science

  • Volkow et al. (JAMA, 2009): PET scans of adults with ADHD showed lower dopamine D2/D3 receptor and transporter availability in reward regions, including the nucleus accumbens and midbrain, and lower availability went with more attention problems.
  • Berridge & Arnsten (Neuroscience and Biobehavioral Reviews, 2013): the prefrontal cortex works best within a middle range of catecholamine signaling, and too little or too much both impair it; stimulant medicines for ADHD are thought to help by moving signaling toward that range.
  • Sonuga-Barke (Behavioural Brain Research, 2002): the dual pathway model proposes that ADHD can involve both weaker executive control and delay aversion, a strong pull toward immediate rewards and away from waiting.
  • Many people with ADHD also describe hyperfocus: on a high-interest activity, like a game or a passion project, they can lock in for hours. Research on it is still early (Ashinoff & Abu-Akel, 2021).

The Protocol

  • Five-minute warm-up: do something you enjoy that has a clear end, then move straight into the boring task.
  • Body double: work next to someone who is also working, in person or on a quiet video call.
  • Novelty reset: if you stall, change rooms or move to a new spot.

See it in real life

Example

Four nights stuck on the first speech balloon

On Saturday, Parker spent six hours shading a single dragon’s wing for his comic and forgot to eat lunch. He’s known he has ADHD since fifth grade, and the part people don’t expect is how long he can stay with something once he’s into it. Getting started is what he can’t do.

His comic is one chapter of an online anthology that six friends from art class are putting together, and the pages are due Friday at midnight. Before then, every page needs its lettering, which means typing all 112 speech balloons and fitting each one by hand. Four nights in a row he has opened page one, looked at the first empty balloon and closed the file within a minute.

Brain scans of adults with ADHD show differences in the dopamine pathway that helps a reward feel worth chasing, and for many people with ADHD, a payoff that’s days away seems to count for less. So a dull job with a far-off reward is very hard to begin. Something he loves is a different story, which is how the same brain that can’t face one speech balloon spent six hours on a wing.

On Tuesday he writes “balloons 1 to 40” on a sticky note, and under it “launch night, May 9,” the night the anthology goes online. He sets a five-minute timer and adds highlights to the dragon’s eye, the part he loves, and the moment it rings he clicks into the first balloon without checking anything else. His sister Jess does her chemistry homework across the table from him. When he stalls around balloon 38, he picks up the tablet and moves out to the back steps.

By 9:15 he’s at balloon 71. He finishes on Wednesday and uploads his pages Thursday night, a whole day early, which Jess says has never happened before.

  1. Where it stallsHe can shade a wing for six hours but can’t start the lettering.
  2. What changes itFive minutes on the part he loves, then straight into the first balloon.
  3. What happens nextHe reaches balloon 71 that night and turns his pages in a day early.

Try it yourself · 2 minutes

Now or later

This shows how much harder a reward pulls when you can have it right now. You don’t need anything, but it’s more fun with a second person.

  1. Read each pair once and pick the one you’d really take. Go with your first answer, without doing any math.
  2. Mark the first line where you’d wait for the bigger amount.
  3. Ask someone else the same five questions, and compare where each of you switched.
120 dollars today, or 22 dollars in a month
220 dollars today, or 25 dollars in a month
320 dollars today, or 30 dollars in a month
420 dollars today, or 40 dollars in a month
520 dollars today, or 60 dollars in a month

There are no right answers. You’re only looking for the line where you switch.

What you’ll probably notice. Twenty dollars today usually wins at least once, even when waiting a month would pay a lot more, and people switch at different lines. Researchers call this pull toward rewards you can have now delay aversion, and they think it’s stronger for many people with ADHD. That’s why a Friday deadline can feel like nothing until Thursday night, and why the plan below puts something you like right before the dull part.

Step by step

Get the dull task started

Pick one task and try all five steps together the first time.

  1. Write down the task and why it matters. Make it small and exact, like “balloons 1 to 40” instead of “lettering.” Underneath, write the thing you care about that this task leads to.
  2. Do five minutes of something you love. A song, a clip or a quick sketch works, as long as it has a clear end. Set a timer so five minutes stays five.
  3. Go straight into the task. When the timer goes off, start the first piece of the task right away, without checking anything on the way.
  4. Work next to someone. A sibling at the table, a friend at the library or a quiet video call where you both work is enough. They don’t have to help, just be there.
  5. Change rooms when you stall. Move to the kitchen, the back steps or the library. A new spot is often enough to get you going again.

How to tell it’s working. Time how long it takes you to get from sitting down to doing the first real piece of the task, once on a normal day and once with these steps. Then compare how much you got done in the first half hour.

When it gets hard. The five minutes can swallow the whole evening if the fun thing never ends on its own, like a feed or a game. Pick something that stops by itself, like one song or one scene.

Good to know. These are self-help steps, and ADHD is a real condition. If starting things is a struggle most days and it’s getting in the way of school or things you care about, talk with a parent and a doctor, and ask a school counselor what support your school can offer.
