The Temptation Bundle

temptation bundling habits

Some useful habits lose simply because the reward arrives too late.

The Science

  • Your nervous system is always negotiating between reward, effort, and time.
  • Temptation bundling attaches an immediate want to a delayed-benefit action.
  • That pairing works best when the tempting part is available only during the useful one.

The Protocol

  • Choose one enjoyable thing that is easy to save for the habit.
  • Allow it only during the habit you keep avoiding.
  • Keep the pairing simple enough that setup stays light.

See it in real life

Example

New episodes only on Boone walks

Boone ate the remote on Tuesday. Before that it was a library book, and before that a corner of the couch cushion.

He’s the two-year-old shepherd mix Freya’s family adopted from the shelter in October, mostly because she begged. The trainer said he needs a long walk every morning, and Freya promised the walks would be hers. In the last ten days she has done three. At 6:30 it’s dark, and the bed is warm. Last night her dad said that if the chewing hasn’t stopped by the end of the month, they’ll have to call the shelter.

Her nervous system is always weighing reward, effort and time, and this deal is lopsided: a cold, dark walk now for a calmer dog weeks from now. Temptation bundling attaches something she wants right now to a habit whose benefit comes later, and it works best when the tempting part is available only during the useful one.

The want is easy to find: the mystery podcast her friend Lina got her hooked on, about a painting stolen from a small-town museum, with eleven episodes still to go. Freya makes one rule. New episodes play only on Boone walks. The earbuds now hang from the leash hook by the front door, which keeps setup to one reach. She wears just one, to hear the street.

Over the next four weeks she walks him on twenty-six mornings, a check for each on the fridge calendar. Twice she adds an extra block to finish an episode. The chewing stops in the second week. By the end of the month nobody has said the word shelter again, and Boone naps on the cushion he used to eat.

  1. Where it stallsThree morning walks in ten days, and Boone chewing the house
  2. What changes itNew podcast episodes only on walks, earbuds on the leash hook
  3. What happens nextTwenty-six walks in four weeks, and the chewing stops

Try it yourself · 10 minutes

Find your bundle

Build your own pairing and try it once today. You need paper, a pen, and the habit you’ve been putting off.

  1. Write two short lists: three habits you keep putting off, and three things you look forward to that would be easy to save, like a podcast, a playlist, a show or an audiobook.
  2. Draw a line between one habit and one treat that can happen at the same time, so your hands or legs do the habit while your ears or eyes get the treat.
  3. Check the rule: could you keep that treat for the habit only? If it’s something you’d have all day anyway, pick something smaller or more specific.
  4. Put the two in one place, like earbuds on the leash hook or in the bag with your running shoes, so starting takes one reach.
  5. Do five minutes of the habit with the treat on, today, and notice how the first minute feels.

What you’ll probably notice. What usually changes is the first minute, because there’s a reason to start that arrives right away instead of weeks from now. That’s the pairing from The Science above, matching the reward’s timing to the behavior. If the treat starts showing up in the rest of your day, the pull fades, which is why the rule matters more than the treat.

Step by step

Lock the treat to the habit

Pick the one habit you keep avoiding and run this for three weeks.

  1. Choose one treat that’s easy to save. A podcast, a playlist, a show or an audiobook works well: something you want, but can live without on other days.
  2. Allow it only during the habit. The rule is the whole tool. The treat starts when the habit starts and stops when it stops.
  3. Keep the setup to one reach. Store the treat with the gear: earbuds on the leash hook, the playlist downloaded on the phone you take running.
  4. Mark the days you did it. A check on a calendar for each day. Look at the count, not at any single day.
  5. Swap the treat when it leaks. If you catch yourself using it outside the habit, or it stops pulling, pick something smaller or more specific.

How to tell it’s working. Count the days you did the habit in the two weeks before the bundle and the two weeks with it. If the second count is higher, or you start sooner after you meant to, it’s working.

When it gets hard. The treat leaks fastest on boring days, when it’s tempting to play it on the bus. Once it’s everywhere, it stops pulling you into the habit, so keep the rule even when it’s inconvenient.

Good to know. If your habit happens outside in the dark, keep one ear free for traffic and wear something easy to see.
