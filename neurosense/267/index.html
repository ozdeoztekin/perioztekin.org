Learn the Route Through Your Talk, Not Every Word

Idea Roadmaps Over Memorized Scripts

Comparing your words with a memorized script uses attention that explaining the idea also needs.

The Science

  • When a sentence leaves the script, you get two jobs: communicating, and comparing your speech with an exact internal version.
  • The problem is not preparation; it is making exact wording the measure of success when the task is getting the meaning across.
  • A roadmap keeps the logical links between ideas: where to start, why the next point belongs, and where the listener should end up.

The Protocol

  • Decide what the listener needs to understand or do, then build the message on three questions: What? So what? Now what?
  • Practice the route aloud in your own words, and on another run let the wording change while the facts and links stay.
  • Put any number, quotation or phrase that must be exact on a note card and read it; when wording changes, return to the next idea.

See it in real life

Example

Silent speakers and an index card

Osei is hosting the community center talent show, and at Thursday’s dress rehearsal his third introduction comes out a little different from the script. He wrote one for each of the fourteen acts and memorized every word in front of the bathroom mirror. Tonight he says “a song she wrote herself” where the script says “her own song,” stops, and starts the sentence over twice while Iona waits at the edge of the stage with her ukulele.

He knows what he wants to say about every act. But once a sentence leaves the script, he has two jobs, introducing Iona and checking his words against the exact version in his head, and the checking uses attention the introduction needs. The preparation helps him, and what trips him up is treating the exact wording as the goal when the goal is getting the meaning across. A roadmap keeps the links between ideas instead, where to start, why the next part belongs and where the audience should end up.

That night he decides what the audience needs from each introduction and builds every one on three questions, “What? So what? Now what?” The “what” is who’s up and what they’ll do, the “so what” is one reason to care, and the “now what” is the welcome. Names, song titles and the raffle numbers go on an index card, since those have to be exact. Then he says each introduction out loud twice, letting the wording change the second time.

On Friday the dance crew’s music won’t start. Osei is alone at the microphone for over a minute, so he tells the audience the five of them rehearsed in a parking garage all winter, reads the song title off his card, and when the sound booth waves, welcomes them on. None of his fourteen introductions comes out the way he wrote it, and he doesn’t stop in the middle of any of them.

  1. Where it stallsWhen one phrase changes, he stops and starts the introduction over twice.
  2. What changes itHe builds each introduction on three questions and puts the names on a card.
  3. What happens nextWhen the music fails, he fills the gap, and no introduction stalls.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

Script, then route

Say the short update below out loud, alone or to a friend. It’s the kind of thing a club or team leader might have to announce.

  1. Read the script three times, then say it once from memory.
  2. Say it from memory again, but open with “Okay, quick change” in place of “Hi, everyone,” and notice what your mind does with the next sentence.
  3. Cover the script. Using only the route and the note card, say the update in your own words, starting any way you like.
  4. Say it once more with different words, reading the times off the card.
Script“Hi, everyone. The tables we ordered won’t arrive until Saturday at noon. That means we can’t set up at nine like we planned. So setup moves to one o’clock, and the sale opens at two.”
RouteWhat? The tables come at noon. So what? No setup at nine. Now what? Setup at one, and the sale opens at two.
Note cardTables at noon · Setup at 1:00 · Sale opens at 2:00

Only the times need to be exact, so they live on the card.

What you’ll probably notice. When the first words change, many people stall a little while they hunt for the sentence that was supposed to come next. That stall is your mind comparing what you said with the exact version, and the comparison uses attention the update needs. With the route, a new opening doesn’t matter, because you’re heading for the next idea rather than the next word, and the times stay exact because you read them.

Step by step

Three questions and a note card

For any talk where the wording can vary, like a toast, an announcement or a presentation.

  1. Decide what the listener needs. Write one sentence about what they should understand or do by the end.
  2. Build the route on three questions. “What?” describes the situation, “So what?” says why it matters and “Now what?” gives the next step. The links between them keep the talk moving.
  3. Put exact things on a note card. Numbers, names, quotations and any phrase that must be word for word go on the card, and you read them from it.
  4. Practice the route out loud. Say it in your own words. On another run, let the wording change while the facts and the links stay the same.
  5. When a phrase changes, go on. During the talk, go on to the next idea rather than hunting for the lost sentence. If you blank, look at the card for the next question.

How to tell it’s working. Record two practice runs on your phone and listen to both. If the words differ while the facts and their order stay the same, you’ve learned the route rather than a script. In the talk itself, notice how often you stop to hunt for a word.

When it gets hard. The script you wrote first will keep trying to come back. When a sentence starts to wobble, finish it in any words that are true and move to the next question.

Good to know. This is a way to prepare a talk, not a treatment for anxiety or a promise that you won’t blank. If fear of speaking keeps you from things you want to do, that’s worth raising with your family or a doctor.
