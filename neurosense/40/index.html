Why Play Wires Your Brain Faster

Play & Plasticity

Play is not just a break from learning. A low-pressure, playful session may give you more attempts to learn from.

The Science

  • Pellis & Pellis (Learning & Behavior, 2017): in rats, play fighting in youth helps the prefrontal cortex develop and builds flexible social and motor skills; rats denied play as juveniles handle social situations less well later.
  • Vanderschuren, Achterberg & Trezza (Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews, 2016): in young rats, social play is rewarding and depends on the brain's opioid, endocannabinoid and dopamine systems, along with the prefrontal cortex.
  • Bjork & Bjork (2011): practice that feels harder in the moment, such as spacing it out, mixing it up and testing yourself, often leads to stronger long-term learning than easy, repetitive drilling.
  • The idea behind this episode, not yet tested directly, is that a playful, low-pressure mood leads to more attempts and more errors to learn from.

The Protocol

  • Pick ONE specific skill that usually makes you tense.
  • Set 10 minutes. Score attempts only, not correctness.
  • Add a silly or weird variation. Bonus points for ridiculous strategies.
  • Do it with a friend if you can; it makes it feel more like a game.
  • Then return to serious practice while you still feel loose.

See it in real life

Example

Granny shots before the drill

Coach Dunn runs one free throw drill, and it has one rule: anybody misses, everybody runs. Each player shoots two at the end of practice while the rest of the team leans on the baseline and watches.

In his driveway, Quentin makes eight of ten. In the drill his elbow locks, the ball comes off flat, and it’s short every time. His teammates have started groaning before he shoots. In games he’s at 41 percent from the line, and the closer the score, the stiffer he gets.

When practice feels like a test, the body gets stiff before the brain gets better. The brain rewires by comparing each attempt with what actually happened, so it needs error signals, lots of them. Under pressure, people protect their ego and repeat the safest move, which means fewer attempts and less to learn from. Play is a strange state, high alertness with low threat. People are awake and curious and trying things, and mistakes don’t feel like danger. That kind of state can produce more attempts, weirder ones and quicker corrections.

He talks his friend Malcolm into ten minutes at the park on Saturday. The only score is attempts, and ridiculous ones count double: granny shots, left-handed, eyes closed, sitting on a ball, one from behind the backboard that clangs off the rim and has them both yelling. Malcolm keeps count on his fingers, then on his phone. When the ten minutes are up, Quentin goes straight to the line for twenty serious free throws.

Sixty-three attempts. On the serious ones his elbow comes through loose, and he makes fourteen of twenty. On Tuesday he steps up ninth in the drill and makes both. The team runs anyway, because Kendrick misses his second.

  1. Where it stallsElbow locked and short every time, with the team watching
  2. What changes itTen minutes of granny shots and trick shots, attempts only
  3. What happens nextFourteen of twenty right after, then both in Tuesday’s drill

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

Serious, silly, serious

A tongue twister stands in for any skill that tightens up. Do it alone, or take turns with a friend.

  1. Serious round: say “red leather, yellow leather” five times fast and count your stumbles.
  2. Silly round, three minutes: say it every ridiculous way you can think of, as a robot, a whisper, an opera singer, a sports announcer, a sleepy pirate, backward. Tap the counter for every attempt. Mistakes don’t matter.
  3. Serious round again: five times fast, counting stumbles.
  4. Compare the two serious rounds, and look at how many attempts the silly round got.

The timer needs JavaScript. A phone stopwatch works just as well.

What you’ll probably notice. Silly attempts usually far outnumber the serious ones from both rounds put together, and the last serious round often has fewer stumbles. Nothing about the twister changed. With the threat gone, your mouth got dozens of extra tries and the errors that came with them, which is the raw material the brain learns from.

Step by step

Ten minutes of play first

  1. Pick the skill that makes you tense. A shot, a dance move, a piano run, a language sound, a game combo. The one where you tighten up when it counts.
  2. Set ten minutes, score attempts only. Every try counts. Whether it goes in, lands or sounds right doesn’t.
  3. Add silly versions. Ridiculous strategies earn bonus points: the other hand, eyes closed, the wrong stance, a version you’d never try in serious mode.
  4. Bring a friend if you can. Playing with someone drops the threat even further. Take turns inventing the next ridiculous version.
  5. Go straight into serious practice. When the ten minutes are up, practice it properly while you’re still loose.

How to tell it’s working. Count attempts in the play bout and compare them with a normal session of the same length. Then compare the serious practice that follows with your usual serious practice: makes, clean repetitions, or how loose you feel.

When it gets hard. Halfway through, play can quietly turn back into a test, like keeping track of makes. If you catch yourself counting makes, add a sillier rule.
