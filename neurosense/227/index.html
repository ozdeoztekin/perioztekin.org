It Was Obvious to You. That Was the Problem

Common Ground & Audience Design

Your mind is the model always at hand for what others know, so a label clear to you can land nowhere.

The Science

  • When you judge what a stranger knows, your own mind is the model at hand, which tilts you toward assuming they think more like you than they do.
  • Conversation runs on common ground, what both people know and take for granted they share, and speakers fit their wording to it: audience design.
  • A reply is evidence of what was understood. Only then has a piece become shared, so common ground grows one confirmed piece at a time.

The Protocol

  • When it matters, swap your label for the description behind it, such as "a friend of mine" instead of a first name.
  • Treat their reply as evidence: listen to what they say back to see what they understood.
  • Count a point as shared only once their reply confirms it, then build on it.

See it in real life

Example

The Snack Shack, next to the Dome

Ayla’s grandfather has been driving around the sports complex for twenty minutes. Her soccer tournament ended at five, her teammates’ cars have pulled out one after another, and her phone is down to nine percent. “I’m by the Snack Shack, next to the Dome,” she tells him for the third time.

To her it’s the clearest direction in the world. She has played here every spring since she was nine, and everyone on her team calls the white inflatable practice building the Dome and the hot dog stand the Snack Shack. Her grandfather has never been here before. “Which shack?” he keeps asking.

When you judge what someone else knows, the one model always at hand is your own mind, so it’s easy to assume they share more of your world than they do. Conversation runs on common ground, the things both people know and take for granted that they share, and speakers fit their words to it, which is called audience design. For someone outside that common ground, a name like the Dome lands nowhere, and the evidence of what got through is what the other person says back.

When she calls back a fourth time, she stops naming things and starts describing them. “Do you see a big white building that looks like a pillow?” He does, on his right. “Drive past it and look for a wooden hut with a red roof.” Each time he tells her what he sees, she knows that piece is shared, and only then does she give him the next one. “There’s a red roof by a fence,” he says. “That’s it. I’m on the bench next to it, in a green jacket.”

Four minutes later his car pulls up beside the bench with the window down. Her phone dies somewhere on the highway, and she spends the rest of the ride telling him about the game.

  1. Where it stalls“I’m by the Snack Shack,” she says, and he circles for twenty minutes.
  2. What changes itShe describes one landmark at a time and waits to hear what he sees.
  3. What happens nextFour minutes later, his car pulls up beside her bench.

Try it yourself · 10 minutes

Draw what I say

You need a partner, two pencils, paper and a book stood up between you, so your partner can’t see your drawing.

  1. Draw a simple picture made of five shapes, like a triangle on top of a square with a circle off to one side, and keep it hidden.
  2. For round 1, describe the whole picture in one go, in your own words, while your partner draws without saying anything.
  3. Draw a new five-shape picture. For round 2, describe one shape at a time, ask your partner to tell you what they drew, and give the next piece only when their answer matches your picture.
  4. Lift the book and compare both drawings with the originals.

What you’ll probably notice. Round 1 usually goes wrong somewhere you were sure you’d been clear, often at a word like “the house shape” that only meant something in your head. In round 2, every answer your partner gives is evidence of what got through, so mistakes get caught one piece at a time. Common ground grows the same way, with each piece confirmed before the next one goes on top.

Step by step

Describe it, then check it landed

Use it when getting it right matters, like directions, instructions or explaining something to someone new.

  1. Catch your labels. Before you explain, notice the names only your group uses, like a nickname for a place, a friend’s first name or a word from your team.
  2. Swap each label for a description. Say what the thing is, not what you call it, like “a friend of mine” for someone they’ve never met or “a wooden hut with a red roof” for the Snack Shack.
  3. Treat their reply as evidence. Listen to what they say back, or ask what they see or what they’ll do. Their answer shows what got through in a way your own feeling of being clear can’t.
  4. Build only on what’s confirmed. Count a piece as shared once their answer matches it, and then give the next one.

How to tell it’s working. Next time you give directions or explain a task, count how many times the other person’s answer doesn’t match what you meant. Compare that with a time you explained without checking, and if the misses drop, keep checking each piece.

When it gets hard. Checking can feel slow, or like you’re testing someone. Ask what they see or what they’re doing, which feels more like teamwork than a quiz.
