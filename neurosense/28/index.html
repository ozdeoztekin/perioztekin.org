Why Your Best Ideas Hit You in the Shower

The Default Mode Network

Insight doesn't only come from working harder. A break that lets your mind wander can help too.

The Science

  • Raichle et al. (Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 2001): a set of regions, including the medial prefrontal cortex, posterior cingulate and angular gyrus, is most active at rest and quiets down during focused, outward tasks; it became known as the default mode network.
  • Buckner et al. (Annals of the New York Academy of Sciences, 2008): the default network is active when people remember the past, imagine the future or think about other people's minds.
  • Christoff et al. (Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 2009): during mind wandering, both the default network and the brain's executive control regions were active, most strongly when people did not notice their minds had wandered.
  • Baird et al. (Psychological Science, 2012): after a break spent on an easy task that let minds wander, people came up with more creative uses for objects they had already seen than after a demanding task, quiet rest or no break.

The Protocol

  • 10 minutes daily. No phone, no music, no podcast.
  • Do something easy that lets your mind wander, like walking, showering or folding laundry.
  • Let your mind wander, and let boredom happen.
  • Keep paper nearby. The best ideas show up during or right after.

See it in real life

Example

Eleven jump scares and a tape recorder

Omar has eleven ideas for the last room of the haunted garage, and every one of them is a jump scare they’ve already done. This is the third Halloween that he, Rory and Petra have turned his garage into a haunted house, and this year they promised the kids a last room that’s really scary.

Halloween is on Saturday. He has been at his desk since nine in the morning, watching clips of haunted attractions, and Rory has texted three question marks and a skull.

All of Omar’s attention is pointed outward, at the list, the clips and the texts. When attention is locked on the world outside, a set of brain areas called the default mode network quiets down. When outside input drops off, that network gets more active, and it’s involved in remembering the past, picturing the future and thinking about other people. In one study, people who took a break doing something easy that let their minds wander came up with more creative ideas for a problem they’d already started on than people who rested, worked hard or took no break.

After lunch he leaves his phone on the desk, puts a pencil and an index card in his pocket, and walks to the hardware store for duct tape. It’s ten minutes each way, and he doesn’t bring earbuds or try to think about the room. Halfway there he’s remembering his grandfather’s old tape recorder, and the day it played his own six-year-old voice back to him and he didn’t recognize it.

By the time he reaches the store, he has the idea, and he writes it on the card. At the entrance, every visitor will say their name into a microphone for the guest book, and in the last room, in the dark, a whisper will say it back. On Saturday a sixth grader screams when she hears her own name, runs out and gets right back in line.

  1. Where it stallsHe has eleven ideas for the last room, and they’ve done every one.
  2. What changes itHe leaves his phone behind and walks to the hardware store for tape.
  3. What happens nextA girl screams at her own name and gets right back in line.

Try it yourself · 10 minutes

Same walk, two ways

You’ll need a five-minute route you can walk twice, like a hallway, a yard or the block, plus headphones, paper and a pen.

  1. Walk the route for five minutes with music or a podcast playing.
  2. Right after, write down everything you remember thinking about, one line each.
  3. Leave the headphones and your phone behind and walk the same route for five more minutes with nothing playing.
  4. Write down everything you thought about this time.
  5. Compare the two lists. Mark the lines that are your own memories, plans or unfinished problems, and star any idea you didn’t have before.

What you’ll probably notice. Often the list from the quiet walk is longer and more personal, with something from years ago, something coming up or a problem you hadn’t finished. With audio playing, your attention keeps getting handed the next thing to listen to. Without it, the default mode network gets more active and starts pulling your own material together, and that’s the stuff ideas that seem to come from nowhere are made of.

Step by step

Ten empty minutes a day

Work on the problem first, then give it ten minutes of quiet.

  1. Leave every input behind. Spend ten minutes a day with no phone, music or podcast. Leave the phone in a different room so it isn’t asking for a turn.
  2. Do something low-input. Walk, shower, fold laundry, shoot free throws or doodle, or anything else your hands can do without your attention.
  3. Let your mind wander. Don’t steer it back to the problem, and don’t judge what shows up. If it feels boring at first, let it be boring.
  4. Keep paper nearby. When something shows up, write it down right away, even if it’s messy, and then go back to wandering. Ideas from these minutes slip away fast.

How to tell it’s working. For a week, keep every idea from the ten minutes in one place and mark the ones you end up using. At the end of the week, count them and compare with how many usable ideas came out of your phone breaks the week before.

When it gets hard. The first few times, nothing shows up and the ten minutes feel long. That’s normal, so keep the paper anyway and wait until the end of the week to judge.
