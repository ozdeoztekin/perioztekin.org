Why Speaking Louder Can Still Leave Words Unclear

Speech in Noise & Visible Cues

Volume is only one part of what a listener needs; less background, a visible face and slower speech can help.

The Science

  • Your eardrum and middle-ear bones pass vibration into inner-ear fluid; that movement bends hair-cell sensors, setting off auditory nerve signals.
  • Your auditory system must pick one voice out of sound arriving all together, and the beginnings and endings of spoken words help that selection.
  • A louder voice can make speech more audible, but it does not remove competing sound or supply the visible cues of a face and mouth.

The Protocol

  • With someone who has difficulty hearing, first cut a competing sound: pause the television or stop speaking over running water.
  • Next, face the person, so your face and mouth can add visible speech cues.
  • Slow down and speak clearly instead of yelling, and check what works for that person rather than assuming one approach fits all.

See it in real life

Example

The news she said three times

Yuki wanted her grandfather to hear it first. She got the summer job in the botanical garden’s greenhouse, the one he used to deliver soil to. But Sunday dinner is at her grandparents’ house, with the ball game on in the corner and her grandmother rinsing pots at the sink.

She tells him anyway, and he smiles and says, “That’s nice.” She says it louder, then louder again over the announcers, until her grandmother turns off the water to say, “Don’t yell at him.” He has told the family that he can follow anyone in a quiet room but loses the thread when a television is on. Yuki says, “Never mind,” and he goes back to his plate.

Volume was only part of it. In the ear, the eardrum and small bones pass vibration into fluid, and that movement bends sensors on hair cells, setting off nerve signals that still have to be read as speech. Her voice, the announcers and the water reached him together, and his hearing had to pick one voice out of the mix, helped by the beginnings and endings of words. Shouting made her louder, but the game stayed on, and half turned toward the screen, she gave him no face to read.

After dessert she tries again. She asks her brother to pause the game, waits for the water to stop and moves her chair to face him, with the window light on her face. She asks what works best for him, and he points her a little more to his right. Then she tells him slowly, one sentence at a time, with less noise competing, her mouth in view and time for each word to land.

This time he follows all of it. “The greenhouse with the green door?” he says. “I used to back the truck up to it.” He asks when she starts and writes the date on the kitchen calendar in his square capitals.

  1. Where it stallsOver the ball game, her grandfather answers her shouted news with “That’s nice.”
  2. What changes itShe pauses the game, faces him and tells him slowly.
  3. What happens nextHe asks when she starts and writes the date on his calendar.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

One voice over another

Find a short video of one person talking to the camera, with captions you can switch on later, and keep them off for now. You also need a second sound in the room, like a radio or a TV with people talking.

  1. Turn the second sound on, and keep it and the video at the volume you’d use any day, nothing loud. Play the first twenty seconds of the video with your eyes shut, and write down every word you’re sure you heard.
  2. Play the next twenty seconds with your eyes on the speaker’s face, and note the words you caught.
  3. Turn the second sound off and play the next twenty seconds, still watching the face.
  4. Switch the captions on, go back to the start and check your three lists.

What you’ll probably notice. Your eyes-shut list often has the most gaps. Both sounds reached your ears at once, so your hearing had to pick one voice out of the mix. Watching the face adds clues from the mouth that sound alone doesn’t carry, though people use those clues to very different degrees. Switching the other sound off takes the competition away, and none of the three rounds needed anyone to speak louder.

Step by step

Make the words easier to catch

For talking with a grandparent, a neighbor or anyone who has a hard time following speech when there’s noise around.

  1. Turn one competing sound off first. Pause the TV, turn off the water or step away from the dishwasher before you start. Less background gives your voice less to compete with.
  2. Face the person. Sit or stand where they can see your face and mouth, with the light on your face rather than behind you, and keep your hands away from your mouth.
  3. Slow down instead of getting louder. Speak clearly at an easy pace, with a short pause between sentences. Yelling makes your voice bigger without taking the noise away.
  4. Ask what works for them. People use these cues differently, so ask whether they’d like you closer, on one side or slower, and then do it their way.

How to tell it’s working. During one meal, count how many times the person asks you to repeat something or answers a question you didn’t ask. At a later meal, use the four steps, count again and ask them which conversation was easier to follow.

When it gets hard. Pausing someone’s game or show can feel rude. Ask for a short pause for the part that matters, and let it go back on when you’re done.

Good to know. These steps make speech easier to follow, and they don’t treat hearing loss. If someone’s hearing seems to be changing, or they’re struggling more than they used to, encourage them to see a doctor or a hearing specialist.
