Why So Many Students Need Glasses

Nearsightedness & Time Outdoors

Screens are not the whole story: for eyes that are still growing, time in daylight outdoors may also matter.

The Science

  • Your cornea and lens focus light onto the retina, the light-sensing brain tissue at the back of your eye.
  • In nearsightedness, or myopia, the eyeball grows too long, mostly before about age 20, so light focuses in front of the retina and the image blurs.
  • In children with growing eyes, more time outdoors may slow worsening nearsightedness, perhaps through brighter light, distant focus or both.

The Protocol

  • Keep the book or phone, but move some of that time outdoors, such as reading on a shaded porch.
  • Never stare at the sun, and do not treat daylight as a cure or a replacement for glasses.
  • If the board or other distant things blur, get your eyes checked by an eye doctor.

See it in real life

Example

The numbers on the courthouse clock

Aarav’s Saturday sketch group is drawing the courthouse from the benches across the square, and everyone else is putting numbers on the clock. For Aarav the clock face is a pale circle with gray smudges where the numbers should be, so he leaves it blank and shades the steps.

That week he also moved up a row in math to read the board. His mom thinks she knows why. He draws at his desk for two hours most nights with his face close to the page, and she wants to cut that to half an hour.

What’s changing is the eyeball. The cornea and lens focus light onto the retina, the light-sensing brain tissue at the back of the eye. In nearsightedness the eyeball grows too long, mostly before about age 20, so light from far away comes into focus in front of the retina and the image blurs. Close-up work gets the blame, but it isn’t the whole story. For children whose eyes are still growing, more time outdoors may slow worsening nearsightedness, maybe through brighter light, maybe through more looking into the distance, and nobody knows the best amount. Genes matter too, and his dad has worn glasses since fourth grade.

A blurry board is a reason to get checked, so the first stop is the eye doctor. On Thursday the doctor finds he’s nearsighted and writes him a prescription. Since the drawing itself isn’t the whole story, Aarav makes his mom a different offer. He keeps the sketchbook, but the first hour after school moves to the back steps, in the shade of the maple, with his back to the sun.

Two Saturdays later he’s on the same bench in his new glasses, and the clock on his page has all twelve numbers, a little crooked at the four. Behind it in the sketchbook are eleven pages from the back steps, mostly the neighbor’s cat.

  1. Where it stallsThe clock numbers everyone else is drawing are gray smudges to him.
  2. What changes itHe gets his eyes checked and moves an hour of drawing outside.
  3. What happens nextIn his new glasses he draws all twelve numbers on the clock.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

The paper retina

Find a magnifying glass or drugstore reading glasses, a sheet of white paper and a lamp or a bright phone screen across a dim room. Do it indoors, and never point the lens at the sun or look at the sun through it.

  1. Stand across the room from the lamp, hold the paper up facing it and hold the lens between them, close to the paper.
  2. Move the lens slowly toward the lamp until a small, sharp, upside-down picture of the lamp appears on the paper. The lens is playing your cornea and lens, and the paper is your retina.
  3. Keep the lens still and move the paper a few inches farther back, the way an eyeball that has grown too long would sit, and watch the picture blur.
  4. Leave the paper there and have someone bring a phone with a bright screen toward the lens until the screen comes into focus.

What you’ll probably notice. The picture is sharp only when the paper sits at the right distance, which is the job your cornea and lens do for the retina. Move the paper back and the faraway lamp blurs, the way it does in an eyeball that has grown too long, while something close can still land sharp. This is the pattern of nearsightedness, where the board goes blurry long before the book does.

Step by step

Keep the sketchbook, add daylight

It fits whatever close-up thing you love, like reading, drawing, gaming or your phone.

  1. Get distance blur checked. If the board, signs or faces across a room look blurry, let a parent know and book an eye exam.
  2. Keep what you love doing. You don’t have to give up the book, the sketchbook or the phone to make room for daylight.
  3. Move some of it outdoors. Take part of that time outside, like reading on a shaded porch, drawing on the back steps or messaging from a park bench.
  4. Wear what the doctor gives you. Daylight isn’t a cure or a replacement for glasses, so wear them the way the eye doctor says.
  5. Sit in the shade, back to the sun. You don’t need direct sunlight for this, and you should never stare at the sun, even for a moment.

How to tell it’s working. Keep a simple log of how much of your reading, drawing or phone time happens outdoors each week, and compare one week with the next. Nobody knows the best amount, and your eye checkups, not the log, are how you’ll learn how your eyes are doing.

When it gets hard. Outside comes with glare on the page and bugs on the steps. Find real shade, turn your back to the sun and keep one spot ready, like a chair on the porch, so heading out takes no planning.

Good to know. Time outdoors is no guarantee, since genes matter too, and it can’t stand in for eye care. Whenever the board or other distant things blur, an eye exam is the next step, so mention it to a parent.
