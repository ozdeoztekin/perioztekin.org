Put Your Phone Across the Room

phone presence and attention

A phone can drain your focus even when you are proudly not touching it.

The Science

  • Researchers have proposed that your own phone uses up some of your limited mental capacity, even when you leave it alone.
  • In a 2017 study, college students with their phone on the desk scored lower on a working memory test than those with it in another room.
  • A 2022 study that repeated part of that work found no such effect, so the evidence is mixed, but moving the phone away is easy to try.

The Protocol

  • Put the phone somewhere that makes you stand up to reach it.
  • Decide your next check time before the work block begins.
  • Keep only the tool you need within arm's reach.

See it in real life

Example

The repeat sign on page two

Kamila’s cousin Lidia gets married on Saturday, and Kamila is playing the piano piece the bridesmaids walk in to. Since dinner she has started it from the top seven times, and all seven times she has lost her place in the same spot on page two, where the left hand changes pattern and the first ending loops back.

Her phone lies face down on the piano lid, on silent. She hasn’t picked it up in forty minutes, and she’s been keeping track. The cousins’ group chat is planning hair appointments and rides for Saturday, and Lidia could ask her something any minute.

Even face down and silent, the phone is a door to the whole weekend. Part of her attention stays posted at that door, either resisting it or wondering what’s behind it, and that part may come out of the same limited working memory page two needs for the new left-hand pattern, the repeat sign and which ending she’s on. Moving the phone farther away may free up some of that attention, though studies testing this have had mixed results.

Before the eighth try, she moves it to the kitchen charger down the hall, where getting it means standing up and walking. On a piece of masking tape she writes her next check time, quarter past nine, and sticks it to the music stand. On the piano she leaves the music, a pencil and the metronome and nothing else.

She circles the first ending in pencil, plays page two twice at a slow tempo and then starts from the top. This time she takes the repeat, lands the second ending and keeps going to the last chord, and by quarter past nine she has played it through three times without stopping. The phone holds twenty-six messages about hair and one from Lidia, asking if Kamila can play it a little slower so the flower girl can keep up.

  1. Where it stallsWith her phone face down on the piano, she gets lost on page two seven times.
  2. What changes itShe moves the phone down the hall and writes her next check time on tape.
  3. What happens nextShe plays it through three times without stopping, repeat and all.

Try it yourself · 10 minutes

Count the phone thoughts

Do this during real work today, anything that takes thinking, like practice problems, a puzzle or a page to memorize. You’ll need your phone, a scrap of paper and a pen.

  1. Put your phone face down on the table beside you, on silent, and start working.
  2. Make a tally mark every time your eyes or your thoughts go to the phone, even for a second and even if you don’t touch it.
  3. After ten minutes, count the marks.
  4. Next to the count, write what most of them were about, like a message you’re waiting on, plans being made or checking whether it lit up.

What you’ll probably notice. Even silent and face down the whole time, the phone may draw more marks than you expected. Each one is a small piece of attention spent keeping track of it, taken from the same limited supply your work needs. If you want a comparison, do the next ten minutes with the phone in another room and count again.

Step by step

Phone across the room

Use it for any block of work that takes real thinking.

  1. Make it a stand-up trip. Before you start, put the phone where you’d have to stand up and walk to reach it, like another room, the kitchen counter or a bag by the door. Face down beside you still counts as beside you.
  2. Decide the next check time first. Pick it before the block begins and write it where you’ll see it, on a sticky note or a piece of tape, so the question of when to check is already settled.
  3. Keep only the tool you need. Keep the book, the calculator, the music and a pencil, and take everything else off the table.
  4. Open only one tool if you need the phone. If the phone is your calculator or your timer, open that one thing, close everything else and silence notifications for the block.

How to tell it’s working. Do one block with the phone face down beside you and one with it across the room, on the same kind of task at the same time of day. Count what you finish in each, and the tally marks you make.

When it gets hard. The first few times, you might get up to check what time it is. Put a watch or a clock where you can see it, so the phone has nothing left to offer.
