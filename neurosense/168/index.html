Put Your Phone Across the Room

phone presence and attention

A phone can drain your focus even when you are proudly not touching it.

The Science

  • Your nervous system still tracks the phone as a nearby portal, waiting and socially loaded.
  • That tracked possibility occupies part of your limited working memory and attention.
  • Moving the phone farther away makes the cue less available, so less attention stays tied to it.

The Protocol

  • Put the phone somewhere that makes you stand up to reach it.
  • Decide your next check time before the work block begins.
  • Keep only the tool you need within arm's reach.

See it in real life

Example

Two inches all the way around

The green dress Kamila borrowed from her mom is two inches too long, and her cousin Lidia gets married on Saturday. Kamila said she’d hem it herself. Since dinner she has pinned it three times, and three times the mirror has shown the same thing: the left side hanging low.

Her phone lies face down by the pincushion, on silent. She hasn’t picked it up in forty minutes, and she’s been keeping track. The cousins’ chat is planning hair appointments and rides for Saturday, and Lidia could ask her something any minute.

Even like that, the phone is a door to the whole weekend. Part of her attention stays posted at that door, ready to resist it or wonder what’s behind it, and that part comes out of the same limited working memory a hem needs: the measurement, the seam she started from, which pins she has checked.

Before the fourth try, she moves it. It goes on the kitchen charger, down the hall, where getting it means standing up and walking. On a piece of masking tape she writes “9:15,” her next check, and sticks it to the sewing machine. Next to the machine she leaves the dress, the pincushion, chalk and a seam gauge set to two inches. Nothing else.

She chalks an X at the left side seam and pins once around, a pin every two inches, back to the X. In the mirror the hem hangs level, and her mom’s yardstick agrees: sixteen inches from the floor all the way around. At 9:15 the hem is pressed and half stitched. The phone holds twenty-six messages about hair and one from Lidia, asking if Kamila can bring safety pins.

  2. What changes itPhone down the hall, 9:15 on tape, four tools by the machine
  3. What happens nextPinned once around, level in the mirror, sixteen inches everywhere

Try it yourself · 10 minutes

Count the phone thoughts

Do this during real work today, anything that takes thinking: practice problems, a puzzle, a page to memorize. You need your phone, a scrap of paper and a pen.

  1. Put your phone face down on the table beside you, on silent, and start working.
  2. Make a tally mark every time your eyes or your thoughts go to the phone, even for a second, even if you don’t touch it.
  3. After ten minutes, count the marks.
  4. Next to the count, write what most of them were about: a message you’re waiting on, plans being made, just checking whether it lit up.

What you’ll probably notice. Even silent and face down the whole time, the phone may draw more marks than you expected. Each one is a small piece of attention spent keeping track of it, drawn from the same limited supply your work needs. If you want a comparison, do the next ten minutes with the phone in another room and count again.

Step by step

Phone across the room

For any block of work that takes real thinking.

  1. Make it a stand-up trip. Before you start, put the phone where you’d have to stand up and walk to reach it: another room, the kitchen counter, a bag by the door. Face down beside you still counts as beside you.
  2. Decide the next check time first. Pick it before the block begins, like 9:15, and write it where you’ll see it, on a sticky note or a piece of tape. Then the question of when to check is already settled.
  3. Keep only the tool you need. The book, the calculator, the instrument, the pins and the chalk. Everything else goes off the table.
  4. Need the phone? Open only that. If the phone is your calculator or your timer, open that one thing, close everything else, and silence notifications for the block.

How to tell it’s working. Do one block with the phone face down beside you and one with it across the room, same kind of task, same time of day. Count what you finish in each, and the tally marks you make.

When it gets hard. The first few times, you might get up to check “just the time.” Put a watch or a clock where you can see it, so the phone has nothing left to offer.
