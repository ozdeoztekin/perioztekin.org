Try Giving Yourself Advice by Name

Distanced Self-Talk & Perspective

Most of us give advice better than we take it. Talking to yourself by name can put you in the advisor's seat.

The Science

  • Distanced self-talk means thinking through your own problem with your name and the word 'you', the way you would talk to someone you are advising.
  • Words like 'you' are the verbal version of pointing a finger at someone else, so using them on yourself can shift you into an advisor's position.
  • That shift can give you some distance from the strong feelings around a problem, so you can consider it a little more calmly and objectively.

The Protocol

  • When you are stuck on a problem, say your own name and ask: how are you going to handle this?
  • After an argument, ask yourself by name what you would tell a friend to do next, then answer as you would for that friend.
  • Use that outside angle to look for where you might have been wrong, while you keep facing the problem itself.

See it in real life

Example

Seven dogs, split down the middle

Since June, Kalani and her friend Merritt have walked seven dogs on Linden Street and split every dollar down the middle. Tonight at Merritt’s house, Kalani said that wasn’t fair anymore, since she does most of the walking. Merritt said Kalani had no idea how much texting goes into every walk, said “Then run it yourself,” and Kalani went home.

An hour later she’s on her bed, writing the third version of a text to Merritt, each one longer than the last. Three dogs need walking at seven tomorrow, and the notebook with every owner’s schedule is at Merritt’s house.

Kalani would know what to tell a friend in this spot. People usually give better advice than they take, and one way around that gap is distanced self-talk. Words like “you” are the verbal version of pointing at someone else, so using your own name and “you” on yourself can move you into the advisor’s seat. That can give you a little distance from the strong feelings while you keep facing the problem.

She puts the phone face down and asks the ceiling, “Kalani, how are you going to handle this?” Then she asks the harder question, “What would you tell a friend to do next?” The answer comes out in the voice she uses on other people. “You wouldn’t send that tonight. You’d go over before the walks and bring your count. And you’d look for where you might be wrong.” That part stings, because the week Kalani spent at her aunt’s in July, Merritt walked all seven dogs alone.

She deletes the draft and sends one line, “Can we talk at 6:45, before the walks?” On Merritt’s porch the next morning they set the walk counts and the owner texts side by side and write a new rule, two dollars of every walk to whoever handles the owners. Then they go get the three dogs together.

  1. Where it stallsAn hour after the fight, she’s on her third draft of a long text.
  2. What changes itShe asks herself, by name, what she would tell a friend to do.
  3. What happens nextShe deletes the draft, and they write a fairer split before the walks.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

Your name, then you

Pick a small problem you’re stuck on, or a recent disagreement that still bugs you, rather than the biggest thing in your life. The back of an envelope or your notes app is plenty.

  1. Write two or three sentences about it the way it usually sounds in your head, with “I” and “me.”
  2. Under that, write your own name and “how are you going to handle this?” Answer in writing, using your name and “you” the whole way, as if you were advising a friend.
  3. Ask yourself by name what you would tell a friend to do next, and answer that in the same voice.
  4. Add one sentence about where you might have been wrong.
  5. Read the two notes side by side and circle every next step you could take.

What you’ll probably notice. The first note usually sounds like the problem talking, with plenty of feeling and not many next steps. The second often reads more like advice, a little calmer and more practical, and it may notice your own part where the first one didn’t. Your name and “you” can put you in the advisor’s seat while you keep looking at the problem. It doesn’t work the same way for everyone, and the advice can still be wrong, so treat it as a draft you can check.

Step by step

Coach yourself by name

Keep it for the next argument you can’t stop replaying, or a problem you keep circling.

  1. Catch yourself stewing. Replaying what was said, drafting messages you don’t send and going around the same problem again are all signs. Any one of them is your cue.
  2. Ask by name how you’ll handle it. Say your own name and ask, “How are you going to handle this?” You can say it out loud, write it down or say it in your head.
  3. Answer the way you’d advise a friend. After an argument, ask yourself by name what you would tell a friend to do next. Then answer with the same practical care you’d give that friend.
  4. Look for where you might be wrong. From the advisor’s seat, ask which part of this might be yours. One honest sentence is enough.
  5. Take the next step, still facing it. Pick one thing to do within a day, like a short message or a talk in person. The step back is for seeing the problem better, not for getting away from it.

How to tell it’s working. For your next few arguments or stuck problems, write down whether you took a next step within a day and whether you sent anything you later regretted. Compare the times you coached yourself by name with the times you didn’t.

When it gets hard. Talking to yourself by name can feel silly at first. Do it in writing or under your breath, and if the advice comes out harsh, ask whether you’d say it that way to a friend.
