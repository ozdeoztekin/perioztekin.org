Coldness Is Not Proof of Competence

Warmth & Competence Judgments

A cold manner can look like expertise, but warmth and competence are two different judgments.

The Science

  • Warmth is how caring or friendly someone seems; competence is how capable they seem of accomplishing a task. They are different judgments.
  • People can treat the two as opposites, so warmth becomes a reason to doubt ability and coldness starts to look like evidence of expertise.
  • That expectation can shape self-presentation too: trying to look highly competent can lead people to act less warmly.

The Protocol

  • Name the specific thing the person needs to do, such as checking a shared calculation or explaining it clearly to the group.
  • Inspect relevant work from each person using that same question, instead of filling the gap with their manner.
  • Consider warmth separately, by how the person treats others, and leave room for someone to be both warm and capable.

See it in real life

Example

The shipping cost nobody wrote down

When judges at the regional pitch competition ask about money, Carys’s team has always let Elspeth answer. Their business club sells tote bags printed with the old train bridge, and Elspeth built the cost sheet in one afternoon. She uses as few words as possible, sighs at repeated questions and sounds as if she has never been wrong.

Fabian jokes through every meeting and draws cartoon totes on the whiteboard. On Tuesday he said, laughing, that he thought the sheet left out the shipping on the blank bags. Nobody wrote it down, and Carys, the team captain, didn’t either.

Warmth is how caring or friendly someone seems, and competence is how capable they seem of getting a task done. People can treat the two as opposites, so friendliness becomes a reason to doubt someone’s math and coldness starts to look like expertise. Elspeth’s sigh shows how she talks, not whether her sheet is right. Wanting to look capable can make people act colder, too, and since becoming captain, Carys has noticed her own answers getting shorter.

On Wednesday she stops letting manner answer the question. She writes down what the money question needs, a sheet that’s right line by line and an answer a judge can follow in under a minute. Then she gives Elspeth and Fabian the same printout and the same job, to mark anything wrong and explain what one bag costs as if she were a judge. How each of them treats the team matters too, but as a separate question.

Elspeth catches a formula that counts the ink twice, and her explanation is right but so fast that Carys loses it halfway. Fabian finds the missing shipping, about a dollar a bag, and explains the cost in four plain sentences. On Saturday Elspeth checks the final numbers, and when a judge asks what a bag costs to make, Fabian answers. The judge writes something down and moves on to marketing.

  1. Where it stallsThe team trusts Elspeth’s sighs and laughs off Fabian’s point about shipping.
  2. What changes itCarys names the job and gives both of them the same sheet to check.
  3. What happens nextBoth catch real mistakes, and Fabian answers the judge’s cost question.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

Trust first, check second

Below are two made-up group chat questions, each with two replies. Have something to write on, and hold off on any math until the second step.

  1. Read both exchanges, and for each one, mark the reply you’d trust more on first impression.
  2. Now work out each answer yourself, or check the arithmetic in every reply.
  3. Put your first marks next to what the math shows.
  4. If a friend is around, read them the replies in the tone they’re written in, and ask which they’d trust before they do any math.
Exchange 1Four friends split a $54 pizza order plus a $6 delivery fee. How much does each person owe? Reply A: “13.50. Obviously.” Reply B: “Ooh, I think it’s 15 each? 54 plus 6 is 60, and 60 split four ways is 15, unless I’m missing something.”
Exchange 2A bus pass costs $22 a month, and a single ride costs $3. What’s the smallest number of rides in a month that makes the pass the cheaper choice? Reply C: “8. Not hard.” Reply D: “Hmm, maybe 7? Seven rides is 21 dollars, so I think 7, but someone check me.”

Both chats are invented, so check every number yourself.

What you’ll probably notice. The flat, certain replies can feel more trustworthy, and the friendly ones can sound unsure of their own math. Yet the blunt reply is wrong in the first exchange and right in the second, and the friendly reply is right in the first and wrong in the second, so tone was no guide in either direction. A sharp tone tells you how someone typed, and only the arithmetic tells you whether they’re right.

Step by step

Check the work, not the tone

This won’t make your judgment perfect, but it gives it a clearer target the next time you choose someone for part of a group task.

  1. Name the job first. Before you choose, write down what the person needs to be able to do, like checking a shared calculation or explaining it so the group can follow. Those can be two different jobs.
  2. Give everyone the same question. Hand each person the same piece of the task, like the same sheet to check or the same minute to explain, so you’re comparing like with like.
  3. Look at the work itself. Judge what each person produced against the job you named. A sharp reply shows how someone spoke, and it doesn’t show that their numbers are right.
  4. Judge warmth on its own. Separately, notice how each person treats others. That can matter for working together, but it measures something different from getting the task right.
  5. Leave room for both, and for gaps. Someone can be friendly and skilled, or blunt and skilled, and either kind of person can have weak spots. One piece of work shows one skill, so stay open to more evidence.

How to tell it’s working. Before you look at anyone’s work, write down who you’d pick from their manner alone. After you’ve checked the work, compare the two, and over a few choices notice how often the first pick held up.

When it gets hard. When time is short, the confident answer can feel like enough. If you can’t check everything, check one line or one example from each person.
