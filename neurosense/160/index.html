Why Being Kind to Yourself Works Better

Self Compassion and Growth

After a bad attempt, your brain can confuse being accountable with attacking yourself.

The Science

  • Self compassion is not pretending the miss did not matter.
  • It creates enough safety to look directly at what happened.
  • When your whole self is not on trial, the useful feedback is easier to use.

The Protocol

  • Say what happened in plain words, without an identity label.
  • Name one thing you can change on the next attempt.
  • Take that attempt before shame hardens into a story about you.

See it in real life

Example

Before they resurface the ice

The double axel opens Marley’s free skate, and in the last run-through before tomorrow’s competition she comes down on her hip. She gets up and finishes the program, every jump after it a little small and careful, then skates straight off the ice.

In the locker room she gets one skate unlaced and stops. The same sentences keep looping: “You always fall when it counts. You’re a choker. Pull out before you embarrass yourself.” Her text to Corinne, her coach, asking to withdraw, is half typed.

That voice sounds like accountability, but it’s noisy feedback: it describes her and leaves out the jump. Being kind to herself wouldn’t mean deciding the fall didn’t matter. It would make it safe enough to look straight at what happened on that takeoff, and when her whole self isn’t on trial, the useful feedback is easier to use.

She deletes the text. Out loud, to the empty locker room, she says what happened: “Fell on the double axel. Came in fast and took off before I was on the edge.” Then she asks what Corinne would tell her to fix. One thing: wait on the edge. She writes WAIT on a strip of tape, sticks it on her water bottle, laces up and finds Corinne at the boards. “I rushed it. Can I try it again before they resurface?”

Corinne checks the clock: nine minutes. On the first try Marley waits on the edge and two-foots the landing. The second one lands clean, on one foot, and she holds the landing edge all the way to the boards. Corinne taps the tape on the bottle. “That one, tomorrow.”

  2. What changes itSays what happened, writes WAIT on tape, asks to try again
  3. What happens nextTwo-footed once, then a clean double axel before the ice is resurfaced

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

Two notes

Think of a recent attempt that went badly: a game, a test, a performance, something you said. You need paper and a pen.

  1. Fold the page in half. On the left, write what you said to yourself afterward, word for word, even if it’s harsh.
  2. On the right, write what you’d text a friend who did exactly the same thing.
  3. Underline every part of each note that tells you what to do differently next time.
  4. Count the underlines on each side, and notice which note you could actually use tomorrow.

What you’ll probably notice. The left side is often louder and has fewer underlines, because insults describe you and leave out the next move. The right side can be just as honest about the miss while being more specific about the fix. That’s The Science above: when your whole self isn’t on trial, the useful feedback is easier to see and to use.

Step by step

Own it, fix one thing, go again

For the next time a game, a test, a performance or a conversation goes badly.

  1. Say what happened in plain words. Leave out the label: “fell on the axel, came in too fast,” not “a choker.” What happened, where and when.
  2. Ask what would actually help. If your mind gets cruel, ask what instruction would help the next attempt, the kind a good coach would give. Write that down instead.
  3. Name one change. One thing for the next attempt: a cue word on a strip of tape, a slower first step, a check before you start.
  4. Tell whoever needs to know. If the miss affected someone, or someone can help you fix it, like a coach or a teammate, say it plainly: “I rushed it. Can I try it again?” One sentence is enough.
  5. Take the next attempt soon. Try again the same day if you can, before shame hardens into a story about who you are.

How to tell it’s working. After a bad attempt, write down the sentence you said to yourself and the one change you chose. Over a few weeks, count how many misses got a change and a next attempt within a day.

When it gets hard. Kinder words can feel like letting yourself off the hook. Check whether the sentence still names exactly what went wrong. If it does, you’re still being accountable, just without the insult.

Good to know. If the voice in your head is harsh most days, or a bad attempt leaves you feeling low for a long time, talk to someone you trust, like a parent, a coach or a school counselor.
