What Your Brain Does With a Mistake

Error Signals and Learning

A mistake can feel like a final grade on who you are, but your brain uses it very differently.

The Science

  • Your error systems flag the gap between what you meant to do and what happened.
  • Feedback tends to work best when it points to the next move instead of attacking you.
  • When the replay gets specific, your brain has a cleaner target to adjust toward.

The Protocol

  • Name the exact miss in plain words, without an identity label.
  • Choose one repair move that is smaller than the full task.
  • Repeat the corrected move once before you leave the practice.

See it in real life

Example

A line on the left side

The line runs along the left side of Dre’s head, a hard little shelf right where the short hair is supposed to fade into the longer hair above it. Dre turns his head in Silas’s bathroom mirror and says, “Bro.” School pictures are tomorrow morning.

Silas has been cutting his friends’ hair since spring, five dollars a cut, with clippers he bought with his birthday money. This is the third fade this month to end with a line, and right after each one he does the same thing. He mutters that he’s terrible at this, snaps on a shorter guard and goes over the whole side again, which is how last month’s cut ended up nearly shaved.

His brain has already flagged the gap between the fade he meant to cut and the line in the mirror. That flag is information, and right now, with the evidence in front of him, it’s easy to read. Calling himself terrible doesn’t point anywhere. Feedback tends to work best when it points to the next move, and a specific replay gives his hands a clearer target to adjust toward.

This time he sets the clippers down for one breath and looks. Then he says it in plain words, “Went straight up past where the short part stops, and didn’t flick out.” He picks one repair smaller than the whole cut, short strokes with a longer guard on the left side only, flicking out right at the line. When the left side blends, he does the same stroke once on the right, where a fainter line has started, before he puts the clippers away.

The shelf is gone. Dre checks both sides, takes a picture and sends it to the group chat with “five dollars, book him.” By bedtime two people have asked about Saturday.

  1. Where it stallsHis third fade this month ends with a line, and picture day is tomorrow.
  2. What changes itHe names the exact miss and uses short strokes that flick out at the line.
  3. What happens nextThe line is gone, the photo goes to the group chat and two people book.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

Name the miss

You’ll need a sheet of scrap paper crumpled into a tight ball, a wastebasket or a box and about three big steps of floor.

  1. Stand three big steps from the basket and throw with the hand you don’t write with.
  2. In round 1, throw ten times, and after each miss, say “miss” and throw again. Count your hits.
  3. In round 2, throw ten more. After each miss, say exactly what happened, like “a hand short and to the left,” and one fix, like “more arc,” and then throw.
  4. Compare the two counts, and think about what your head was doing between throws in each round.

What you’ll probably notice. Hits often go up in round 2, with misses that bunch closer together instead of scattering. A specific replay helps because “short and left” gives your aim a target and “miss” gives it very little. Round 2 also came after ten practice throws, so switch the order another day and see whether the difference holds.

Step by step

Turn the miss into an instruction

It fits any practice where you get another try, like a sport or an instrument.

  1. Take one breath before you reset. When a miss happens, wait one breath before you scrap it, start over or walk off, and look at what’s in front of you while it’s still there.
  2. Name the exact miss. Say it in plain words, with no label about yourself, like “toss drifted too far back” instead of “useless,” including where, when and how much.
  3. Pick a repair smaller than the task. Choose one stroke, one toss, one bar or one turn, something you can do in under a minute.
  4. Do it once before you stop. Repeat the corrected move one time, cleanly, before the practice ends, even if you’re tired.
  5. Write the instruction down. Put one line in your phone or on a strip of tape on your gear, like “flick out at the line,” so the next practice starts from the fix.

How to tell it’s working. For two weeks, keep a short list of each miss you named, the repair you chose and whether you did the corrected move before you stopped. Compare how often the same miss shows up in the second week and the first.

When it gets hard. Right after a miss, embarrassment is loud, especially if someone saw it. Wait one breath, and then say the instruction instead of the insult, even if it’s under your breath.
