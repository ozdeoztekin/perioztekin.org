The Advice You Give Can Help You Start

Advice Giving & Self-Efficacy

Sometimes what is missing is confidence, not another tip; giving advice can show you what you already know.

The Science

  • When you are struggling, the useful knowledge is sometimes already there; what is missing is confidence that you can use it.
  • Advising someone with a similar challenge turns what you understand into a recommendation: evidence that you have something to contribute.
  • Giving advice cannot create expertise you lack, but it can help you recognize knowledge that low confidence kept you from using.

The Protocol

  • Pick a challenge you genuinely know, then advise someone facing something similar, or write a short reply as if they had asked.
  • Give honest, specific advice, such as telling a friend stuck on a presentation to pick one main point and an example.
  • Ask whether that advice fits your own task, and if it does, use it on your next attempt instead of only collecting more tips.

See it in real life

Example

What he told Colette at the piano

For three weeks Ignacio has been collecting tips. His grandmother turns eighty soon, and his mom wants him to play the old bolero she hums at the stove, but the only version he can find is a melody with chord letters above it in a songbook. He has saved fourteen videos on arranging and asked his teacher for tips twice, and he hasn’t played a note.

He’s had lessons for six years and knows his chords, but he doesn’t feel like someone who can put a song together, so each new tip seems like the one he needs before he can start.

On Wednesday Colette from next door knocks with her lesson book. She’s had piano for two months and wants to play “Happy Birthday” for her dad with both hands, but her book only shows the tune with letters above it. Ignacio tells her to play the tune with her right hand and, with her left, the note each letter names, whenever a new letter appears. Twenty minutes later she plays it slowly, start to finish.

Sometimes the useful knowledge is already there, and what’s missing is confidence that you can use it. Advising someone with a similar problem turns what you understand into a recommendation, evidence that you have something to contribute. That sense that you can act is called self-efficacy, and it can support motivation. Advice can’t create knowledge he doesn’t have, but after Colette leaves, he asks whether his advice fits his own song, and it does, since his songbook gives him the same thing.

That night he closes the videos and plays the bolero the way he told Colette to. It’s plain, but it’s the whole song. One spot sounds wrong, a chord written with a slash, so at Thursday’s lesson he asks his teacher about that chord and nothing else. On Sunday he plays it through for his mom, and by the second verse she’s singing along.

  1. Where it stallsHe saves fourteen videos on arranging and plays none of the song.
  2. What changes itHis advice to the beginner next door fits his own song.
  3. What happens nextHe plays the whole bolero and asks about the one chord he doesn’t know.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

Answer the beginner first

Think of something you’re stuck on that you know a fair amount about, like a sport, an instrument or a kind of assignment. Grab something to write on.

  1. Before anything else, write down a number from 0 to 10 for how sure you are that you can make progress on it.
  2. Think of a beginner stuck on something similar, and write them a short reply with one specific suggestion and why it fits.
  3. Keep it honest and small. One thing you know works is enough.
  4. Read your reply as if it were written to you, ask whether it fits your own task, and write down a new number for how sure you feel.

What you’ll probably notice. Often the reply holds something you could use yourself, and the second number comes out a little higher. Turning what you understand into a recommendation is evidence that you have something to contribute, and that sense that you can act, called self-efficacy, can support motivation. If the reply came out empty, that’s worth knowing too, since advice can’t create knowledge you don’t have, and that part needs learning or asking.

Step by step

Give the advice, then take it

For a task you know something about but keep stalling on while you collect more tips.

  1. Pick a challenge you know. Choose one where you have real experience, like a sport, an instrument or a kind of assignment, so your advice comes from something you’ve done.
  2. Advise someone facing something similar. Help a beginner, a friend or a younger student, or write a short reply as if they had asked you.
  3. Make it honest and specific. Give one suggestion and say why it fits, like telling a friend stuck on a presentation to choose one main point and an example. A small contribution counts.
  4. Ask whether it fits your own task. Read your advice as if it were meant for you. If it fits, use it on your next attempt rather than looking for another tip.
  5. Check the result and adjust. Feeling sure doesn’t make an answer right, so look at what happened. Ask for guidance on any part you don’t know.

How to tell it’s working. Count your attempts on the stalled task in the week before and the week after you give the advice. If the attempts go up and the result holds when you check it, keep using what you already knew.

When it gets hard. If your advice comes out vague, or you find you don’t know enough to give any, that’s worth knowing. Pick a part you do know, and ask someone about the part you don’t.
