Why a Cliffhanger Is Physically Hard to Ignore

Information Hunger

You cannot stop watching the mystery box reveal because an open question feels almost like an empty stomach. Curiosity is your brain detecting a gap and pushing you to close it.

The Science

  • Loewenstein (1994), Psychological Bulletin: the information gap theory frames curiosity as the felt distance between what you know and what you want to know.
  • Kang et al. (2009), Psychological Science: high curiosity activates reward related regions including the caudate, treating information itself like a reward.
  • Gruber, Gelman and Ranganath (2014), Neuron: curiosity ramps up a dopaminergic circuit and the hippocampus, improving memory for the answer and even unrelated nearby information.
  • Net effect: a gap your brain believes it can close is motivating, and the seeking it triggers makes the answer stick.

The Protocol

  • Before reading, turn each heading into a mystery question so your brain wants the answer.
  • Write the question down so the gap feels real, then predict the answer before you look.
  • Read to close the loop, not just to consume.
  • Make distraction one step harder while you are hunting, then quiz yourself later.

See it in real life

Example

One question before the first video

The camera is two years old, with a scratch on the screen and 40,000 shutter clicks, and the seller is holding it for Giselle until Friday at noon. It costs $260 of the $280 she made babysitting this summer, so she wants to be sure.

Three nights in a row she has sat down to research it. One review leads to a teardown of a different camera, then to a video about film someone found at a thrift store, then to a feud between two photographers she’d never heard of. By Wednesday she still can’t answer the one question that matters, which is whether 40,000 clicks is a lot.

Each video opened a gap between what she knew and what she wanted to know, and the brain treats closing that kind of gap like a reward. In brain scans, curiosity turns up activity in reward areas before the answer even shows up, and it brings in the hippocampus, which helps the answer stick. The catch is that each video closes one gap by opening another. The same pull can work for her if she picks the question herself.

On Thursday, before opening anything, she writes one question on a sticky note, “Is 40,000 clicks too many for this model?” Under it she writes what done looks like, an answer in one sentence, and her guess, which is that it’s too many. She turns off autoplay, and when a video called “The Mystery of the Banned Lens” shows up in the sidebar, she adds it to a list labeled “later” and keeps going.

Fourteen minutes later she has her sentence. That model’s shutter is rated for about 150,000 clicks, so 40,000 leaves plenty, and her guess was wrong. She closes the tab and texts the seller, “Still on for Friday.” On Friday she explains the shutter math to him without looking anything up.

  1. Where it stallsAfter three nights of rabbit holes, she still can’t answer the one question.
  2. What changes itShe writes the question down, makes a guess and parks everything else.
  3. What happens nextShe has her answer in fourteen minutes and texts the seller.

Try it yourself · 10 minutes

Question first, or just read

You’ll need something with headings that you have to read anyway, like a chapter, a manual, a rulebook or a long article, plus paper and a pen.

  1. Read the first section the way you usually would.
  2. Before the second section, turn its heading into a question you can’t answer yet and write it down. “Battery care” might become “What wears a battery out?”
  3. Write a one-line guess, then read to close the loop, and stop when you can answer in one sentence.
  4. Close the page and explain each section out loud in one sentence, noticing which one comes out more easily.

What you’ll probably notice. The question-first section usually feels quicker to read and comes back more easily, because you were hunting for something instead of scanning. Once your brain spots a question it thinks it can answer, the answer starts to feel worth chasing, and memory tends to work better when you’re curious. It’s only one round, so try the order the other way on another day.

Step by step

Question before content

Do it before you scroll, research, read or practice.

  1. Write one question first. Before you open anything, write down the one thing you want to know. For reading, turn each heading into a question you can’t answer yet.
  2. Set the finish line. Decide what done looks like, like being able to say the answer in one sentence or to choose one next action.
  3. Guess before you look. Write a quick prediction. Being wrong is fine.
  4. Park new mysteries on a later list. Turn off autoplay before you start. When something new and interesting pops up, write it on the list and stay on your question.
  5. Close the loop, then quiz yourself. When you reach the finish line, close the tab or switch tasks. The next day, say the answer again without looking.

How to tell it’s working. Count how many sessions this week ended at your finish line instead of somewhere else, and how many answers you could still say the next day. Compare that with a few sessions done your usual way.

When it gets hard. The later list will look more interesting than your question almost every time, because it’s full of open gaps. Give it a set time, like Sunday afternoon, so it has somewhere to go.
