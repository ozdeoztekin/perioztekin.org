All-Nighters Poison Your Brain

The Glymphatic System

Sleep isn't downtime. In mice, sleep speeds up the brain's waste clearance, and in people, one night without sleep left a little more waste protein behind.

The Science

  • Xie et al. (Science, 2013): in mice, the space between brain cells grew by about 60% during sleep, letting cerebrospinal fluid flow through and clear waste faster, including beta-amyloid, the protein linked to Alzheimer's disease.
  • Iliff et al. (Science Translational Medicine, 2012): in mice, cerebrospinal fluid flows into the brain along the outside of blood vessels and carries waste out, a route the team named the glymphatic system; Xie and colleagues then showed it runs much faster during sleep.
  • Shokri-Kojori et al. (Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 2018): after one night without sleep, PET scans of healthy adults showed a small rise, about 5%, in beta-amyloid in the hippocampus and thalamus.
  • Walker & Stickgold (Neuron, 2004): sleep after learning strengthens many kinds of memory, from facts to motor skills, so an all-nighter costs both clearance and consolidation.

The Protocol

  • Cut caffeine 8-10 hours before sleep. It blocks the adenosine pressure your brain needs.
  • Keep the bedroom cool, around 65°F (18°C); body temperature naturally dips at night, and a cool room makes sleep easier.
  • Try a 3-2-1 wind-down: no big meals three hours before bed, no screens two hours before, and dim lights in the last hour.

See it in real life

Example

The new ending, the night before

Elijah has run the new ending 31 times in the garage since seven, and the last five were the worst. His dance crew competes at regionals tomorrow. On Thursday the choreographer changed the final 32 counts, and Elijah’s part, a turn that drops into a floor slide, keeps landing on the wrong foot.

It’s eleven at night, and his plan is to keep going until it’s automatic, even if that takes all night. He figures he can sleep after.

That plan gives up two things sleep would do for him. In mice, sleep widens the spaces between brain cells so fluid can wash through and carry off the waste that builds up while they’re awake, including a protein called beta-amyloid. In one study, healthy adults who skipped a single night of sleep had a little more of it in parts of the brain the next day. Sleep is also when a new skill gets strengthened. Staying up would cost him both, the night before he needs the move most.

So he films one last run on his phone to watch in the morning, and stops. He cracks his bedroom window, turns on the fan, leaves the phone charging in the kitchen and has the light off by 11:20, with his old alarm clock set for 7:20. When it goes off, he sits on the front steps in the early light for ten minutes before doing anything else.

Then he marks through the ending twice in the driveway, and the first run is clean, with his right foot coming down right on the count. At noon the crew hits the new ending together, and in the video his mom takes from the stands, he’s right on the beat.

  1. Where it stallsThirty-one runs since seven, and the last five land on the wrong foot.
  2. What changes itHe stops at eleven and sleeps in a cool room with his phone in the kitchen.
  3. What happens nextThe first run in the morning is clean, and the crew lands it together at noon.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

The crowded tray

This is a hands-on model of what sleep changes. You’ll need a baking pan or a shoebox lid, about 15 small objects of the same size, like dice, toy blocks or sugar cubes, a spoonful of rice and a sheet of paper.

  1. Pack the objects tightly at one end of the pan so they touch on every side. They’re brain cells while you’re awake.
  2. Sprinkle the rice over them and brush the top with your hand so the grains settle into the cracks. That’s the day’s waste.
  3. Tilt the pan over the paper, tap it twice, and see how much comes out.
  4. Spread the objects so there’s a gap about a finger wide between each one, the way the spaces open up during sleep.
  5. Tilt and tap the same way again, and compare the two piles.

What you’ll probably notice. Packed tight, very little moves. Open the gaps and most of the grains slide out with the same tilt. It’s only a model, but it shows the change that happens in the brain. During sleep, at least in mice, the spaces between cells widen so fluid can flow through and carry waste away. Staying up all night keeps the pan packed.

Step by step

Sleep before it counts

Start a few nights before the big day, and keep it going.

  1. Set a stop time that leaves eight hours. Count back from when you have to get up, and keep about the same bedtime every night, weekends included.
  2. No caffeine after lunch. Soda, coffee, iced tea and energy drinks all count. Caffeine blocks the sleep pressure your brain builds up during the day, and it lasts for hours.
  3. Wind down with 3-2-1. Finish big meals three hours before bed, turn off screens two hours before, and keep the lights low for the last hour.
  4. Keep the room cool and dark. Around 65°F (18°C) is a good target, and a cracked window or a fan helps. Charge your phone outside the room.
  5. Get some morning light. Spend ten minutes outside soon after you wake up, before screens if you can. Morning light helps set your body clock for the next night.

How to tell it’s working. For a week, write down when you fell asleep and when you woke up. After about five nights, notice whether you start waking up before your alarm, and whether what you practiced or studied the day before comes back more easily.

When it gets hard. The night before something big is exactly when “one more run” sounds most reasonable. Decide your stop time that afternoon, while you’re calm, and tell someone at home so you stick to it.

Good to know. These steps assume ordinary sleep. If you often lie awake for a long time, snore loudly or feel exhausted even after full nights, talk to a parent and a doctor.
