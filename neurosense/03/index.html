Why Multitasking Is Slowing You Down

Task Switching

There's no such thing as parallel attention. Every 'multitask' is rapid switching, and every switch costs you measurably.

The Science

  • Treisman & Gelade (Cognitive Psychology, 1980): Feature-Integration Theory shows that attention must be directed serially across each stimulus whenever conjunctions of more than one separable feature are needed. The attentional 'spotlight' is one-at-a-time.
  • Rubinstein, Meyer & Evans (J. Experimental Psychology: Human Perception, 2001): every task switch forces the prefrontal cortex to log out of one task set and load another. Switch-cost is real and additive, reducing productivity by ~40% on complex tasks.
  • Pashler (Psychological Bulletin, 1994): even the mere presence of a second concurrent task creates a Psychological Refractory Period, in which response to the second task slows because central processing is serially bottlenecked.
  • Ward et al. (J. Consumer Research, 2017): even the presence of a silenced smartphone in the room measurably reduces available working-memory capacity. Cognitive resources are recruited just to keep the phone in attention's blind spot.

The Protocol

  • One tab. One task. If you need to look something up, finish the paragraph first.
  • Phone in another room. Pocket and face-down both fail (Ward et al.).
  • Batch all notifications into one 5-min window every 90 minutes, align with your ultradian rhythm (V1).

See it in real life

Example

212 words since dinner

Leila’s history essay is due at 11:59 tonight. It’s 9:40, she sat down right after dinner, and the document says 212 words.

She isn’t slacking. The group chat is deciding who’s driving to Saturday’s game, and every time her phone lights up she answers, because it takes two seconds. Then she’s back in the essay, rereading her last paragraph to figure out what she was doing with the quote she pasted in. Three sentences later, the phone lights up again.

Those two seconds were never the real cost. To write that paragraph, Leila has to keep her argument, the quote and her next sentence active at the same time. The chat runs on a different set of rules, and her brain can only run one set at a time, so every flip means dropping the essay’s setup and building it again when she comes back. That rebuilding is where the evening went. Even lying face down next to her laptop, the phone takes a slice of her attention, just to keep her from picking it up.

At 9:45 she sends “essay mode, back at 10:15,” puts the phone in a kitchen drawer, closes every tab except the essay and the article she’s quoting, and sets a 25-minute timer on her laptop. When she isn’t sure about a date, she types [CHECK] and keeps writing instead of opening a search.

When the timer goes off, the count is 490, and the paragraph she’d been circling all night is done. The chat settled Saturday without her. She answers two messages, fixes her three [CHECK]s, and starts a second 25 minutes on the conclusion.

  1. Where it stalls212 words in an hour and a half, with the chat going off
  2. What changes itPhone in a drawer, one tab, a 25-minute timer
  3. What happens next490 words when the timer rings, the stuck paragraph done

Try it yourself · 2 minutes

The switch test

You can feel a switch cost on yourself. Grab a pen and some scrap paper, and use the stopwatch here.

  1. Write the letters A to J in a row, as fast as you can while staying neat. Time it.
  2. Write the numbers 1 to 10 in a row underneath. Time that too.
  3. Now alternate them, A 1 B 2 C 3, all the way to J 10. Time it.
  4. Compare the third time with the first two added together.
Round 1A B C D E F G H I J
Round 21 2 3 4 5 6 7 8 9 10
Round 3A 1 B 2 C 3 … J 10

The timer needs JavaScript. A phone stopwatch works just as well.

What you’ll probably notice. The third round is the same twenty characters, but it’s usually slower than the first two put together, and it’s where people slip, writing a K or skipping a number. That stumble is the reload described in The Science above: letters, then numbers, then letters again, twenty times in a row. A message in the middle of an essay is the same move with a lot more to reload.

Step by step

Make it work this week

Try it on the assignment you’ve been avoiding the longest.

  1. Open only what the task needs. Before you start, close every tab except the document and the one source you’re actually using. A new question doesn’t get its own tab yet.
  2. Put your phone in another room. Not face down, not in your pocket. Another room. If someone might really need to reach you, leave the ringer on for calls, and use your laptop’s clock for the timer.
  3. Say when you’ll be back. Send one message first, like “doing chemistry, back at 8:30.” It’s a lot easier to stay out of the chat when nobody is waiting on your reply.
  4. Park questions instead of chasing them. When you need to look something up, type [CHECK] in the text and keep going. Do every lookup at the end of the block.
  5. Work in 25-minute blocks. Set a timer for 25 minutes and stay in that one tab until it rings. Messages wait for the break: check everything at once, five minutes at most, then start the next block. After about 90 minutes of blocks, take a real break away from the screen.

How to tell it’s working. Pick one thing to count, like words written or problems finished. Do one block with the phone on your desk and one with it in another room, at about the same time of day. If the second number is higher, or you notice you’re rereading your own work less, it’s working for you.

When it gets hard. For the first few days you’ll probably reach for a phone that isn’t there. Keep a sticky note next to you and write down whatever you wanted to check. It’ll still be there at the five-minute window.
