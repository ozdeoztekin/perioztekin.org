How Dyslexia Hides Behind Good Listening

Dyslexia & Phonological Awareness

Struggling to read a word is not the same as failing to understand it, and good listening can hide that gap.

The Science

  • When you listen, the brain's pattern recognition fills in speech sounds that are not crystal clear, which can make a reading difficulty easy to miss.
  • The brain never evolved a reading area. Reading borrows a patch of visual cortex, the visual word form area, which becomes tuned to seeing words.
  • Print must then connect with speech sounds. In many children with dyslexia, the trouble is phonological awareness: picking out sounds inside words.

The Protocol

  • If a science chapter makes sense when you hear it but reading it drags, listen to it first, then read it.
  • Judge how well you understand an idea separately from how quickly you can read it.
  • Treat listening first as a way to reach the content, not as a treatment for dyslexia.

See it in real life

Example

Twelve pages on weather fronts

When her earth science class talks about weather fronts, Soraya is the one who explains the diagram to everyone at her table. Then she takes the twelve-page chapter home for Thursday’s lab, and two hours later she’s on page five, reading the same paragraph for the third time. Soraya has dyslexia, and she has known it since second grade, but tonight she’s starting to think she’s bad at science.

Following the ideas and reading the page are different jobs. When she listens, the brain’s pattern recognition fills in speech sounds that aren’t crystal clear, so every word lands. The brain never evolved a reading area, since reading is too recent an invention. It borrows a patch of visual cortex, the visual word form area, which becomes tuned to seeing words, and from there the signal has to connect with the parts that pick out the sounds inside spoken words. In many kids with dyslexia, picking out those sounds, called phonological awareness, is where the trouble is, so the mapping from print to sound works differently. A slow page doesn’t mean she’s failing to understand it.

So she flips the order. She turns on the textbook’s read-aloud button and listens to the whole section first with the diagrams open, pausing to sketch each one as it comes up. Then she reads the same pages with the ideas already in her head. She checks understanding on its own, closing the book and answering the five review questions out loud to her little brother, Omid, and she writes down how long the reading took on a separate line.

The reading still takes her more than an hour. In Thursday’s lab, her group draws the cold front on the wrong side of the map, and Soraya catches it before the teacher reaches their table. In her notebook the two numbers sit on separate lines, five questions answered and 70 minutes of reading.

  1. Where it stallsShe explains the diagram in class, then spends two hours on five pages.
  2. What changes itShe listens to the chapter first, then reads it, and checks understanding separately.
  3. What happens nextShe catches her group’s misplaced front, though the reading still takes over an hour.

Try it yourself · 2 minutes

The sounds inside words

Say everything out loud, alone or with someone, using the box below and one finger to tap with.

  1. Say each word in the first row and count its sounds, not its letters, tapping a finger for each sound.
  2. Say each word in the second row without the sound named after it.
  3. Swap the first sounds of each pair in the third row, out loud.
  4. Check yourself against the note, and notice which rows felt instant and which took effort.
Count the soundsshrimp, eight, knight, through
Take one outslip without l, stack without t, plant without n
Swap the startsfast car, pet shop, lucky dog

The counts are 5, 2, 3 and 3. The others become sip, sack and plat, then cast far, shet pop and ducky log.

What you’ll probably notice. You knew what each word meant the moment you saw it, but taking the sounds apart probably took some effort, especially in eight and knight, where the letters and the sounds don’t line up. Picking out the sounds inside words is called phonological awareness, and reading leans on it, because each written pattern has to be matched to sounds. In many kids with dyslexia that matching works differently. Understanding is a separate job, so a slow page says little about how well someone follows the ideas.

Step by step

Listen first, then read

Start with the next chapter that makes sense when you hear it but drags when you read it.

  1. Find a way to hear it. Use the read-aloud button in a digital textbook, an audio version, a text-to-speech app or someone reading to you.
  2. Listen first with the pictures open. Play the section once while you look at the diagrams and headings, and pause to sketch anything that clicks.
  3. Then read the same pages. Read with the ideas already in mind, at whatever pace the page takes.
  4. Check understanding on its own. Close the book and explain the main idea out loud, or answer the section’s review questions.
  5. Keep two separate numbers. Write down how much you understood and how long the reading took on different lines, so one never stands in for the other.

How to tell it’s working. After a week, look at the two lines side by side. If you can answer the questions even on days when the reading was slow, your understanding is showing up on its own, and listening first is getting you to the ideas.

When it gets hard. Listening can drift into background noise. Keep the diagrams in front of you and pause after each section to say one sentence about it before you go on.

Good to know. Listening first is a way to reach the content, not a treatment for dyslexia. If reading is slow or tiring for you, a parent, a teacher or the school’s reading specialist can help you find support that fits.
