When Less Salt Tastes Too Bland

Salt Preference & Repeated Exposure

The salt level you like can shift with what you keep tasting. A bland first bite need not be final.

The Science

  • Two judgments are at work: how salty a food tastes, and how salty you want it to taste. They are not the same.
  • Repeatedly eating one salt level sets a familiar reference, so a lower level can at first seem incomplete because it differs from what you expect.
  • Over sustained exposure, the salt level people find most pleasant can shift. Tasting the salt matters, not simply how much sodium enters the body.

The Protocol

  • For a food you eat often, use a modest reduction or lower-sodium version you still enjoy, and a smaller step if it is unpleasant.
  • Taste it before automatically adding back your usual amount.
  • Repeat it and revisit your liking, possibly over months; this concerns flavor, not your medical or exercise-related sodium needs.

See it in real life

Example

Salt before the first spoonful

Over fall break, Bao’s dad watched him salt the chicken and ginger soup Bao grew up on before he’d tasted it. At eighteen, in his first apartment, Bao makes a pot every Sunday from his dad’s recipe, and his version has drifted saltier. Now his dad’s tastes flat to him.

Back at the apartment he tried to move his pot toward his dad’s by cutting the salt in half, and the first bowl tasted like hot water with ginger in it. He salted it at the table until it tasted right, and the next Sunday he went back to his old amount.

How salty a food tastes and how salty you want it are separate judgments. Eating one salt level again and again sets a familiar reference, and his Sunday pots had been setting his, so a lower level seemed incomplete because it differed from what he expected. With sustained tasting, the level people like best can shift. Tasting the salt matters, and liking can move without a matching change in how salty the food tastes, so it isn’t a simple taste-bud reset. Half the salt was too big a step to enjoy, and salting the bowl brought the old signal straight back.

The next Sunday he takes a smaller step. He leaves one spoonful of salt out of the pot, little enough that it still tastes like his soup, and puts the shaker in the cupboard so he tastes every bowl before adding any. The first bowl tastes a little quiet, and he eats all of it without getting up.

He keeps the pot at that level for four Sundays, and by the fourth it tastes like his soup again, so he leaves out a little more. Over winter break his dad’s soup still tastes slightly light, but he tastes it first and finishes the bowl with the shaker untouched in the middle of the table.

  1. Where it stallsHalf the salt tastes like hot water, so he salts his bowl right back.
  2. What changes itHe leaves out a little salt and tastes each bowl before the shaker.
  3. What happens nextFour Sundays later, the lighter pot tastes like his soup again.

Try it yourself · 2 minutes

Two questions, one bite

Try this during a meal or snack you’re already having, with something salty in it like soup, toast or popcorn. Don’t add any salt for the test.

  1. Take one bite and, on the first slider, rate how salty it tastes.
  2. Then ask yourself a different question and answer it on the second slider, how salty you would want it to be.
  3. Press Compare and look at the gap between your two numbers.
  4. If someone else is eating the same food, ask them both questions too, and compare their answers with yours.

The timer needs JavaScript. A phone stopwatch works just as well.

What you’ll probably notice. The two numbers often don’t match, and two people can agree on how salty a bite tastes while wanting different amounts. How salty something tastes and how salty you want it are separate judgments, and the second leans on what you usually eat. That second judgment can shift, over weeks or months of tasting a slightly lower level.

Step by step

Step down, taste, give it time

For one food you eat often, if you’re an adult and you’d like it a little less salty. It’s about flavor, not a health rule, and if someone else does the cooking, decide it together.

  1. Pick one food you eat often. Choose something you have most weeks, like a soup you make, eggs or a sandwich, and leave the rest of your meals as they are.
  2. Take a modest step down. Use a little less salt, or a lower-sodium version, that you still enjoy. If the change makes the food unpleasant, make the step smaller.
  3. Taste before adding salt back. Take a few bites before you reach for the shaker. Adding your usual amount right away brings the familiar signal back before the new level has a chance to feel ordinary.
  4. Keep eating that version. Have it the same way meal after meal, without switching back and forth.
  5. Check your liking over weeks. Ask yourself again every week or two, and allow for it to take months. There’s no deadline and no promised reset after a few days.

How to tell it’s working. Every week or two, rate the lighter version from 0 to 10 for how much you like it, and note whether you reached for the salt. A rating that creeps up, or a shaker you leave alone more often, suggests your familiar level is moving.

When it gets hard. The first bowl of the new version is the one most likely to taste flat. Give it repeated meals before you decide, and if it still tastes unpleasant, make the step smaller and keep going.

Good to know. This is written for adults, so if you’re under 18, use it only for noticing. If a doctor has given you advice about salt, or you’re an athlete with sodium needs for long or sweaty training, follow that instead. This is about how food tastes to you, and nothing here means eating less.
