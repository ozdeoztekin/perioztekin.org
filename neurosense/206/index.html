What If Waiting Is About Trust?

Trust & Delayed Gratification

When promises have not been kept, taking the treat now may be a reasonable choice, not weak self-control.

The Science

  • For a child, a bigger later reward may seem less worth waiting for if the adult has already failed to bring promised art supplies.
  • Waiting is not passive: part of it is keeping a future goal active while something tempting pulls at you, a job for the prefrontal cortex.
  • Children start with shorter-term goals and learn with experience that holding a longer one can pay off, a lesson that may depend on kept promises.

The Protocol

  • Before calling a quick grab weak self-control, ask whether earlier promises of a later reward were actually kept.
  • When something tempting sits in front of you, imagine a frame around it so it becomes a photo, not the real thing.
  • When helping a child wait, try covering the snack or suggesting they picture a frame around it.

See it in real life

Example

Ten minutes under a dish towel

“That boy has no patience,” one of the aunts says as Kabir grabs a cookie off Nani’s cooling rack and blows on his fingers. He’s six, and every Sunday goes the same way, with sweets gone the moment they appear and the grown-ups shaking their heads.

Ishani looks after him on Sunday afternoons while the grown-ups cook, and she isn’t sure patience is the problem. His dad promised him a box of paint markers three Sundays in a row and got called in to work every time, and the zoo trip has been “next weekend” since August. From where Kabir stands, later doesn’t come very often.

Waiting takes work. Part of it is holding on to a future goal while something tempting pulls at you, a job for the prefrontal cortex, the brain region that keeps goals active and applies them. Children start with short-term goals and learn with experience that holding a longer one can pay off, a lesson that may depend on promises being kept. When promised rewards keep falling through, taking what’s there now can be a reasonable choice rather than a lack of self-control.

Ishani makes one promise she can keep. “When this timer rings, we frost them together, and you pick first.” She puts out the frosting and sprinkles and starts a ten-minute timer. Then she lays a dish towel over the rack, since covering a treat can help a kid wait, and shows him how to make a frame with his thumbs and fingers, so the cookie becomes a picture of a cookie. He lifts a corner of the towel twice and puts it back. When the timer rings, she does exactly what she said.

She keeps the same promise the next two Sundays, to the minute. On the third Sunday Kabir sets the timer himself, sits on the step stool with his fingers held up in a frame and waits for it to ring.

  1. Where it stallsKabir grabs every cookie, and the aunts say he has no patience.
  2. What changes itIshani keeps a small promise, covers the cookies and teaches a finger frame.
  3. What happens nextBy the third Sunday he sets the timer himself and waits.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

Frame it, then cover it

You’ll need a snack you like on a plate, a napkin or a small bowl, and something to write on. This isn’t about going without, so eat it at the end if you want it.

  1. Look at the snack in the ordinary way for one minute. Notice the smell and the wanting, and write down how strong the pull is from 0 to 10.
  2. Make a frame with your thumbs and index fingers and look at the snack through it for one minute, as if it were a photo in a cookbook. Rate the pull again.
  3. Cover it with the napkin or bowl for one more minute, and rate it a third time.
  4. Compare the three numbers, then eat the snack if you still want it.

What you’ll probably notice. The pull often eases a little when the snack turns into a picture, and again when it disappears under the napkin. Waiting is active work, keeping a later goal in mind while something tempting pulls at you, and tricks like these may take some of that load off. They come from work with young children, where covering a treat or picturing a frame around it helped them wait, so trying them on yourself is an experiment.

Step by step

Make waiting worth it

Use it with a younger kid you look after, and try the frame on your own temptations.

  1. Check the promise record first. Before you call a quick grab weak self-control, ask whether the last few promises of something better later were kept.
  2. Make small promises you can keep. Start with short waits, like five or ten minutes, and keep your word to the minute, every time.
  3. Cover the treat. Put a napkin, a bowl or a towel over it, or move it out of sight while they wait.
  4. Turn it into a picture. Show them how to make a frame with their fingers, so the treat becomes a photo of a treat.
  5. Try the frame on yourself. When something tempting sits in front of you, frame it as a photo. These tricks were studied with young children, so treat your own use as an experiment.

How to tell it’s working. Keep a short note each time with how long the wait was and whether your promise was kept. Over a few weeks, see whether the waits stretch out as the kept promises pile up.

When it gets hard. A broken promise may teach the opposite lesson. Promise only what you’re sure you can deliver, and if one slips anyway, say so and make it right quickly.
