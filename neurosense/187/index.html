The Low Stakes Exposure

gentle exposure and fear

Avoiding what scares you makes sense for a minute, then it quietly teaches your brain the fear was right.

The Science

  • Exposure works by building new safety associations, not by waiting for fear to vanish first.
  • Approaching a feared cue creates fresh evidence that it can exist without disaster.
  • Fear can still be present while your nervous system updates its prediction during the attempt.

The Protocol

  • Choose the smallest version of the avoided action that is safe and real.
  • Stay long enough to collect evidence while keeping the dose humane.
  • Repeat it in a slightly different setting so the learning travels.

See it in real life

Example

Biscuit behind the glass door

Biscuit weighs seventy pounds and thinks every person is his best friend. Tamsin’s family brought him home in March, and since then Selin has answered every invitation to Tamsin’s house the same way, by asking, “Can we do mine instead?” Big dogs have scared her since she was little. Lately Tamsin has stopped asking, and her birthday sleepover is in three weeks, at the house with the dog.

Every no brings relief for an afternoon, and then the next invitation makes Selin’s stomach drop all over again. She doesn’t want to miss the sleepover, but she can’t picture walking through that door.

Avoiding something scary makes sense in the moment, but each time, it teaches the brain again that the fear was right. Exposure works the other way around. It doesn’t wait for the fear to go away. It builds new evidence that the dog can be there without anything bad happening, and the nervous system can update its prediction even while the heart is pounding.

Selin asks Tamsin to help, and she picks every step herself. The first is the smallest real one she can find, with Biscuit on his leash in the backyard, Tamsin holding it and Selin in the kitchen behind the closed glass door. Her heart is pounding, and she stays four minutes while he sniffs the grass, flops down in the sun and yawns. The next visit, the door is open and Biscuit lies at Tamsin’s feet across the patio for six minutes. The time after that, Selin tosses him a treat from the kitchen table while Tamsin holds the leash, and he catches it and lies back down. Then she tries the neighbor’s old beagle on the sidewalk, so the practice isn’t only about one dog.

At the sleepover, Biscuit falls asleep on the rug about three feet from Selin’s sleeping bag. Some time after midnight she reaches over and rests her hand on his side, and he doesn’t even wake up.

  1. Where it stallsShe answers every invitation to Tamsin’s house with “Can we do mine instead?”
  2. What changes itShe watches Biscuit from behind the glass door for four minutes, then takes small steps.
  3. What happens nextAt the sleepover, Biscuit falls asleep three feet from her sleeping bag.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

Stay one more minute

Pick something that makes you a little uneasy, like spiders, snakes or bees, not your worst fear, and use a drawing if a photo is too much. You’ll need a pen and a phone timer.

  1. Before you look, rate how uneasy you expect to feel, from 0 to 10, and write it down.
  2. Look at the picture for 60 seconds, and write your number at the start and again at the end.
  3. Notice what your body does in the first ten seconds and in the last ten.
  4. Later today, in a different room, look at a different picture of the same thing for 60 seconds, and write your start and end numbers again.

What you’ll probably notice. Your number may drop a little by the end of the minute, and the second round often starts lower than the first. Nothing about the picture changed, but you stayed long enough to collect evidence that it can be there without anything happening, and trying it somewhere else can help that evidence carry over. If the number didn’t move, that’s common too, since the learning can happen while the fear is still there.

Step by step

Your small-step ladder

It’s built for everyday fears that keep you from things you want, like a phone call or a crowded room.

  1. Write a short ladder. List four or five versions of the thing you avoid, from the smallest to the biggest. Every rung has to be safe, and you choose each one.
  2. Start with the smallest real rung. Make it small enough to do on a messy day and real enough to count, like the dog behind the glass, not a video of a dog.
  3. Stay long enough to learn something. Stay until you’ve seen what happens, even with your heart pounding, and decide before you start how long that will take.
  4. Write down what happened. Write what you predicted, what happened and your fear from 0 to 10 at the start and at the end.
  5. Repeat it somewhere a little different. Do the same rung in a different place, at a different time or with a different person, and move up a rung when the current one feels smaller.

How to tell it’s working. Look at your notes after a week. If your starting number on a rung drops from one try to the next, or you’re doing a rung you’d have refused two weeks ago, it’s working, even if the fear hasn’t gone away.

When it gets hard. Some days a rung will feel bigger than last time. Step down one, do that and count it as a real try. Nobody gets to push you up the ladder, and a dare never counts as a rung.

Good to know. This is for everyday fears. If a fear is overwhelming, is tied to something frightening that happened to you or gets in the way of school, friends or sleep, work on it with a doctor or therapist instead of on your own.
