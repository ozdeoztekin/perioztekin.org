When the Prize Overshadows the Activity

Prizes & Intrinsic Motivation

When a prize is added to something a child already enjoys, the payoff can start to overshadow the enjoyment.

The Science

  • There are two kinds of pull: intrinsic, doing something for its own sake, and extrinsic, doing it for a separate payoff such as a gold star.
  • A gold star can turn a child's drawing into something to earn, and when the stars stop, choosing to draw can lose some of its pull.
  • Dopamine supports wanting and is involved in marking time. One possible explanation: focusing only on an end payoff makes doing feel less rewarding.

The Protocol

  • Keep some drawing or craft sessions free of any prize bargain, so there is room to do it just because you want to.
  • In the hard stretches of something you love, treat the effort itself as the good part.
  • Keep rewards occasional rather than attaching a prize to every session, without treating all rewards as harmful.

See it in real life

Example

Page nine since June

Page nine of Fergus’s comic about a raccoon who runs a bakery at night has been half inked since June. He has been drawing it for years, mostly for himself and his little sister.

This summer his drawing app ran a daily prompt event, and every finished prompt earned a gold badge and coins to spend on new brushes. He finished sixty-one prompts in a row and set the comic aside, since it earned nothing. The event ended in August, the badges stopped, and so did he. When he opens the sketchbook now, drawing for nothing feels pointless.

There are two kinds of pull. Intrinsic pull is doing something for its own sake, and extrinsic pull is doing it for a separate payoff, like a gold star or a gold badge. A payoff attached to something you already enjoy can start to overshadow the enjoyment, and when it stops, choosing to do the thing can lose some of its pull. Dopamine helps with wanting and seeking and is involved in how the brain marks time. One possible explanation, still a hypothesis, is that aiming only at a payoff at the end ties the reward to finishing, so the drawing itself feels less rewarding.

Fergus starts keeping Sunday mornings for the raccoon, in his sketchbook, with no app, no posting and no plan to show anyone. When he reaches the part he dreads, the raccoon’s paws curled around a rolling pin, he tells himself this is the part he chose and that the effort is the good part, and he draws the paws again and again until one pair looks right. He still joins an app event now and then and spends the coins without a second thought.

Three Sundays later the comic has five new pages. On a Wednesday night, with no event running and nothing to earn, he takes the sketchbook to the couch and starts page fifteen.

  1. Where it stallsWhen the app’s summer badges stopped, so did his drawing.
  2. What changes itHe keeps Sunday mornings prize-free and calls the hard part the good part.
  3. What happens nextThe comic gains five pages, and he draws midweek with nothing to earn.

Try it yourself · 10 minutes

Ten minutes, nothing riding on it

Grab whatever you make things with, like a pencil and scrap paper, yarn or an instrument, and use the timer below.

  1. Before you start, decide that whatever you make won’t be posted, sold, graded or shown to anyone.
  2. Start the timer and make something, anything, until it rings.
  3. Each time your mind drifts to how it will look to other people or what it’s for, notice it and go back to the line, stitch or note in front of you.
  4. When the timer rings, write down one moment when you enjoyed the doing itself, then keep what you made or toss it.

The timer needs JavaScript. A phone stopwatch works just as well.

What you’ll probably notice. With no payoff waiting at the end, attention tends to settle on the doing, like the curve of one line or a color you mixed. If your mind kept reaching for who would see it, that was the separate payoff pulling, even with nothing at stake. One possible explanation for why payoffs can crowd out the enjoyment is that they tie the reward to the finish, and a session like this gives the doing some room of its own.

Step by step

Keep some sessions prize-free

Try it with anything you already love doing, like drawing, crochet, music or a sport.

  1. Pick one prize-free session a week. Choose a regular time, like Sunday morning, when nothing you make will be posted, sold, graded or rewarded.
  2. Make what you’d make anyway. Work on whatever you’d choose if nobody were paying or watching, in a separate sketchbook or folder if that helps.
  3. Call the hard stretch the good part. When you reach the part you dread, like hands or a tricky bar, tell yourself it’s the part you chose. This is a recommendation rather than a tested method, so notice whether it changes the session for you.
  4. Keep rewards occasional. Rewards aren’t the problem, so join the challenge or take the prize now and then, without attaching one to every session.
  5. Count the times you start on your own. Make a note each time you pick up your craft with nothing due and nobody asking.

How to tell it’s working. Compare how often you start on your own in the month before and the month after. More starts with nothing due suggest the pull is coming back, though a month is a short time to judge.

When it gets hard. You’ll want to post the good ones. Keep prize-free pages in their own sketchbook, or wait a week before you share anything from those sessions.
