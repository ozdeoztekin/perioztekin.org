Why No Two Brains Run the Same Way

The Mechanism of Neurodiversity

School asks every brain to work the same way, and some brains just do not fit the mold. That mismatch is not a defect. It is a wiring difference.

The Science

  • Baron-Cohen (2017), Journal of Child Psychology and Psychiatry: neurodiversity frames different wiring as variation, not defect.
  • Every brain has its own connectome of strengths, sensitivities, and trade-offs.
  • A brain wired one way can stall in the wrong setting and fly in the right one, so the question is what setup fits.
  • Not fitting the mold is a wiring difference, not a defect.

The Protocol

  • Map your own patterns: when you focus hardest, what drains you, what lights you up.
  • Build the routine, tools, and environment around that map.
  • Stop comparing your machine to a different machine.
  • Notice the signal, name the mechanism, and change one input before autopilot.

See it in real life

Example

Maren’s chart on the fridge

The homework chart on the fridge is Maren’s, laminated and still up two years after Sage’s big sister left for college: 3:30 to 5:30 at the kitchen table, silence, phones in the basket. It got Maren straight A’s, so in September their mom put up a fresh copy with Sage’s name on it.

Every afternoon at 3:30, fried from a day of hallways and bells, Sage sits at that table and reads the same biology paragraph five times while the dishwasher hums. By 5:30 she has one assignment of four done, and the rest drag past eleven. The grade portal shows six missing. Her mom has started saying, “You’re not even trying.”

No two brains run the same way. Each one is wired with its own strengths, sensitivities and trade-offs, and a brain can stall in one setting and fly in another. Maren’s chart was built around Maren’s brain. The real question was never what’s wrong with Sage, only what setup fits.

For a week she keeps three columns in the back of her planner: focused hardest, drained me, lit me up. Her best focus comes around seven at night, standing up, on days she got outside after school. The kitchen drains her, and so does sitting still past half an hour. Drawing a problem out lights her up. She shows her mom the columns and gets a two-week trial: a bike ride and a snack at 3:30, then homework at 7, standing at her dresser, with foam earplugs, a whiteboard for diagrams and 25-minute blocks.

On Thursday all four assignments are done by 8:45. Two weeks later the portal shows zero missing, and Maren’s chart goes in a drawer. Sage’s planner page goes under the magnet instead.

  1. Where it stallsTwo hours at the kitchen table, one assignment of four done
  2. What changes itA week of three columns, then a setup built on them
  3. What happens nextDone by 8:45, and zero missing two weeks later

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

The bother list

You need a pen, paper and one other person: a friend, a sibling or a parent.

  1. Rate each item on the list from 0 to 10 for how much it bothers you or pulls at your attention. Write your numbers down without showing anyone.
  2. Ask the other person to rate the same list without seeing yours.
  3. Put the two sets side by side and circle every item where you’re 4 or more points apart.
  4. Pick one circled item and ask what it’s like for whoever rated it higher.
A clock ticking in a quiet room
The tag in the back of a shirt
Someone chewing near you
A flickering light
A crowded hallway between classes
Music with words while you read
A scratchy sweater
A phone buzzing on the table
Bright sun in your eyes
A strong perfume or cologne

0 means you barely notice it, 10 means you can’t think around it. There are no right answers.

What you’ll probably notice. You’ll probably find a few big gaps, sometimes on things one of you barely notices. Neither of you is wrong: your brains weigh the same input differently, which is the kind of wiring difference the page describes. What drains you is information about your own machine, and it belongs on your map.

Step by step

Build the setup that fits

One week to map, then two weeks to test.

  1. Keep three columns for a week. When I focused hardest, what drained me, what lit me up. Add a line or two each day, with times and places.
  2. Circle what repeats. At the end of the week, look for anything that shows up three or more times: a time of day, a room, a sound, a kind of task.
  3. Put hard work in your best window. Move the work that needs the most focus to the time your columns say you focus hardest, even if nobody else works then.
  4. Change one tool or place. Earplugs, a standing spot, a whiteboard, a quieter room: pick whatever answers your biggest drain or your biggest spark.
  5. Ask for a two-week trial. Show the adults involved your columns and ask to test the new setup for two weeks. Data is easier to say yes to than “I just work better at night.”

How to tell it’s working. Count something concrete, like assignments finished by a set time or missing work on the grade portal. Compare the two trial weeks with the two weeks before. That’s the comparison that counts: you against your own last month.

When it gets hard. Sometimes the setup that fits isn’t allowed, like earbuds in class or a fixed practice time. Look for the closest version you do control, and ask a teacher or school counselor what’s possible.

Good to know. If school stays hard no matter the setup, or you wonder whether something like ADHD, dyslexia or being on the autism spectrum is part of it, a parent, school counselor or doctor can help you find out and get support.
