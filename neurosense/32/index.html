Why You Miss Things Right In Front of You

The Science of Perception

You think you see everything in front of you. You actually miss a lot of it.

The Science

  • Simons & Chabris (Perception, 1999): in the famous "invisible gorilla" experiment, about half of the people asked to count basketball passes failed to notice a person in a gorilla suit walking through the scene. Similar results have been found many times since.
  • Rensink (Annual Review of Psychology, 2002): in change-blindness studies, people often miss large changes to a scene unless their attention is on the part that changes, so what we see depends heavily on attention.
  • Mack & Rock (Inattentional Blindness, 1998) named this effect: when attention is busy, even clear, unexpected objects can go unseen. Magicians use the same principle in misdirection.

The Protocol

  • Every 90 minutes of focused work, deliberately look up.
  • Wide gaze. Soften your eyes. Notice 5 things in your environment that have nothing to do with what you were focused on.
  • Looking up on purpose gives your attention a chance to widen before you dive back in.

See it in real life

Example

A 61 on the bench outside Maple Grove

Twice a month Ravi does a card magic show in the dayroom at Maple Grove, the retirement home on his street. Mrs. Halloran has picked a card at every one of his shows, and every time she wants it to come out of his shoe.

On Saturday, sitting on the bench outside, his phone buzzes with his chemistry test grade. It’s a 61. Ten minutes later he still hasn’t moved. College, his parents and the whole semester are all he can see, and he has typed a message to the activities director saying he isn’t feeling great and is so sorry. The show starts at one.

Attention works something like a spotlight. Whatever’s inside it feels sharp, and the brain can miss things outside it, even big changes right in front of it. In one famous experiment, about half the people counting basketball passes never noticed a person in a gorilla suit walking through the game. Magic runs on this, since the room watches Ravi’s right hand while his left does the real work, and right now a 61 has taken over his attention in the same way.

He puts the phone in his pocket and gives himself ten seconds. He softens his eyes, widens his gaze and names five things he hadn’t noticed. The rope on the flagpole is clanking, the grass has been cut, a bus is pulling up at the corner, his deck box is worn at the corners, and Mrs. Halloran is waving from the dayroom window. Then he asks himself what else could be true. It’s one test out of eleven, there’s a retake on Thursday, and a room full of people has been waiting since lunch.

He deletes the message and goes inside. He does nine tricks in twenty minutes, and Mrs. Halloran’s signed four of hearts comes out of his left shoe to the biggest laugh of the day. On the walk home he emails his chemistry teacher about the retake.

  1. Where it stallsA 61 on his phone has him ready to cancel the show.
  2. What changes itHe takes ten seconds to widen his gaze and name five things he’d missed.
  3. What happens nextHe does the show, then emails his teacher about the retake.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

Three changes

You’ll need one other person and nothing else. It’s a pocket version of a classic attention test.

  1. Stand facing each other and look carefully for 30 seconds, trying to take in everything about how the other person looks.
  2. Turn back to back. Each of you changes three small things about how you look, like rolling up a sleeve, taking off a watch, moving a hair tie, untucking a shirt or putting a ring on a different finger.
  3. Turn around and find the other person’s three changes, saying each one out loud as you spot it.
  4. Go again with three new changes, or swap who looks first.

What you’ll probably notice. At least one of the three changes usually slips past, sometimes an obvious one, even while you’re staring straight at the other person and trying hard. Your eyes took in the whole picture, but only what your attention locked onto stayed with you. A bad grade or a fight can work a little like that, because when one thing fills the spotlight, the rest is easy to miss.

Step by step

Move the spotlight

Reach for it before you send the text, quit the task or decide the whole thing is hopeless.

  1. Notice the takeover. One grade, one fight, one notification or one comment is filling your whole mind, and you’re about to decide something because of it.
  2. Pause for ten seconds. Put your phone face down or in your pocket. Nothing gets sent, deleted or quit during those ten seconds.
  3. Widen your gaze and name five things. Soften your eyes, take in the whole room and name five things you weren’t focused on, like a sound, a smell or something across the room.
  4. Ask what else could be true. Look for one piece of evidence outside the spotlight, like the rest of your grades, the rest of the friendship or the rest of the day.
  5. Look up during long work, too. After about 90 minutes of focused work, do the same reset. Look up, widen your gaze and find five things that have nothing to do with the task.

How to tell it’s working. Over the next seven days, make a tally mark each time you use the reset before sending, deleting or quitting something, and write next to it whether you went ahead, changed it or waited. If some decisions changed, or you went ahead feeling surer, the reset is doing its job.

When it gets hard. In the moment you’ll forget the reset exists, because that’s what a takeover does. Tie it to one physical cue, like your thumb hovering over Send or Delete, so the pause starts there.
