When Your Sleep Score Keeps You Up

Sleep Scores & Orthosomnia

Trackers only estimate your night, and treating the score as a verdict can backfire on your day and sleep.

The Science

  • Sleep labs sort your night into stages by reading brain waves; trackers only guess at those stages, many from heart rate and movement.
  • Negative feedback about your sleep can make thinking tasks harder, even when it disagrees with your own sense of how the night went.
  • Treating an estimate as a verdict can backfire: worrying about sleep until it hurts your sleep has a name, orthosomnia.

The Protocol

  • If the score stresses you, check your data once a week, such as Sunday afternoon, instead of starting every morning with it.
  • If tracking has become truly upsetting, put the device in a drawer until sleep confidence returns, then reconsider using it.
  • Notice whether you are sleepy during the day, and if you are sleepy most days, see a doctor.

See it in real life

Example

A 58 before the farm truck leaves

The score comes up before Ottilie is fully awake. On market days her watch buzzes at ten to five, and the first thing she does is check how she slept. This morning it says 58.

She felt fine until she read it. In the farm truck she announces she’ll be useless today, and at the stand every slow moment becomes proof, like asking the price of leeks twice. Every Saturday since she got the watch has gone this way, and on Friday nights she lies there listening for the alarm, wondering what the number will be.

That number is an estimate. Sleep labs sort a night into stages by reading brain waves, and her watch only guesses at them, mostly from her heart rate and movement. Still, bad news about sleep can make thinking tasks harder, even when it disagrees with how the night felt, and adding up a basket in your head is a thinking task. Worrying about sleep until it hurts your sleep has a name, orthosomnia, and lying alert for an early alarm can make deep sleep less deep.

The watch stays her alarm, but she takes the score off its face and opens the sleep data once a week, on Sunday afternoon. Her mornings start without a grade, and Friday night has one less thing to wait for. In the slow hour around eleven she asks herself whether she’s sleepy right then and writes yes or no on the back of the price board.

The next Saturday she doesn’t know her number when she ties on her apron, and she rings up the morning rush from the price board in her head without once reaching for the calculator. On Sunday afternoon she looks at the week all at once, a couple of lower nights among the rest, next to her own notes, which say no every day but one.

  1. Where it stallsA 58 on her watch at dawn becomes proof she’ll be useless.
  2. What changes itShe checks the score once a week and notes daytime sleepiness.
  3. What happens nextShe rings up the rush in her head and reviews the week once.

Try it yourself · 2 minutes

A made-up score

All this takes is the two sliders below. The score in it is invented, and nothing here measures your sleep.

  1. Before you read any further, use the first slider to rate how rested and clear-headed you feel right now.
  2. Read this as if your watch had shown it to you first thing this morning. “Sleep score 58. Poor night. You may feel tired today.”
  3. Picture the rest of your day with that line in mind for a few seconds, and then rate yourself again on the second slider.
  4. Compare the two numbers. Nothing about your night changed between them.

The timer needs JavaScript. A phone stopwatch works just as well.

What you’ll probably notice. Even with a score you know is invented, the second rating can come out a little lower. What a score says can shape how a day feels, and bad news about sleep can make thinking tasks harder even when it doesn’t match how you felt. A real tracker’s number is an estimate, often built from heart rate and movement, so it deserves a weekly look, not a vote on every morning.

Step by step

Meet the number once a week

For anyone whose sleep score has started to set the mood for the day.

  1. Pick one weekly check time. Choose a time like Sunday afternoon to open the sleep data and look at the week. The tracker can keep measuring the rest of the time.
  2. Start mornings without the number. If your device allows it, take the score off the watch face or lock screen, so the day doesn’t begin with a grade.
  3. Notice daytime sleepiness. Once a day, at a set point like the end of lunch, ask whether you feel sleepy right then and note yes or no. Sleep needs vary, and sleepiness during the day can be a sign you need more.
  4. Use the drawer if it gets upsetting. If tracking has started to make you anxious about sleep, put the device in a drawer until you feel confident about your sleep again, and then decide whether to go back to it.
  5. Read the week as a whole. On check day, look at the trend and your sleepiness notes together, and leave single nights alone.

How to tell it’s working. After two weeks, count the mornings that started with the score and the days your sleepiness note said yes. If the score no longer sets the tone of your mornings, keep the weekly check. If the yeses cover most days, that’s a sign to see a doctor.

When it gets hard. The urge to peek is strongest the morning after a rough night. If you look anyway, close it, don’t try to make up for the number, and save the rest for check day.

Good to know. If you feel sleepy most days, or you lie awake most nights, tell a parent or guardian and get it checked by a doctor. A score can’t settle that, and neither can a weekly look.
