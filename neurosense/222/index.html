ADHD: When Five Minutes Feels Like One, or Twenty

ADHD & Judging Elapsed Time

With ADHD, sensing how much time has passed can be inconsistent, and the misses can go either way.

The Science

  • With ADHD, judging elapsed time can be unusually inconsistent: an interval can seem shorter or longer than it really was.
  • No single brain area tracks elapsed time. In one account, your brain slices experience into frames, and dopamine helps set how finely.
  • In this account, finer frames make time feel longer; coarser ones could make it feel shorter, which fits running late, not misses the other way.

The Protocol

  • Put markers outside your head: give a task a block in your daily schedule with a visible start and end.
  • Mark the start with one small routine, such as clearing your desk when the homework block begins.
  • Use the same routine at the same point each day, so it marks a new stretch of time.

See it in real life

Example

Eleven minutes that felt like thirty

On Monday Thandiwe put her cello down after what felt like a solid half hour, and the kitchen clock said eleven minutes. On Wednesday she sat down “for five minutes” before dinner and looked up an hour later, when her dad called her name for the third time.

Seating auditions for the youth orchestra are in two weeks, and her teacher wants thirty minutes a day on the audition excerpt, written in a practice log. So far the log reads 11, 64, 0 and 9. Thandiwe has ADHD, and she loves the cello. What she can’t trust is her sense of how long she’s been playing.

With ADHD, judging how much time has passed can be unusually inconsistent, and the misses can go either way. No single brain area keeps track of it. One account says the brain slices experience into frames, with dopamine, a chemical messenger, helping set how finely, so finer frames make a stretch feel longer and coarser ones could make it feel shorter. That account can’t explain every miss, so the practical move is to put the markers outside her head.

She writes “Cello, 4:30 to 5” on the whiteboard by the fridge and moves the old kitchen clock to the bookshelf, where she can see it from her chair. Every day at half past four she does the same small routine. She clears the music stand down to the excerpt, then tightens and rosins her bow, and that marks the start of a new stretch. When the clock on the shelf says five, she stops, even mid-phrase.

Her sense of time still misses some days, in both directions, but the block doesn’t depend on it. Over the next two weeks the log fills up with thirties, with blanks only for her cousin’s wedding weekend, and she makes it to the table before dinner gets cold every night.

  1. Where it stallsWhat feels like half an hour of practice is eleven minutes.
  2. What changes itShe writes the block on the whiteboard and starts by rosining her bow.
  3. What happens nextHer log shows thirty minutes on twelve of the next fourteen days.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

Guess a minute, twice

You need the stopwatch below and something to doodle on or read. Try not to look at the numbers until both rounds are done.

  1. Press start for round 1, then look away and sit still with nothing to do. Press stop when you think one minute has passed.
  2. Press start for round 2 and doodle or read something you like, and press stop when you think another minute has passed.
  3. Now look at both times and compare each one with 60 seconds.
  4. If you want more, try another pair tomorrow at a different time of day and see whether the misses move.

The timer needs JavaScript. A phone stopwatch works just as well.

What you’ll probably notice. Few people land right on 60 seconds, and the two guesses often miss by different amounts, sometimes in different directions. The brain has no single clock for this, so every guess is an estimate. With ADHD, those estimates can be unusually inconsistent, which is a good reason to let a clock you can see and a block with a written start and end keep the time.

Step by step

Blocks with a start and an end

Pick one thing that keeps running too short or too long, like practice, homework or getting out the door.

  1. Give it a block in your day. Write the start and the end where you’ll see them, like “4:30 to 5:00, cello” on a whiteboard, a planner or a note on your door, at the same time each day if you can.
  2. Keep the end in sight. Put a clock where you can see it without picking anything up, so the end of the block doesn’t depend on how long it feels.
  3. Start with one small routine. Do the same short routine each time the block begins, like clearing your desk, laying out your music or filling a water bottle, so it marks the start of a new stretch.
  4. Repeat it at the same point each day. A routine that always comes at the same point works as a marker whether the day felt fast or slow.
  5. Write down what the clock said. After each block, note when it started and ended by the clock and how long it felt, so you can see which way your sense of time drifted that day.

How to tell it’s working. After a week or two, count the blocks that started and ended at the times you wrote. If that count is climbing, keep the routine and the clock where they are.

When it gets hard. Some days you’ll look up and the block ended twenty minutes ago, and other days you’ll be sure it’s over when it isn’t. Both are the inconsistency this plan is built around, so go by the clock over the feeling and start the next block on time.

Good to know. This is a way of working with time, not a treatment for ADHD. If time trouble keeps causing problems at school or at home, bring it up with a parent, a doctor or a school counselor, who can help you find support that fits.
