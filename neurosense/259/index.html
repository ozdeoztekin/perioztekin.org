Ask for the Next Move, Not a Verdict

Task-Focused Feedback & Next-Time Advice

A verdict says how you did. Asking for one thing to do better next time makes a usable next step more likely.

The Science

  • Useful feedback depends on where it sends your attention: a broad verdict can turn the question into "Am I good at this?"
  • A harsh judgment can pull you toward defending yourself, while a specific observation points to one part of the task to keep or change.
  • Asking "What is one thing I could do better next time?" moves the answer from judging the past toward planning a future action.

The Protocol

  • After a presentation or other attempt, ask: "What is one thing I could do better next time?"
  • If the answer stays vague, bring it back to the work: "Which part should I repeat, and which part should I change?"
  • Try one relevant suggestion, such as putting your recommendation before the numbers, then look at that specific change.

See it in real life

Example

Two verdicts and a blank page seven

“It’s so good,” Camille said on Thursday, flipping through the first six pages of Nasir’s comic on the bus. That evening, at the comic shop’s drawing night, the artist who runs it looked through the same pages and said, “The storytelling needs work.” One verdict felt better than the other. Neither told him what to draw on page seven.

He hasn’t drawn it yet. All weekend he argued with the harsher verdict in his head and redrew the opening panel of the comic three times, and page seven is still an empty border.

Useful feedback depends on where it sends your attention. A broad verdict can turn the question into “Am I good at this?” and a harsh one can pull you toward defending yourself. A specific observation points at one part of the work to keep or to change, something you can repeat or correct. Asking what to do better next time moves the answer away from judging the past and toward planning what comes next.

The next Thursday he brings the same pages and asks the artist, Clement, “What’s one thing I could do better on the next page?” Clement flips back a few pages and taps a panel. “Here I read her answer before his question, because her balloon is higher and farther left.” Nasir asks which part he should keep, too. “The close-ups when someone’s surprised. Those land.”

On page seven, every question sits higher and farther left than its answer, and the surprise gets a close-up. Before he shows anyone, he runs a finger through each panel in reading order to check. Then he hands the page to Camille without saying what changed, and she reads it straight through, without flipping back, and laughs at the last panel, where the joke is.

  1. Where it stalls“So good” and “needs work” leave him with a blank page seven.
  2. What changes itHe asks the artist for one thing to do better on the next page.
  3. What happens nextHe fixes the balloon order, and Camille laughs at the right panel.

Try it yourself · 10 minutes

Ask it two ways

Take something small you’ve made, like a drawing, a paragraph, a song recording or a playlist, to a friend or someone in your family. Have something to write on.

  1. First ask, “What do you think?” and write down their answer word for word.
  2. Then ask, “What’s one thing I could do better next time?” and write that answer down too.
  3. If the second answer is vague, bring it back to the work by asking which part you should repeat and which part you should change.
  4. Read your notes and underline anything you could try on your next attempt.

What you’ll probably notice. Their first answer is often a verdict, kind or not, with little in it to underline. The next-time question sends the other person’s attention to the work and to the future, so the answer is more likely to name one part to keep or change. It can’t make every suggestion a good one, but it gives you something to try.

Step by step

Ask for the next move

After any attempt someone else saw, like a game, a performance, a drawing or a presentation.

  1. Ask the next-time question. Ask, “What’s one thing I could do better next time?” rather than “How did I do?”
  2. Bring vague answers back to the work. If you hear “It was fine” or “Make it pop,” ask which part you should repeat and which part you should change.
  3. Keep what already works. Write down the part to repeat, too. Getting better doesn’t mean replacing everything.
  4. Try one relevant suggestion. Pick the one that fits what you’re trying to do and use it on the next attempt, like putting your recommendation before the numbers in a talk.
  5. Look at that one change. Check whether that specific change did what you hoped, rather than giving the whole attempt a new grade.

How to tell it’s working. Keep a short list of the suggestions you tried and what each one changed. If more of your feedback turns into something you can try, and you can point to the change in your next attempt, keep asking the question.

When it gets hard. Not every suggestion is right, and the question can’t change that. If one doesn’t fit what you’re trying to do, thank the person and set it aside.
