Why You’re Starving After a Study Session

The Sodium-Potassium Pump

One pump in your neurons runs nonstop, moving 3 Na+ out and 2 K+ in per ATP, and it is one of the biggest energy costs in your brain.

The Science

  • Skou (Biochimica et Biophysica Acta, 1957): in crab nerves, he found an enzyme that splits ATP only when sodium and potassium are present, the Na+/K+-ATPase, work that earned a share of the 1997 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. Each cycle of this pump uses one ATP to move three Na+ ions out of the cell and two K+ ions in.
  • Attwell & Laughlin (Journal of Cerebral Blood Flow & Metabolism, 2001): their energy budget for rodent gray matter estimated that most of the energy used for signaling goes to pumping ions back across the membrane after action potentials and synaptic activity.
  • Howarth, Gleeson & Attwell (Journal of Cerebral Blood Flow & Metabolism, 2012): an updated budget, using newer evidence that action potentials are more efficient than once thought, put about half of the cortex's signaling energy into reversing the ion flow at synapses, about a fifth into action potentials and about a fifth into holding the resting potential.
  • Without active pumping, the slow leak of ions would gradually run down these gradients and neurons would lose the ability to signal, so the pump has to keep working all the time.

The Protocol

  • Steady meals: choose slower carbohydrates, like oatmeal or whole-grain bread, over a sugary snack before studying.
  • Sleep: protect it on study days; in rats, brain energy levels rose sharply in the first hours of sleep.
  • Steady Supply: The pump runs nonstop whether you are studying or resting, so steady meals through the day matter more than a special snack at study time.

See it in real life

Example

Why the blower never stops

Sebastian’s sister Ada is timing him at the kitchen table, and the first 40 seconds go fine, three sodium out and two potassium in for every ATP. On Monday he interviews for a summer lab program, and every applicant has to teach one biology idea in two minutes. Then Ada asks why the pump has to keep going once the ions are where they belong, and twice all he can come up with is “It just runs.”

On Saturday, at his party rental job, he watches a kid chasing a balloon kick the blower’s cord loose. The bounce castle is empty because everyone is at the cake table, and within a minute its towers fold over and the front wall sags down to his knees.

The castle leaks air through its seams all day long, so the blower can never stop, and a neuron works the same way. Sodium keeps trickling back in, so the pump keeps spending ATP, the cell’s fuel, to push three sodium out and bring two potassium in. Without it, the leak would slowly erase the voltage the neuron needs in order to signal. Every signal adds more to clean up, and all that pumping makes it one of the brain’s biggest energy costs.

He plugs the castle back in. That night at the kitchen sink he pokes a pencil hole near the bottom of a paper cup, draws a line inside, and keeps the water at the line by pouring from a glass while he explains it all to Ada. When he stops pouring, the water drops. When he adds a second hole, for a burst of signals, he has to pour faster.

On Monday he tells the bounce castle story, draws the cup and finishes in a minute and 50 seconds. When the interviewer asks what a neuron spends so much of its energy on, Sebastian says, “Keeping the castle up.” She laughs and writes something down.

  1. Where it stallsHe knows the ratio but can’t explain why the pump never stops.
  2. What changes itA sagging bounce castle and a leaky paper cup give him the answer.
  3. What happens nextHe teaches it in under two minutes and handles the follow-up.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

The leaky cup

This is a model of a pump that can never stop. You’ll need a paper cup, a pencil, a marker, a glass of water and a sink.

  1. Poke a small hole near the bottom of the cup with the pencil tip, and draw a line inside about two-thirds of the way up.
  2. Over the sink, fill the cup to the line and keep it there for 30 seconds by pouring slowly from the glass.
  3. Stop pouring and watch the level.
  4. Poke a second hole, fill the cup to the line again and try to hold it there.

What you’ll probably notice. The cup can’t hold the line on its own, and the moment you stop pouring, the level drops. In a neuron the leak runs the other way, with sodium trickling in, and the pump keeps pushing it back out, three sodium out and two potassium in for every ATP. The second hole is like a burst of signals, since each one leaves more to restore, and all that endless pouring is one of the brain’s biggest energy costs.

Step by step

Teach the pump in two minutes

Practice on someone at home before it counts.

  1. Count it on your hand. Hold up three fingers for the sodium going out, two for the potassium coming in, and make a fist for the one ATP it costs. Do it every time you say it.
  2. Draw the leak next to the pump. Draw a line for the membrane, a small arrow of sodium leaking in, and the pump sending three sodium out and two potassium in.
  3. Show someone the cup. Do the leaky cup for someone at home and tell them what each part stands for. The hole is the leak, and your pouring is the pump.
  4. Answer the nonstop question. Say it in one sentence. The leak never stops, so the pump can’t either, and without it the neuron’s resting voltage would slowly drain away.
  5. Time yourself tomorrow. With no notes, explain all of it out loud against a two-minute timer. If you run over, cut words, not steps.

How to tell it’s working. If you can explain three out and two in, why the pump never stops and why it costs so much energy, with no notes and in under two minutes, you’ve got it. If the person you showed the cup to can explain the leak back to you, that counts too.

When it gets hard. If you mix up which ion goes which way, start from the leak. Sodium is the one sneaking in, so sodium is the one the pump throws out.
