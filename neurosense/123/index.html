Why Curiosity Makes Memory Stick

Curiosity, Retrieval & Active Memory

Memory improves when the brain knows why this exact information is worth keeping.

The Science

  • Curiosity acts like a tag, telling attention and memory systems that a detail might matter later.
  • Turning material into a question changes reading from soaking to hunting, which leaves stronger tracks.
  • Testing yourself without looking makes you rebuild the memory, and a little difficulty is often a sign it is working.

The Protocol

  • Turn the material into a question before you read.
  • Connect the idea to a friend, game, song, mistake, or goal.
  • Test yourself without looking, so retrieval becomes the training repetition.

See it in real life

Example

Sixteen questions at the touch tank

The species binder in Jada’s apron pocket is meant for emergencies, but on her first Saturday volunteering at the aquarium’s touch tank, she pulled it out about every twenty minutes.

Kids ask fast, and they ask about everything, like whether the urchin has a mouth, whether the hermit crab can feel a finger and why the sea star is rough. Five times in two hours she had to say she wasn’t sure, and one dad looked up the urchin on his phone while she stood there. She had read the binder three times on the bus, and every page felt familiar, but very little came back with a seven-year-old staring up at her.

Reading it three times was soaking. Her eyes passed over every line, and nothing told her brain which lines mattered. Curiosity works like a tag, so when a detail answers a question you care about, your attention and memory mark it as worth keeping. Starting with a question turns reading into hunting, which leaves a stronger trace, and answering without looking rebuilds the memory each time. If that feels a little hard, the difficulty is a sign it’s working.

On the bus home she had typed every question she couldn’t answer. That week she copies each one onto the top of its page and reads only to find the answer, and next to it she writes who asked, like the boy in the shark shirt or the dad with the phone. Then she closes the binder and answers every question out loud, to her cousin or to the bathroom mirror, and tries the misses again the next morning.

At her next shift she keeps count, and she answers fourteen of sixteen questions without opening the binder. When a girl asks whether the urchin has a mouth, Jada lifts one out of the water and shows her the mouth on its underside.

  1. Where it stallsShe isn’t sure five times in two hours, and a dad checks his phone.
  2. What changes itShe puts a kid’s question on every page and answers with the binder closed.
  3. What happens nextShe answers fourteen of sixteen questions with the binder in her apron.

Try it yourself · 10 minutes

Question first

Use something you need to learn this week, like a textbook section, a manual, a playbook or a set of lines. You’ll need paper and a pen.

  1. Split the material into two parts of about the same length.
  2. Read part one the way you usually do.
  3. Before part two, write two or three questions you want answered, and then read it hunting for the answers.
  4. Close everything and wait two minutes. Then, without looking, write down all you remember from each part.
  5. Compare the two lists, and circle the answers that took some effort to pull back.

What you’ll probably notice. Reading with questions usually leaves you with the longer list, because each detail was attached to something you were looking for. The effort of writing it down without looking is part of the training, and that bit of difficulty is a sign it’s working. If the two lists come out even, try it again on a harder page.

Step by step

Make it a hunt, then test it

Use it for anything you need to know without notes, like a test or a game plan.

  1. Write a question before you read. At the top of each page or section, write a question you want answered. Questions people have asked you are the best ones.
  2. Read to find the answer. Read like you’re hunting for it, and underline only the line that answers your question.
  3. Tie it to someone or something. Next to each answer, write who asked, a mistake you made, or a song, game or goal it connects to, since your brain stores the context along with the fact.
  4. Answer out loud without looking. Close the material and answer each question out loud or on paper, and mark the ones you miss.
  5. Retest the misses tomorrow. Start the next day with the marked questions before you open anything. If it feels a little hard, that’s the training working, not a failure.

How to tell it’s working. Before the day it counts, answer all your questions cold. Count how many you get without looking, and compare that with your count from the first day.

When it gets hard. If no question comes to mind, turn the heading into one, like “Why does this happen?” or “What would someone ask me about this?” And if testing yourself feels bad because you blank, the blanks are your review list.
