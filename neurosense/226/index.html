Stuttering Isn't Caused by Nerves

Stuttering & Speech Coordination

In stuttering, the ideas and grammar are all there; what falters, at moments, is the coordination of speech.

The Science

  • Nerves can trigger or worsen a stutter, but they are not the cause. The cause is still unclear, while the ideas and grammar are all there.
  • At moments, coordination of voice box, lips and jaw breaks down; disruption in speech-related basal ganglia, deep movement circuits, may contribute.
  • Changing how a speaker hears their own voice can change how much they stutter, for better or worse, a clue the speaking-hearing link may be involved.

The Protocol

  • When someone stutters, skip telling them to just relax or that they are just nervous.
  • Wait, and let them finish their own words rather than finishing for them.
  • Respond to what the person said, not to how fluently it came out.

See it in real life

Example

The raccoon story, told all the way through

Kojo has been saving the raccoon story since Saturday. At lunch on Monday he gets as far as “So my dad goes out to the,” and the first sound of “garage” won’t come.

Before it can, Teddy says “Garden?” and Kiri says, “Relax, man, you’re fine.” Kojo says “never mind,” and the table moves on. He has stuttered since he was little, and lately he’s stopped starting stories at lunch at all. These are his closest friends, and he isn’t nervous around them. He gets stuck on the same sounds at the kitchen table with his mom.

Nerves can make a stutter worse, but they aren’t the cause, which is still unclear. His ideas and his grammar are all there. What breaks down, at moments, is the precise coordination of his voice box, lips and jaw, sometimes right at the first sound, and a disruption in deep movement circuits called the basal ganglia may be part of it. So “relax” aims at the wrong thing, and every guess at his word swaps his story for someone else’s.

On the walk to the bus that afternoon, he tells Teddy and Kiri what would help. It takes a while, and they wait through it. He stutters, it isn’t nerves, and when he gets stuck he wants them to wait, not guess, and then answer what he said.

The next day he starts the raccoon story over. He gets stuck on “garage” in the same place, and nobody fills it in. Teddy keeps looking at him the way he would at anyone who’s talking, and a couple of seconds later the word comes. By the time Kojo gets to his dad in a bathrobe chasing the raccoon across the yard with a broom, the whole table is laughing, and Kiri wants to know whether the raccoon got to keep the hot dog buns.

  1. Where it stallsHis friends guess his word, so he says never mind.
  2. What changes itHe asks them to wait, not guess, and to answer what he said.
  3. What happens nextHe tells the whole raccoon story, and the table laughs at the right part.

Try it yourself · 2 minutes

Feel your voice box switch

Find a spot where you can talk to yourself for a couple of minutes. All you need is your voice and two fingertips.

  1. Rest two fingertips lightly on the front of your throat, under your chin, and say a long “zzzz.” Then switch to “ssss” without moving your mouth.
  2. Feel the buzz switch off and on while your tongue and lips stay put. That buzz is your voice box.
  3. Keep your fingers there and say a sentence slowly, like “My dad ran out to the garage with a broom,” and notice how often the buzz starts and stops.
  4. Say the same sentence at normal speed and notice that you never had to plan any of it.

What you’ll probably notice. One plain sentence asks your voice box to switch on and off many times, precisely timed with your lips and jaw, and it all happens without you thinking about it. In stuttering, that coordination breaks down at moments, sometimes at the first sound, while the ideas and the grammar are all there. So what a listener can offer is time to finish, not advice to calm down.

Step by step

Wait for the word

For listening to anyone who stutters, whether it’s a friend, a teammate, a classmate or a coworker.

  1. Skip telling them to relax. Nerves can make a stutter worse, but they aren’t the cause, so “relax” or “you’re nervous” aims at the wrong thing and can add pressure.
  2. Wait, and keep looking at them. Stay with them the way you would with anyone who’s talking, and let the word come. The idea is already there.
  3. Let them finish their own words. Guessing the word, even to help, puts your words in place of theirs.
  4. Answer what they said. When they finish, respond to the joke, the question or the idea, not to how smoothly it came out.
  5. Ask in private what helps. If you know the person well, ask when you’re alone together how they’d like you to respond. People differ, and they know best.

How to tell it’s working. In your next few conversations with someone who stutters, notice whether you filled in a word or said anything about their speech. Count the times you waited, and see whether that number grows.

When it gets hard. A silence of a few seconds can feel long. Keep your eyes on them and count slowly in your head, the way you’d wait for anyone else to finish a thought.

Good to know. If you stutter and want to work on it, speech therapy is the route, and it can work on the link between what you hear and what you say. A parent or a school counselor can help you find a speech-language therapist.
