Why Next Month Looks So Much Easier

Why Versus How Thinking

From far away you see why a plan matters; up close you mostly see how hard it is to do.

The Science

  • From a distance, you think abstractly about why you want something, and the whys of hard things are usually positive.
  • Up close, you think concretely about how you will do it, and the hows of hard things are mostly negative, because they are hard.
  • Being tired or emotionally worked up can also pull you toward the here-and-now how.

The Protocol

  • Before you start a hard task, name your whys, several of them, not just one.
  • When the work arrives and the hows feel loud, bring the why back on purpose.
  • Separate the reason from the work, as in music practice: playing with friends is the why, repeating the awkward part is the how.

See it in real life

Example

The index card by the alarm

In September, running the phone help table at the senior center sounded like the best thing Noa had agreed to all year. She and her friend Hattie would spend Saturday mornings helping people with their phones.

Now it’s the fifth Saturday, her alarm is going off after a late Friday, and all Noa can think about is how. She’d have to catch the 8:20 bus, sit in a room that smells like coffee and carpet, and explain to somebody, for the fourth time, how to attach a photo to a text. She skipped two weeks ago. Her thumb is already on a message to Hattie saying she can’t make it.

From a distance, people tend to think about why they want something, and the whys of hard things are usually good. Up close they think about how they’ll do it, and the hows of hard things are mostly bad, because they’re hard. So the hard part lands right when the hows are loudest, and being tired pulls her toward the here-and-now how even more. By Sunday, with some distance back, the why looks obvious again and she wishes she’d gone. Bringing the bigger reason back on purpose may help her choose the bus over the blanket.

The night before, she wrote several whys on an index card and propped it against the alarm clock. Mr. Ferreira wants to video-call his granddaughter in Porto. She’s good at explaining things slowly. Her own grandmother has the same phone and nobody nearby to help. Afterward, she and Hattie walk to the bakery for pastries. She puts the phone down and reads the card before deciding anything. The why is Mr. Ferreira’s call, the how is a twenty-minute bus ride, and she gets up.

She makes the bus. At a quarter past ten, his granddaughter’s face fills the screen of Mr. Ferreira’s phone, and he turns it around so the whole table can wave.

  1. Where it stallsOn the fifth Saturday she lies in bed, about to cancel.
  2. What changes itShe reads the whys on her index card before deciding anything.
  3. What happens nextShe makes the bus, and Mr. Ferreira video-calls his granddaughter.

Try it yourself · 2 minutes

Same plan, two distances

Think of something you signed up for that’s coming up soon, and grab a pen and some scrap paper.

  1. Picture it as if it were a month away, and write down the first three words that come to mind.
  2. Now picture the morning it’s due, at the moment you’d have to start, and write down the first three words again.
  3. Mark each word as a why, meaning a reason or a feeling, or a how, meaning a step or a cost.
  4. Write three more whys, each a different kind of reason, and read them over once.

What you’ll probably notice. The faraway list usually leans toward whys, like fun, friends or proud, and the close-up list toward hows, like early, cold or long. The plan didn’t change between the lists, only the distance, and with it the question your mind was asking. Writing several whys on purpose can bring back some of the faraway view at the moment you need it.

Step by step

Bring the why back

Use it for anything you said yes to that gets harder as it gets closer.

  1. Name several whys before you start. Write at least three reasons of different kinds, like a person who’s counting on you, something you’re good at and something you’ll enjoy afterward.
  2. Put them where the hard moment happens. Lean the card against your alarm, tuck it in your gym bag or clip it to your music stand.
  3. Read the whys, then decide. When the hows get loud, read the card first and make the call second.
  4. Keep the reason and the work apart. Say the why and the first how separately, like “the why is his call, the how is the bus,” so the work doesn’t swallow the reason. The work stays the same, but the reason can get easier to hold in mind.
  5. Watch for tired or worked-up moments. Being tired or upset can pull you toward the how, so those are the times to reach for the card on purpose.

How to tell it’s working. Over a month, tally the times you showed up or started on time, and compare that with the month before. Note the mornings you read the card before deciding, too, because that step is the one you control.

When it gets hard. The same card can start to feel stale. Add a fresh why every week or two, like something that happened the last time you went.
