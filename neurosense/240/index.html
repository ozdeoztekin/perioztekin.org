Why You Try Less When Others Are Trying Too

Social Loafing & Brainstorming

When others can cover your share, easing off gets easier. Brainstorming in a group runs into the same pull.

The Science

  • Social loafing is putting in less effort as part of a group: when someone else can cover your share, it becomes easier to ease off.
  • It can happen even without a real partner to coordinate with: your voice can get quieter when you think others are shouting alongside you.
  • Larger groups tend to invite more coasting, and group brainstormers usually put in less effort and come up with fewer ideas than people working alone.

The Protocol

  • For your next club event, have everyone think of ideas alone first, with nobody to lean on.
  • Then pool the lists in a small group rather than one large one.
  • When you notice yourself easing off because someone nearby could cover your work, do your own share anyway.

See it in real life

Example

Twelve kihaps, then one at a time

On the last move of the demonstration team’s opening form, all twelve of them kihap, the sharp shout that goes with a strike. “Twelve of you,” Master Baek says, “and it sounded like four.” He has them do it one at a time, and each shout rings off the mirrors, Celeste’s included. In the group, she had barely used her voice.

Then comes the meeting she dreads. The town’s harvest festival wants a ten-minute show, and after two meetings the whiteboard holds three ideas, all about breaking boards, from the same two people. Celeste has a page of ideas at home and hasn’t said one, figuring someone else will.

That pull is called social loafing, which means putting in less effort as part of a group. When someone else can cover your share, it gets easier to ease off, and it can happen even without real partners to coordinate with, since your voice can get quieter when you only think others are shouting alongside you. Larger groups tend to invite more coasting, and people brainstorming in a group usually put in less effort and come up with fewer ideas than people working alone.

At the next meeting, Celeste asks Master Baek for five minutes before anyone talks. Everyone gets an index card and writes ideas alone, with nobody to lean on, and Celeste writes her share too, all seven from her page. Then they pool the cards in groups of three, not one big circle, and each small group brings its best two to the whiteboard.

The cards hold forty-one ideas, and before the hour is up the team has its show, a slow-motion sparring match with a narrator, a form performed side by side by the youngest students and the black belts, and a board for every first-year member to break. The next Thursday, they start rehearsing.

  1. Where it stallsIn the group her kihap fades, and the idea board is stuck at three.
  2. What changes itEveryone writes ideas alone first, then they pool cards in threes.
  3. What happens nextForty-one ideas come in, and the show is planned in one meeting.

Try it yourself · one day

Count your coasting

Pick an ordinary day with some group time in it, like a group chat, a team practice, a family chore or a group project. Use the counter below, or keep a tally on paper.

  1. Each time you catch yourself easing off because someone else could cover it, tap the counter. Staying quiet on a group call, skipping a reply because others will answer and slowing down in a team drill all count.
  2. Make a quick note of where each one happened and roughly how many people were there.
  3. Pick one of those moments and do your own share anyway.
  4. That evening, look at where most of your taps came from.

The timer needs JavaScript. A phone stopwatch works just as well.

What you’ll probably notice. The count is often higher than people expect, and many taps come from bigger groups and group chats. When someone else can cover your share, easing off gets easier, and more people can make that pull stronger. Spotting it in the moment is what makes the third step possible.

Step by step

Alone first, then pool

Bring it to the next club or team meeting that needs ideas.

  1. Share the question ahead of time. Tell everyone what you’re deciding before the meeting, like “a ten-minute show for the festival” or “three fundraiser ideas.”
  2. Think alone first. Give everyone five minutes and a card to write ideas on their own, with no talking and nobody to lean on. Ask for at least five, and say that silly ones count.
  3. Pool in small groups. Combine the cards in groups of three or four rather than one big circle, since larger groups tend to invite more coasting.
  4. Bring each group’s best to everyone. Each small group brings its top two ideas, and the whole group chooses from there.
  5. When you ease off, do your share anyway. If you catch yourself going quiet because someone nearby could cover it, say your idea or do your part.

How to tell it’s working. Count the ideas from your next alone-first round and compare them with the last meeting where everyone brainstormed out loud together. Count how many people offered at least one idea, too.

When it gets hard. Some people freeze when told to write alone. Give a number and a timer, and say out loud that silly ideas count.
