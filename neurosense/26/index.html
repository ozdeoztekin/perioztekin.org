Why Watching a Skill Can Help You Learn It

Mirror Neurons

Watching a skill can help your brain prepare for the movement, especially when you turn watching into active rehearsal and practice immediately after.

The Science

  • Rizzolatti & Craighero (Annual Review of Neuroscience, 2004): when you watch a goal-directed action, parts of your own movement-planning system become active. In monkeys, some of the same motor neurons fire whether the animal does an action or watches it done; in people, brain imaging shows overlapping activity.
  • Iacoboni (Annual Review of Psychology, 2009): reviews evidence that this matching between watching and doing supports imitation, which may be one way watching helps you learn a movement.
  • Pascual-Leone et al. (Journal of Neurophysiology, 1995): people who only imagined practicing a five-finger piano exercise for five days showed motor cortex changes like those from real practice, though their playing improved less; after one two-hour session of real practice, they caught up with five days of physical practice.
  • Watching does not replace practice; it prepares practice, so pair each viewing with real repetitions.

The Protocol

  • Choose one tiny skill.
  • Watch once for the whole movement.
  • Watch again for the starting position.
  • Watch a third time for rhythm and finish.
  • Lock the screen and do three slow repetitions immediately.
  • Ask: what looked different in my body? Fix one thing next.

See it in real life

Example

Twelve daisies for the flower girl

The daisy in Javier’s hand has a hole in the middle big enough to push a pencil through. It’s his ninth try at the magic ring, the loop crocheters use to start anything round, and every time he pulls the tail to close it, the stitches slide loose. His aunt’s wedding is on Saturday, his little sister Luz is the flower girl, and he promised her twelve crocheted daisies for her basket.

He has watched about thirty crochet videos in bed at night, one after another, flowers and blankets and little bears, and his rings still come apart.

When you watch someone do a skilled movement, parts of your own movement-planning system can become active, a bit as if you were getting ready to do it yourself. In monkeys, some of the same neurons fire whether the animal does an action or watches it, which is where the idea of mirror neurons comes from. Watching closely can give practice a head start, but it gets you ready for practice instead of replacing it, and thirty clips in bed without a single stitch afterward skipped the part that counts.

On Wednesday he picks one slow, close-up clip from a crocheter who teaches beginners, and he watches it three times. The first time he watches the whole ring. The second time he watches only how she starts, with the yarn wrapped around two fingers and the tail pinched underneath, and the third time he watches her rhythm and how she finishes. Then he locks his phone and makes three slow rings at the kitchen table. He notices he has been pinching the tail on top, so on the fourth try he moves it underneath, and the ring pulls shut.

By Thursday night five daisies sit on the windowsill with their centers closed tight. On Saturday Luz carries all twelve down the aisle, and at the reception she gives one to the bride.

Try it yourself · 10 minutes

Three watches, three tries

Pick a small skill you can find a short, clear clip of, done by someone who’s good at it. The fast T-shirt fold works well, and so does a square knot or a card shuffle.

  1. Watch the clip once all the way through, paying attention to the whole movement.
  2. Watch it again only for the starting position, meaning where the hands are and what they’re holding.
  3. Watch it a third time for the rhythm and the finish.
  4. Lock the screen and do it three times right away, slowly.
  5. Ask yourself what looked different in your body compared with the clip, and fix that one thing on a fourth try.

What you’ll probably notice. Your first slow try is often closer than you expect, and the thing to fix after the third is usually something you can name, like a hand starting in the wrong place. Watching with one question at a time gives your own movement planning a clear model to get ready from, and the slow tries right afterward are where it turns into a real movement.

Step by step

Watch three times, then do it

Try it the next time you’re learning a move from a video.

  1. Choose one small skill. Pick a single movement, like the start of a crochet ring, a turn, a chord change or a release. If it takes more than a few seconds, cut it down.
  2. Find a skilled model and a clear view. Look for someone good at it, filmed up close from an angle that shows the hands or feet. Slow motion helps.
  3. Watch three times, one question each. Watch for the whole movement first, then for the starting position, and then for the rhythm and the finish.
  4. Lock the screen and do three slow ones. Do them right away, before you watch anything else. Going slowly is fine, since what you’re after is getting your body into the shape you just watched.
  5. Fix one thing. Ask what looked different in your body, pick one difference and change only that on the next try.

How to tell it’s working. Film one of your slow tries on the first day and again after a few sessions, and put both next to the clip. Count how many details match now, like the starting position, the rhythm and the finish.

When it gets hard. If you’re on your sixth clip and haven’t tried once, lock the screen and do your three slow tries before you watch anything else.
