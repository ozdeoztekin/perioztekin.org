Why Cringe Feels Physical

Social Pain

That old embarrassing memory makes you wince for a reason. Social pain and physical pain run on the same neural hardware.

The Science

  • Eisenberger, Lieberman & Williams (Science, 2003): in a Cyberball social-exclusion fMRI study, being left out activated the dorsal anterior cingulate cortex (dACC), a region also involved in physical pain, and more dACC activity went with more reported distress.
  • Eisenberger (Nature Reviews Neuroscience, 2012): in a review, social and physical pain activate overlapping regions, including the dACC and anterior insula; later work suggests their finer-grained brain patterns differ (Woo et al., 2014).
  • One evolutionary explanation: for early humans, being cut off from the group was dangerous, so a brain that treated rejection as urgent may have helped survival.
  • DeWall et al. (Psychological Science, 2010): people who took acetaminophen daily for three weeks reported fewer hurt feelings, and in a small brain-imaging study it reduced dACC and insula responses to exclusion.

The Protocol

  • Name the feeling: 'That's the old cringe again.' Putting a feeling into words can take some of the sting out.
  • Reality check: ask who in your life now would even remember a three-year-old social slip.
  • Hardware forgiveness: thank your brain for guarding a high-stakes system.

See it in real life

Example

The seat by the window

Aisha has eaten lunch in the library for fifteen school days in a row. She moved here in January, and the window table in the cafeteria is where Mina and Sophie from her chemistry lab group sit. Mina has told her twice that she should sit with them sometime.

Every time Aisha starts toward that table with her tray, seventh grade comes back. She had sat down at a table, a girl said “That seat’s saved” loud enough for everyone to hear, and the whole table laughed while she carried her tray away. It was three years ago, and it still lands like a punch in the stomach. Her face gets hot, and she turns toward the library.

That punch is more real than it sounds. When people are left out, some of the same brain areas that respond to physical pain become more active. For most of human history, losing your group was dangerous, so a brain that treated rejection as urgent may have helped people survive. The trouble is that this alarm doesn’t check how old a memory is. Seventh grade can set it off as easily as today.

On Thursday, when it hits, she stops by the trash cans instead of turning around and names it to herself. That’s the pain alarm, going off over seventh grade. Then she asks herself who in this cafeteria even knows about that day, let alone cares, and the honest answer is nobody. She feels a little silly thanking the alarm, like thanking a smoke detector for going off over burnt toast, but she does it anyway, takes a breath, walks to the window table and asks, “Is this seat free?”

Mina moves her backpack. They spend lunch talking about the titration that turned their flask bright pink, and Sophie adds her to the lab group chat. On Friday Mina’s backpack is on the same chair again, and she moves it the second Aisha walks up.

  1. Where it stallsAn old memory keeps sending her to the library at lunch.
  2. What changes itShe names the alarm, asks who would care, and takes one breath.
  3. What happens nextShe sits at the window table, and the next day the seat is saved for her.

Try it yourself · 2 minutes

Name the wince

Pick a small embarrassing memory, one that makes you cringe a little but doesn’t really hurt. Skip anything painful or recent.

  1. Bring the memory back for about ten seconds and notice where you feel it, whether it’s your face, chest, stomach or shoulders. Rate it on the first slider.
  2. Name it in your head: “That’s the pain alarm, going off over an old memory.”
  3. Then ask yourself who in your life right now would care about this. Try to name one person.
  4. Thank the alarm for trying, take one slow breath, and rate the wince again.

The timer needs JavaScript. A phone stopwatch works just as well.

What you’ll probably notice. The second number often drops a point or two, and the memory may start to feel more like a story than a hit. The wince comes from a social alarm in the brain that overlaps with the one for physical pain. Naming it and checking the facts won’t erase the memory, but it often takes some of the heat out.

Step by step

When an old cringe hits

This is for the next time an old embarrassing moment stops you from doing something.

  1. Notice where it lands. It might be your face, your chest, your stomach or your shoulders. That physical wince is your cue to do the next steps.
  2. Name the alarm. Say it in your head, “That’s the pain alarm,” as flatly as if you were reading a label.
  3. Ask who would care now. Think about who in your life right now would care about that moment. Usually nobody comes to mind, or only you do.
  4. Thank it, then take one breath. The alarm is doing its job, just over old news. Take one slow breath and let it pass.
  5. Do the next thing anyway. Walk over to the table or send the message, instead of following the memory down the rabbit hole.

How to tell it’s working. Rate the wince from 0 to 10 when it hits and again a minute after the steps. Over a week, notice whether the drop gets bigger, whether the memory shows up less often, or whether you finally do the thing you’d been avoiding.

When it gets hard. The label can feel silly the first few times. Say it anyway. It’s supposed to sound a little boring.

Good to know. Cringing at old moments is normal. If replaying social moments takes up hours, keeps you up at night or makes you avoid people, a school counselor is a good person to tell, because there is real help for that.
