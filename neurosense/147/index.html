Why Comparison Is Bad Data

Status, Comparison & Values

Comparison can feel like measurement, but it often measures the wrong thing.

The Science

  • Your brain is built to notice status, because groups and acceptance matter.
  • But status is noisy data, especially when you only see the polished surface.
  • Someone else's highlight can rewrite what you value before you even choose.

The Protocol

  • Compare effort to yesterday's effort, especially when nobody claps.
  • Compare skill to the repetition you used to miss.
  • Compare your path to the life you actually want, not the image that got applause.

See it in real life

Example

The 97 in the group chat

The group chat got Maya’s chemistry test before Fatima got her own back. A photo of a 97, circled in red, and under it: “didn’t even study.”

Then her teacher sets Fatima’s test on her desk: a 78. She studied four nights for it. By lunch her stomach still hasn’t settled, and she has the level-change form open on her phone, the one that would move her from honors chemistry down to regular. She wants to be a veterinarian. An hour ago she was sure chemistry was part of that.

Her brain caught that 97 in a second. Brains are built to track where they stand, because groups and acceptance matter. But a photo in a group chat is noisy data. Fatima sees one polished number. She doesn’t see how long Maya actually studied, what Maya got last time, or how many scores nobody posts. One highlight can also change what she values before she has chosen anything: this morning she cared about understanding chemistry, and now she cares about a 97.

At home she runs a comparison she can check. She puts her September test next to this one. In September she missed all four stoichiometry problems; today she got three of the four. In September she crammed the night before; this time it was four nights. Then she writes one line on a sticky note about why she’s in the class: “vet school needs this, not a 97.”

Under it she adds “stoichiometry: 0 of 4, now 3 of 4” and sticks the note inside her binder. She closes the level-change form without sending it. Then she texts the group chat, “nice job, Maya,” and means most of it.

  2. What changes itSeptember’s test next to this one, problem by problem
  3. What happens nextThree of four where she used to get zero, form unsent

Try it yourself · 10 minutes

Finish lines and middles

You need any feed you use (a team page and a group chat both count) and something old of your own: a test, a sketch, a clip of you playing, a page of notes.

  1. Scroll the feed for two minutes. Count the posts that show a finished result: a score, a trophy, a perfect photo, an acceptance.
  2. Scroll two more minutes and count the posts that show the middle: practice, a mistake, a messy first try.
  3. Put the phone away. Find something you made or did a few months ago and set it next to something recent of the same kind.
  4. Write three things you can do now that you couldn’t do then. Be specific, like “all four stoichiometry problems” or “the chord change in time.”

What you’ll probably notice. The first count is usually far bigger than the second, so most of what a feed shows is finish lines with the work cut out. That’s the noisy status data from The Science above. Your own then-and-now is the opposite: dull to look at, but every bit of it is evidence you can check.

Step by step

Run a comparison you can check

Use it the next time a score, a post or a list lands in your stomach.

  1. Name what you actually saw. When a comparison stings, write down exactly what you saw (one score, one post, one list) and one thing you couldn’t see behind it.
  2. Compare effort to yesterday’s effort. Write today’s effort in numbers you can check: minutes practiced, problems tried, nights studied. Count it even when nobody claps.
  3. Compare skill to the old miss. Keep one old example, like a test, a clip or a sketch, and check it against today. Look for the repetition you used to miss and now make.
  4. Check the direction you want. Write one sentence about what you’re doing this for. Rank is not the same as direction.
  5. Ask them one question. If someone’s ahead of you, ask them one question about how they practice. Treat the answer as ideas to borrow.

How to tell it’s working. Once a week, put an old piece of work next to a new one and count what has changed. If the list keeps growing and the sting from other people’s posts fades faster, it’s working.

When it gets hard. Some weeks the then-and-now shows nothing new. That’s when the effort comparison carries you: if you showed up as often as last week, that counts.

Good to know. If comparing yourself with other people leaves you feeling low for days at a time, tell someone you trust, like a parent, a coach or a school counselor.
