A Question Can Change What You Remember

Misinformation Effect & Leading Questions

A question can carry its own answer, and that suggested detail can slip into your memory of what you saw.

The Science

  • Asking how fast a car was going when it passed the yield sign already assumes a yield sign. If you saw a stop sign, the two can compete.
  • That is the misinformation effect: what you learn after an event can seep into your memory of it, even through one word in a question.
  • Remembering is a partial reconstruction, so another person's description can become an input, even if you saw different details.

The Protocol

  • Before you compare stories with anyone, write down what you saw in your own words.
  • Ask questions that leave the answer open, such as 'What was at the corner?', instead of naming the sign.
  • Keep what you personally noticed separate from what you heard afterward from others.

See it in real life

Example

What he saw from the left block

Dario and a kid from Westfield went up for the same rebound and came down tangled, and the Westfield kid got up holding his mouth. The referee ejected Dario for a flagrant foul. The league reviews every ejection before the playoffs, so each player who was on the floor has to send the coach a written account by Wednesday night.

Santiago was on the left block, about ten feet away. In the parking lot, Dario’s mom catches his sleeve and says, “You saw that kid shove him first, right?” At home the team chat is past a hundred messages, most of them about a blurry clip from the Westfield bleachers captioned “dirty elbow.”

A question can carry its own answer. “Shove him first” already assumes a shove, and if that isn’t what Santiago saw, the suggested detail can compete with his own memory. This is called the misinformation effect, when what you learn after an event seeps into your memory of it, even through one word in a question. Remembering is a partial reconstruction, so the clip, the chat and Dario’s mom can all become pieces he rebuilds the play from, and a chat full of people agreeing wouldn’t settle what happened either.

Before he opens the chat, he writes his own account at the kitchen table, in his own words. He saw both of them jump and heard the hit. He didn’t see an arm, because number 22 was between them. Under a line he writes what he heard afterward, the shove, the elbow and the clip. Then he texts Wendell, who was at the top of the key, “What did you see right before they jumped?”

He sends the coach both halves of the page. On Thursday at lunch, someone plays the clip again and says, “Look, elbow,” and for a second Santiago can almost see one. His page from Tuesday night says he never saw the arm.

  1. Where it stallsThe chat says “elbow,” and Dario’s mom asks a question that assumes a shove.
  2. What changes itHe writes what he saw before he reads a single message.
  3. What happens nextHis page says what he saw and what he couldn’t see.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

Write it before you compare

Try it with a friend or someone at home. You each need a pen and paper, plus something ordinary to watch for 30 seconds, like the street out a window or a short video with the sound off.

  1. Watch the same 30 seconds together without talking.
  2. Separately, each of you writes down six things you noticed, like colors, people, objects and anything that moved.
  3. Read your lists to each other, and circle anything only one of you wrote or that the two of you remember differently.
  4. Have your friend ask you a question with a detail tucked inside, like “What was the man with the umbrella doing?”, and then an open one, like “What did you see on the left?”
  5. Notice which question made a picture show up faster, and check it against your list.

What you’ll probably notice. Two people watching the same half minute rarely write down the same six things. A question with an umbrella in it can call up an umbrella whether or not there was one, while the open question leaves room for what you saw. Once you start comparing, the other person’s description can become part of what you remember, so the list you wrote first is the one to check against.

Step by step

Your account first

These are precautions rather than a guarantee, worth using after anything you might be asked about later, like a collision in a game, a fender bender or an argument you saw.

  1. Write it down before you talk. As soon as you can, and before you read the chat or compare stories, write what you saw in your own words.
  2. Stick to what you noticed. Say where you were and what you could and couldn’t see. “I didn’t see his arm” is a useful sentence.
  3. Put what you heard later below a line. Draw a line and write underneath it what other people said, what a clip showed and what you were asked.
  4. Ask questions that leave the answer open. Ask “What did you see right before?” or “What was at the corner?” and leave out the detail you expect.
  5. Don’t count agreement as proof. When everyone ends up telling the same version, ask where it came from, since agreeing after talking it over doesn’t settle what was there.

How to tell it’s working. When you compare stories afterward, count the details in your memory that came from your own page and the ones you picked up later. If the first page makes them easy to sort, keep writing first.

When it gets hard. Opening the chat first is tempting when everyone is talking about it. Turn notifications off for ten minutes and write your page before you look.

Good to know. This is about everyday memory, not legal advice. If someone was hurt or something serious happened, tell your coach or a parent right away, and let them handle what comes next.
