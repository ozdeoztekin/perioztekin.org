A Dating Filter Cannot Tell You the Whole Person

Dating Preferences & Attraction

A trait on your list is much simpler than the person carrying it, and meeting adds what the label leaves out.

The Science

  • In the abstract, "ambitious" can mean drive or purpose; in daily life, ambition also comes with a way of treating people and spending time.
  • What people say they want and whom they actually like face to face can be weakly related, though that does not make every filter pointless.
  • A genuine life requirement has another job: if you want children, whether the other person does can matter even if attraction is strong.

The Protocol

  • Separate two questions: "Does this fit a life I want to build?" and "Do I think this trait will make me attracted to this person?"
  • Keep your genuine requirements, and treat a guess about attraction as something you still need information about.
  • When you meet, notice how the trait shows up: ambition as enthusiasm you enjoy, or constant competition that wears you out.

See it in real life

Example

Every box checked on the river walk

A year out of high school, Greta keeps a list on her phone. It says ambitious, funny and into old movies, and under those, someone who’s fine with her weekends going to the flower shop, since Saturdays are weddings and she plans to run it with her aunt one day. Her friend Eliza says Federico, who graduated with them, checks every box. He runs his own lawn business and wants to hire two people by summer.

Their first walk along the river left Greta tired in a way she couldn’t explain. He took two calls from customers, and when she mentioned the shop’s new delivery van, he talked about his own longer hours. She still said yes to a second walk, because on paper he’s exactly what she wanted.

On a list, “ambitious” can mean drive and purpose, but in a person it also comes with a way of treating people and spending time, so the same word can feel very different. What people say they want and whom they end up liking face to face can be only loosely related, though that doesn’t make every filter pointless. A requirement about the life she wants to build answers a different question from a guess about attraction.

Before the second walk, she splits the list. The weekends line stays as a requirement, and it holds, since Federico works weekends too. “Ambitious” becomes a guess she still needs information about, and this time she watches what his ambition looks like, enthusiasm she enjoys or competition that wears her out.

She likes him most when he talks about the trees he planted behind the library. The rest of the time he compares his hours with hers and checks his phone at every bench. On the bus home she texts that she had a nice time but doesn’t feel a spark, and wishes him luck with the hiring. Then she sets her alarm for five, because Saturday is a wedding.

  1. Where it stallsHe checks every box on her list, and their first walk wears her out.
  2. What changes itShe keeps her weekend requirement and treats ambitious as a guess to check.
  3. What happens nextOn the second walk she watches how it shows up, then texts him kindly.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

One word, two people

This works for anyone you might spend time with, like a new friend, a teammate or a date. Have somewhere to write three numbers.

  1. Before you read the two descriptions below, rate from 0 to 10 how much you’d enjoy spending an afternoon with someone described only as ambitious.
  2. Read Person A and rate an afternoon with them the same way.
  3. Read Person B and rate them too.
  4. Compare the three numbers, and notice what the word alone left out.
Person AAmbitious. Training for a half marathon and saving to start a bike repair business. When you mention your own plans, asks follow-up questions and remembers them the next time you meet.
Person BAmbitious. Training for a half marathon and saving to start a bike repair business. Turns most conversations into a contest over whose week was harder, and cancels plans when something better for the business comes up.

Both people are made up, and both fit the same word.

What you’ll probably notice. The word on its own probably got a middling number, and the two people who both fit it got very different ones. A trait on a list is much simpler than the person carrying it, because the list leaves out how the trait shows up alongside everything else about them. Treating a trait you hope for as a guess leaves room for meeting someone to answer it.

Step by step

Keep requirements, test guesses

Your list can still be useful when each line does the right job.

  1. Write your list down. Put down what you’re looking for in someone you’d date, as truthfully as you can.
  2. Mark your requirements. For each line, ask, “Does this fit a life I want to build?” Something like whether you both want children someday, or whether they’re fine with how your weekends are spent, can matter even if attraction is strong.
  3. Mark the guesses. For the rest, ask, “Do I think this trait will make me attracted to this person?” Lines like ambitious, funny or tall often belong here.
  4. Keep requirements, hold guesses loosely. Don’t drop a real requirement to seem open. Treat each guess as something you still need information about.
  5. Notice how a trait shows up. When you spend time with someone, watch what the trait means in what they do, like ambition as enthusiasm you enjoy or as constant competition that wears you out.

How to tell it’s working. After meeting someone, write next to each guess what it looked like in person and how you felt. Over time, notice which guesses held up and which ones the label couldn’t settle.

When it gets hard. Someone can check every box and still feel wrong, or miss a guess and feel right. Neither one means your list was useless. It means meeting them told you something the list couldn’t.

Good to know. Keep early meetings in public places, and let someone you trust know where you’ll be. Anyone who pressures you or keeps going after a no has answered a more important question than anything on your list.
