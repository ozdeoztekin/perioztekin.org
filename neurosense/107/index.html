Why Some Muscles Get Weaker the More You Use Them

The Neuromuscular Junction & Myasthenia Gravis

Some people notice their eyelids drooping by evening, or their arms giving out after a few easy repetitions. In some conditions this is not normal tiredness. The message from nerve to muscle is getting blocked.

The Science

  • Gilhus (2016), New England Journal of Medicine: in most people with myasthenia gravis, antibodies target acetylcholine receptors at the neuromuscular junction, where nerve meets muscle, so the muscle picks up less of the nerve signal.
  • The hallmark is fatigable weakness: strength drops with repeated use and recovers with rest, often affecting the eyes first.
  • Howard (2018), Annals of the New York Academy of Sciences: these antibodies can also switch on complement, part of the immune system, which damages the junction and worsens signal loss.

The Protocol

  • Learn the pattern: weakness that worsens with use and improves with rest.
  • Watch for early eye signs such as drooping lids or double vision.
  • Do not treat true, fluctuating weakness as something to push through.
  • Seek a medical evaluation for an accurate diagnosis.

See it in real life

Example

Every photo after dinner

Lorenzo noticed it in the photos first. He takes most of the family’s dinner pictures, and in every one taken after about eight this week, his sister Giulia’s left eyelid hangs halfway down. In the breakfast photos, it looks fine.

Giulia is home after her first year of college. On Tuesday she couldn’t finish braiding their little sister’s hair because her arms kept dropping, and after five minutes on the couch, she finished it. On Wednesday night the TV looked doubled, and she blamed her contacts. She says she’s tired, and she plans to start running to get her energy back.

Every movement starts with a nerve sending a chemical signal across a narrow gap to a muscle, which catches it with special receptors. In some conditions, including one called myasthenia gravis, the immune system attacks those receptors, so the signal gets sent but fewer are left to catch it. The usual sign is weakness that gets worse with use and better with rest, often showing up first in the eyes as a drooping lid or double vision. Lorenzo has no idea what’s going on with Giulia, and it isn’t his job to figure it out, but it’s the kind of pattern nobody should push through.

For a week he keeps a note on his phone, with the time, what she was doing, which muscle, how long rest took to bring it back and anything about her eyes. By Sunday there are six entries. He shows her a breakfast photo next to an after-dinner one, along with the list. She reads it twice, stops blaming it on being tired and goes to find their mom.

At Friday’s appointment, Giulia hands the doctor the list before she says anything. The doctor reads every line and orders tests, and the running waits until they know more.

  1. Where it stallsGiulia’s eyelid droops in every evening photo, and her arms give out mid-braid.
  2. What changes itHe keeps a week of notes on the time, the muscle, what rest did and her eyes.
  3. What happens nextGiulia hands the doctor the list, and tests are ordered.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

The egg-carton junction

This is a hands-on model of the gap between nerve and muscle. You’ll need an empty egg carton with 12 cups, about 20 dried beans or pieces of dry pasta, and some paper and tape.

  1. The carton is the muscle’s side of the gap, and each cup is a receptor. The beans are the chemical signal the nerve sends.
  2. Set the carton flat on a table, drop all 20 beans at once from about a foot above it and count how many land in cups.
  3. Gather the beans and tape paper over 8 of the 12 cups, which stand for receptors the immune system has attacked.
  4. Drop the same 20 beans from the same height and count again.
  5. Do each version two more times and compare the totals.

What you’ll probably notice. The nerve sent the same signal every time, with the same beans and the same drop. With most cups covered, far fewer land, so the muscle gets a weaker message. That’s the problem at the junction in conditions like myasthenia gravis, where the signal is sent but fewer receptors are left to catch it. It’s only a model, since it shows where the message gets lost and leaves out why the weakness grows with use.

Step by step

Know the pattern

This is for understanding and noticing, and diagnosing is a doctor’s job.

  1. Sketch the handshake. Draw a nerve ending, the narrow gap and the muscle with its receptors, and label the signal crossing and the catch.
  2. Learn the pattern. Watch for weakness that gets worse with use and better with rest, out of proportion to the effort, after a few easy movements rather than a hard workout.
  3. Know the eye signs. Look for a drooping eyelid or double vision, especially later in the day, since the eyes are often the first place it shows.
  4. Write down what you see. Whether it’s you or someone else, note the time, what they were doing, which muscle and what rest did. Photos help.
  5. Take it to a doctor. Don’t push through true weakness that comes and goes. Bring the notes to a doctor, with a parent or another trusted adult.

How to tell it’s working. Without notes, you can draw the junction and say in one sentence why weakness that grows with use is different from being tired. You also know what to tell a doctor, including when it happens, which muscle and what rest does.

When it gets hard. The hardest part is someone who insists they’re only tired. Don’t argue about what it is. Show them what you wrote down, and ask them to show a doctor instead of pushing harder.

Good to know. New, unexplained or fluctuating muscle weakness, a drooping eyelid or double vision should be checked by a doctor promptly, so tell a parent or another trusted adult.
