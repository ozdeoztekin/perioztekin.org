When Noise Helps You Focus

White Noise & Attention

Silence is not always the cleanest focus setting. Sometimes a little steady noise makes the signal easier to hold. Your auditory system keeps sampling the room, even when you are trying not to listen.

The Science

  • The auditory system keeps sampling the environment even while you work.
  • In one small brain-scan study of 20 young men, soft white noise mixed into spoken words helped them hold the words in mind, a working memory task, better than quiet did.
  • Steady, low level sound may help by masking sudden distractions without becoming a target of attention, while louder noise can hurt.

The Protocol

  • Use plain, steady sound at low volume for one work block.
  • Stop if the sound becomes interesting or pulls attention.
  • Keep volume conservative, and do not turn focus sound into sleep sound.

See it in real life

Example

Every click in a silent house

The summer drawing program at the art college downtown wants ten portfolio pieces and one essay of 500 words or fewer, titled “Why do you make art?”, due on Friday. Hazel’s ten pieces are photographed and uploaded. After two hours at her desk, the essay has 140 words, and she has rewritten the first sentence seven times.

Everyone else is at her brother’s swim meet, and the house is so quiet she can hear the kitchen clock. Silence was supposed to be the easy part. But the fridge shudders off, the radiator ticks and the cat’s bell jingles down the hall, and each time she jumps and loses the sentence she was halfway through. A playlist was worse, because by the second song she was mouthing the words.

Her hearing keeps checking the room while she writes. In a silent house a small sound has nothing to hide in, so each one gets noticed. Holding a half-built sentence uses working memory, and each interruption can knock it loose. Steady, plain sound at a low volume can cover sharp surprises without becoming something to listen to, and in some studies low-level white noise even helped working memory.

At four she picks one plain brown noise track, sets the laptop volume low, just loud enough to hear, and starts a 30-minute timer. There’s no melody and no voices. She goes back to the third paragraph, the one about drawing her little brother asleep on the couch.

The radiator keeps ticking somewhere under the rush of the track, and when the fridge shudders off, the sentence she’s in the middle of survives. When the timer goes off at 4:30, the essay has 420 words and a first sentence she leaves alone.

Try it yourself · 2 minutes

Count the surprises

You’ll need a fan or a plain noise track, like white noise, brown noise or rain without thunder, and the counter below or a pen and scrap paper. Keep the volume low the whole time.

  1. Sit somewhere ordinary, like your room or the kitchen, and rest your eyes on one spot.
  2. For one minute, tap the counter or make a mark every time a sound grabs your attention, like a car, a door, a voice or the fridge, and write the number down.
  3. Turn on the steady sound at a low volume, just loud enough to hear, and reset the counter.
  4. Do another minute and count again.
  5. If you caught yourself listening to the sound itself, it’s too loud or too interesting, so try a plainer one or turn it down.

The timer needs JavaScript. A phone stopwatch works just as well.

What you’ll probably notice. With the fan or noise track on, the count usually drops, even though the same cars and doors are still out there. Your ears were checking the room the whole time, and the steady sound made the sudden ones stand out less. If your count went up instead, the sound was too loud or too interesting for you. Plain and low is the setting that tends to help.

Step by step

One block with steady sound

It works best for tasks where you hold things in your head, like a sentence you’re building, a problem or a plan.

  1. Pick a plain, steady sound. Choose a fan, white noise or brown noise, with no melody, no voices and nothing that changes. Pick it before you start, so the search doesn’t eat your block.
  2. Keep it low. Make it just loud enough to soften the sudden sounds around you. If you’d have to raise your voice to talk over it, it’s too loud.
  3. Use it for one work block. Pick one task and about 30 minutes. When the block ends, turn the sound off and take your break in ordinary sound.
  4. Stop if it gets interesting. If you notice you’re listening to the sound instead of the work, turn it down or switch it off.
  5. Keep it out of bedtime. Save your focus sound for work, and use something different, or nothing, at night.

How to tell it’s working. Do one block in your usual quiet and one with steady sound, on the same kind of task at about the same time of day. Count something, like words written, problems finished or times you lost your place, and after a few sessions, notice which rooms and tasks it helps.

When it gets hard. Some days the sound will start to feel loud or annoying. That’s a cue to lower it or go without, not to hunt for a more exciting track.

Good to know. Keep the volume gentle, especially in earbuds, since loud sound over long stretches is hard on your hearing.
