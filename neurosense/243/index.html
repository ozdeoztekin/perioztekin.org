The First Number Can Change What Feels Reasonable

Anchoring & Insufficient Adjustment

The first number you hear can become your starting point, and you often adjust away from it too little.

The Science

  • Anchoring: the first number you meet can become your starting point, and you adjust up or down from it to reach your own estimate.
  • You may judge the anchor too low or too high and adjust, but often not far enough, so a different starting number can lead to a different answer.
  • The pull can work even when the starting number is arbitrary, and knowing about anchoring alone may do little to loosen it.

The Protocol

  • For a project deadline, write down your own time estimate before anyone says how long it should take.
  • Note the reasons behind your number, so your starting point is your reasoning rather than someone else's figure.
  • When another figure comes up, adjust from your written estimate rather than starting over from theirs.

See it in real life

Example

His number before hers

The greyhound sweater was supposed to take a week. Ms. Albers next door had called it “a weekend project, tops,” so Kemal said a week to be safe, and he finished it a month later while her dog, Pancake, shivered through the first cold mornings.

Now she wants two crocheted puppies for her twins’ birthday, small gray copies of Pancake, and she has asked him over on Saturday to “figure out timing.” He can already hear how it will go. She’ll say a number first, and he’ll agree to something close to it.

That pull is called anchoring. The first number you hear can become the starting point for your own estimate, and you adjust up or down from it, but often not far enough, so your answer stays tilted toward a number you never chose. It works even when the first number is arbitrary, and knowing about it may do little to loosen it. Her weekend had never been based on anything, and his week was her weekend plus a little.

What he can change is where he starts. On Thursday night he crochets one puppy’s head and times it, counts the pieces in the pattern and checks how many free evenings his week has. Before anyone can say a number, he writes his own on an index card, five weeks, with the reasons under it, about seven hours a puppy, three or four free hours a week and two evenings for sewing.

On Saturday Ms. Albers says, “Two weeks? They’re so small.” He feels the pull of “two” right away and looks at his card. The party is a month off, and the long weekend before it adds the hours he’s short, so he moves his own date in by a week, for a reason he can name. On the Friday before the party, two gray puppies with floppy ears sit in a gift bag by her door.

  1. Where it stallsHe promised a dog sweater in a week, and it took a month.
  2. What changes itHe times one piece and writes his estimate before she names a number.
  3. What happens nextHe adjusts from his own card, and the puppies arrive on time.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

Same book, two numbers

Find a thick book whose page count nobody in the house knows, like a dictionary or a cookbook, and two people who can each answer without hearing the other.

  1. Before you ask anyone, look at the closed book, write your own guess of the page count and fold the paper.
  2. Show the first person the closed book and ask, “Does it have more or fewer than 150 pages?” Then ask for their best guess and write it down.
  3. Somewhere the first person can’t hear, show the second person the book and ask, “More or fewer than 1,500 pages?” Then ask for their best guess.
  4. Open to the last numbered page and compare all three guesses with the true count.

What you’ll probably notice. Often the person who heard 1,500 guesses higher than the one who heard 150, even though both looked at the same book and both numbers were only part of the question. Each one started from the number you said and adjusted away from it, but not far enough. Your folded guess started from nothing but your own look at the book, which is the whole idea behind writing your estimate first.

Step by step

Write your number before theirs

Use it for any project someone else will put a date on, like a commission, a group project or a shift you’re covering.

  1. Write your estimate before anyone talks. As soon as you hear about the project, and before anyone says how long it should take, write down how long you think it will take you.
  2. Build it from one piece you can time. Time one small part, count how many parts there are and check how many free hours your week has.
  3. Write the reasons under the number. With the reasons on paper, your starting point is your own reasoning, and a figure someone says later has something to push against.
  4. Adjust from yours, not from theirs. When another number comes up, ask what it’s based on. Move from your written estimate only as far as a good reason takes you, like a deadline you didn’t know about or extra time over a long weekend.
  5. Compare it with what happened. When the project is done, put your estimate next to how long it took. The gap tells you how to set the next one.

How to tell it’s working. Keep each estimate and the true time side by side for your next few projects. If the gap shrinks and the dates you give start to hold, keep writing yours first.

When it gets hard. Someone else’s number can feel more official than yours, especially from an adult or a customer. You don’t have to argue with it. Start from your card and say what your number is based on.
