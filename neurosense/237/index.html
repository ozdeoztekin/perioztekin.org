Do You Have to Feel Happy About This?

Mixed Emotions & Grief

Happy and sad are not two ends of one dial, so a milestone can hold joy and loss at the same time.

The Science

  • Positive and negative emotions are not two ends of one dial. They tend to run independently, so you can feel both at once.
  • A possible account of grief: close bonds can carry an automatic expectation that the person will always be there, which facts alone do not switch off.
  • Moving back and forth between the loss and ordinary life can be part of healthy grieving, and being able to feel both can be part of coping well.

The Protocol

  • When a laugh breaks through on a hard day, let it, without forcing yourself to keep it going.
  • If guilt follows a moment of joy, remind yourself that a warm moment does not take the loss away.
  • Allow yourself to move back and forth between the loss and ordinary life, instead of holding to one feeling.

See it in real life

Example

No whistle after his name

When the principal reads Emeka’s name, there’s clapping and his aunt shouts, but the sound he’s listening for doesn’t come. At every game and concert since he was six, his grandfather whistled through two fingers loud enough to turn heads, and he had promised to do it at graduation. He died in February.

Before the ceremony, his friend Tunde whispered that the microphone sounded like a goose, and Emeka laughed out loud. A moment later the laugh felt like a betrayal. Now his eyes keep searching the bleachers for his grandfather’s green hat, and he texts his mom that he might skip the party at his aunt’s.

Happy and sad at once can feel wrong, but they aren’t two ends of one dial. They tend to run independently, and being able to feel both can be part of coping well. One possible explanation for grief’s mixed feelings involves attachment. A close bond can carry an automatic expectation that the person will always be there, which facts alone don’t switch off. Memory knows his grandfather is gone while that belief keeps running, which may be why his eyes keep searching, and why a laugh can bring guilt.

He goes to the party for his mom. In the backyard his little cousin tries their grandfather’s whistle, cheeks puffed, and gets out a wet hiss. The laugh is out before he can catch it, and this time he leaves it alone, without pushing it to keep going. When the guilt comes, he tells himself that a warm moment doesn’t take the loss away. Later he sits on the porch steps holding the green hat, then goes back in for cake.

In the family photo he’s laughing, wearing the hat. That night he shows his grandmother a video of the hiss, and she laughs until she cries, and then she cries for a while, and then she asks to see it again.

  1. Where it stallsHe laughs before the ceremony, feels like a traitor and plans to skip the party.
  2. What changes itWhen his cousin’s whistle fizzles, he laughs and doesn’t argue with it.
  3. What happens nextIn the family photo he’s laughing, wearing his grandfather’s green hat.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

One dial or two

Something to draw on is enough. Pick an ordinary bittersweet moment, like the last night of camp, a friend’s going-away party or the last game of a season, rather than a loss that still hurts.

  1. Draw a single line, write “sad” at the left end and “happy” at the right, and mark one spot for how that moment felt.
  2. Under it, draw two separate bars, one for happy and one for sad, each running from 0 to 10.
  3. Fill in both bars for the same moment.
  4. Compare the single mark with the two bars.

What you’ll probably notice. On the single line, a bittersweet moment usually ends up somewhere in the middle, which makes it look lukewarm. With two bars, many people fill both fairly high, because positive and negative feelings tend to run independently rather than canceling each other out. In grief, that’s how a laugh can sit next to a loss without taking it away.

Step by step

Let both feelings in

For a day that’s happy and hard at once, like a milestone with someone missing.

  1. Expect the day to be mixed. A graduation, a birthday or a first game can hold joy and loss at the same time. A mixed day doesn’t mean you’re feeling it wrong.
  2. When a laugh breaks through, let it. Don’t stop it, and don’t force it to keep going either. Let it be as big or as small as it is.
  3. Answer the guilt with one sentence. If guilt follows a moment of joy, remind yourself that a warm moment doesn’t take the loss away.
  4. Let yourself go back and forth. Spend some time with the loss, like a few minutes with a photo or something of theirs, and some time in ordinary life, without holding on to one feeling.

How to tell it’s working. At the end of a hard day, write down one warm moment and one heavy one. Over a few weeks, notice whether both kinds keep showing up and whether the warm ones come with less guilt.

When it gets hard. On some hard days nothing funny breaks through, and that’s fine, since this isn’t about making yourself happy. If the guilt sticks, say the sentence from step 3 out loud.

Good to know. Grief has no schedule, and it looks different for everyone. If it makes ordinary days hard to get through for weeks, or you ever feel you can’t keep yourself safe, go to a parent, the counselor at school or your doctor right away.
