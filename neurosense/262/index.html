Give Your Goal Something to Move Toward

Approach & Avoidance Goals

A goal to avoid something can hide much of your progress; a goal to do something gives you actions to count.

The Science

  • An avoidance goal defines success as preventing an unwanted outcome. An approach goal defines something you want to do or reach.
  • Tracking an avoidance goal can mean noting every temptation and every time the behavior did not happen; a completed action is an event you can count.
  • Achieving a wanted result can feel like accomplishment, while avoiding a bad result can feel like relief. Both responses can be useful.

The Protocol

  • Take a goal that only says stop or avoid, ask what purpose it serves, then name an action that moves you toward that purpose.
  • Make the action realistic and countable, such as "include a vegetable at lunch" in place of "avoid unhealthy meals."
  • Track completed instances, what you did rather than how motivated you felt, and keep any limits that still matter.

See it in real life

Example

Fourteen clicks and three on-ramps

Since her first drive on a learner’s permit, Elodie’s goal has been not to mess up, mostly so her mom never again grabs the handle above the passenger door. The road test is five weeks away, and the notes column in her practice log says “no problems” all the way down, except for one line, “merge on Route 9, honked at.”

Most drives end with her listing what didn’t go wrong. The good ones bring relief, but relief can’t tell her whether her lane changes are getting any better, and the drive she remembers best is still the one with the honk.

Hers is an avoidance goal, which defines success as preventing something unwanted. Tracking it means noticing every time nothing went wrong, so much of her progress stays invisible. An approach goal names something you want to do, and each time you do it, there’s an event you can count. The feelings can differ too, since reaching a wanted result can feel like accomplishment and avoiding a bad one can feel like relief, and both can be useful.

On Saturday she asks what the goal is for, which is changing lanes safely on her own by the test. That points to a single routine, a mirror check, a signal and a look over her shoulder before every lane change. Her mom clicks an old tally counter each time Elodie does all of it and says “shoulder” whenever she skips the look. The first-day rules stay, with her phone in the glove box and her speed never over the limit, since some outcomes still need avoiding.

After Sunday’s drive the counter says fourteen, and the three times her mom said “shoulder” were all on on-ramps, where Elodie was busy matching speed. On Monday evening they take the quiet on-ramp by the mall again and again. On Wednesday the counter says nineteen, and Elodie writes the number in the notes column of her log.

  1. Where it stallsEvery drive ends with a list of what didn’t go wrong.
  2. What changes itBefore each lane change she checks, signals and looks, and her mom counts.
  3. What happens nextThe misses cluster on on-ramps, and two drives later the counter says nineteen.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

What would you count?

Use a goal of your own, or borrow one from the list below, and have something to write on.

  1. Write down a goal that starts with stop, don’t or avoid.
  2. Under it, write what you would count tonight to show it went well today, as specifically as you can.
  3. Ask what the goal is for, and write one action that would move you toward that purpose, small enough to do today.
  4. Write what you’d count for the new action, and compare your two answers.
Avoid goalsAvoid unhealthy meals · Stop wasting the time after school · Don’t be late to practice
One rewrite“Avoid unhealthy meals” becomes “include a vegetable at lunch,” which either happened today or didn’t.

The meal example comes from the video. Try your own rewrite before you read this one.

What you’ll probably notice. The first answer is often fuzzy, because success there is something that didn’t happen, and a day without the problem looks a lot like any other day. The action gives you a plain yes or no, or a number, which is an event you can count. Neither goal is wrong, and some limits are worth keeping, but only one of them shows you what you did.

Step by step

Give the goal a direction

Wording alone won’t make a goal succeed, but it can change what you’re able to see and count.

  1. Find a goal that only says stop. Look for one that names only what not to do, like “avoid unhealthy meals” or “don’t mess up on the road.”
  2. Ask what it’s for. Write down the purpose behind it, the thing you were trying to protect or reach, so the new version still serves it.
  3. Name one realistic action. Choose something you can see happen that moves you toward that purpose, like “include a vegetable at lunch” in place of “avoid unhealthy meals.” Keep food goals about adding something, never about skipping meals.
  4. Count what you did. Keep a tally of completed actions, what you did rather than how motivated you felt. Once a week, look at the record to see where the plan is working and where it needs adjusting.
  5. Keep the limits that protect you. Some outcomes should still be avoided, like texting while driving. The new goal adds a direction and a way to see progress, and the protective rules stay.

How to tell it’s working. After a week, put your tally next to what you could have said about the old goal. If you can point to what you did and see where the misses cluster, adjust the action there and keep counting.

When it gets hard. Trading a hard negative goal for an impossible positive one doesn’t help. If your tally stays near zero, shrink the action until it fits an ordinary day.
