Why Can’t You Sleep After Hard Evening Practice?

Evening Exercise & Autonomic Recovery

Tired muscles and a settled nervous system are different things, and recovery continues after practice ends.

The Science

  • During exercise, sympathetic nerve activity helps your heart beat faster while the parasympathetic influence that normally slows it is reduced.
  • After you stop, sympathetic activity recedes and parasympathetic influence returns; this return, parasympathetic reactivation, helps slow the heart.
  • Harder exercise can delay parts of this recovery: a shorter, intense session can need more recovery than a longer, easy one, and fitness matters.

The Protocol

  • If you keep struggling to settle after hard practice, tell a parent and coach which sessions and how hard and long they were.
  • Ask if the hardest part can move earlier; if not, discuss skipping optional hard extras; do not change required training yourself.
  • Keep your usual opportunity for enough sleep instead of staying awake a set number of hours after practice.

See it in real life

Example

Eight more sprints on Thursday nights

Ulla’s legs feel finished by the end of Thursday practice, yet she’s still awake at midnight. Thursday is race-pace night at her swim club, ending with all-out 50s off the blocks, and since September she has stayed after with Kaia for the optional extras, eight more sprints.

On Tuesdays, when the team swims longer and steadier, she falls asleep fast. After Thursdays she lies in the dark with her heart still thumping, and Fridays drag. Her fix was two more sprints, figuring that if she came home wrecked enough, sleep would have to come.

Tired muscles and a settled nervous system are different things. During hard effort, sympathetic nerves help her heart beat faster while the parasympathetic influence that normally slows it is turned down. When she stops, sympathetic activity recedes and parasympathetic influence returns, helping slow her heart, but harder exercise can delay parts of that recovery, so a short, intense session can need more of it than a longer, easy one. Sleep usually brings more parasympathetic influence, so late all-out sprints can leave recovery overlapping her usual settling time, and more exhaustion doesn’t automatically prepare her for sleep.

With her mom there, she tells Coach Jovan which practices come before the restless nights and how hard and long they are, twenty minutes at race pace plus the extras. When she asks whether the race-pace set could come earlier, he says the blocks are free only in the last part of the team’s pool time, but the extras are optional, so the three of them move hers to Saturday morning. Everything required stays, and so does her lights-out at ten.

The first Thursday without extras, it still takes her a while to drop off. Over the next three, by her rough guess, she’s asleep within half an hour, though she can’t be sure the sprints were the whole story. On Saturday mornings she swims them with Kaia.

  1. Where it stallsHer legs feel finished after Thursday practice, but her heart keeps thumping.
  2. What changes itWith her mom and her coach, she moves the optional sprints to Saturday.
  3. What happens nextOn most Thursdays after that, she’s asleep within about half an hour.

Try it yourself · 10 minutes

Long and easy, short and brisk

Do this during the day with a little floor space, a chair and the stopwatch below. Keep both rounds well short of your limit, and skip the brisk one if you have a heart or breathing condition or feel dizzy.

  1. Sit for a minute with one hand flat on your chest and notice how your heartbeat feels at rest.
  2. March in place for two minutes at an easy pace you could chat through. Then sit, start round 1 and stop it when your heartbeat feels about the way it did at rest.
  3. Once you’ve settled, march for one minute at a brisk pace, knees higher and arms swinging, while you can still say a full sentence. Sit, start round 2 and stop it once your heartbeat feels like it did at rest again.
  4. Compare the two times, and think back to how your legs felt while you waited each time.

The timer needs JavaScript. A phone stopwatch works just as well.

What you’ll probably notice. The brisk round was half as long, yet it often takes longer to settle, and your legs probably felt fine well before your heartbeat did. During effort, sympathetic nerves speed the heart while the parasympathetic brake on it eases off. After you stop, that brake comes back over time, which is called parasympathetic reactivation, and harder work can delay parts of it, though fitness matters too. How your legs feel and how settled you are can be two separate readings, and after a long, hard practice the legs may say finished while the rest of you is still settling.

Step by step

Plan the hard part with your coach

For any sport with evening practice you don’t schedule yourself. The research behind it comes from adults, from small groups of trained or active men and from active adults wearing trackers, so it sets no timing rule for anyone your age.

  1. Notice which sessions come before restless nights. For a couple of weeks, jot down each evening session’s hardest part, how long it lasted and roughly how long you took to settle that night. Look at how hard the sessions were as well as when they ended.
  2. Tell a parent and your coach. If trouble settling keeps following the harder sessions, tell them which ones, how hard they were and how long. “Thursdays, the last twenty minutes at race pace, plus extras” tells them more than “practice ends late.”
  3. Ask whether the hardest part can move earlier. If the schedule has room, the hardest set might fit earlier in the session or on a day when practice starts earlier. Your coach knows what the training plan needs.
  4. If practice is fixed, talk about the extras. Discuss skipping or moving optional hard extras, such as added sprints after practice. Required training stays as your coach planned it, and any change is decided with your coach and a parent.
  5. Keep your usual chance for enough sleep. Go to bed at your usual time, rather than staying up a set number of hours after practice to let your body settle. More exhaustion isn’t automatically better preparation for sleep.

How to tell it’s working. Keep the same rough notes for a few weeks after any change, and compare how long you took to settle after hard sessions before and after it. Look at the pattern with your coach, since any one night can go either way.

When it gets hard. Extras can feel like the reason you’re getting faster, so dropping them feels like falling behind. Ask your coach where that work fits better, so the change is about timing rather than effort.

Good to know. No watch or app reading decides when you’re ready to sleep. If you lie awake after easy days too, or you get chest pain, dizziness or a racing heart that doesn’t settle with rest, tell a parent right away and get checked by a doctor.
