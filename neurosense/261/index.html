Fix the Rules Before Asking for Teamwork

Reward Rules & Team Cooperation

When helping a teammate can lower your own rank, the reward rule itself can work against teamwork.

The Science

  • In a strongly competitive ranking, another person getting better can feel like you are getting worse, even if your own work has not changed.
  • When the immediate goal is avoiding the bottom of the ranking, a creative experiment may look like a chance to be eliminated.
  • So a team can contain capable people and still lose useful information; the problem is partly what the situation makes costly.

The Protocol

  • Before a group task, ask: "If I help you succeed, does the way we are evaluated make that a loss for me?"
  • If you control the rules, give the group a shared outcome and count useful contributions, like shared explanations and handoffs.
  • Then give information room to move: let people contribute and share the conversation, not only defend their position.

See it in real life

Example

The names come off the whiteboard

The points board was Thiago’s idea for making the captains’ practices tougher. Until official practice starts in November, he and the other captain run open gym, and three weeks ago he started writing every player’s scrimmage points on the whiteboard, with the bottom three running lines at the end.

Now the scrimmages look like ten one-on-one games at once. Nobody passes out of a double team, and the backdoor cut from Monday’s drills hasn’t shown up once. On Tuesday Thiago yelled “move the ball” until people started saying it back to him, and he counted two assists in forty minutes.

In a ranking like his, a teammate getting better can feel like you getting worse, even when your own game hasn’t changed. A pass that sets up a layup helps the team but also lifts the player you’re trying to stay ahead of, and when the goal is staying out of the bottom three, the new cut looks like a chance to miss and land there. So capable players keep their passes and tips to themselves, and another speech may miss the cause, which is partly the board.

Before Thursday’s scrimmage he asks himself, “If I help you score, does the way we’re counted make that a loss for me?” On his board it does, so he erases the names. Now the board shows two team scores, the losing team runs together, and every pass that leads to a basket earns its team a tally, so passing helps the passer too. Between games each team huddles for a minute, and he asks the players who never talk what they’ve noticed.

Gael, a freshman who has barely spoken all fall, says the other team’s wing keeps jumping into the passing lane. They run the backdoor cut behind him twice, and both times it ends in a layup. The losers still run, complaining all the way, and after forty minutes the board holds eleven tallies.

  1. Where it stallsHis points board turns scrimmages into one-on-one games with two assists.
  2. What changes itHe erases the names, scores the teams and tallies passes that lead to baskets.
  3. What happens nextA freshman spots an opening, and the board ends with eleven tallies.

Try it yourself · 10 minutes

Rank round, team round

Play this with one other person, like a teammate or a parent. Each of you needs paper and a pencil, and the timer below keeps the rounds even.

  1. In round one, whoever finds more words wins. Start the timer, and each of you writes as many words as you can make from the letters in SANDWICHES. Either of you may call out a word you’ve found, if you want to.
  2. In round two, your two counts are added into one team score. Start the timer again and make words from WATERMELON, with the same freedom to call words out.
  3. Afterward, tell each other how many words you called out in each round.

The timer needs JavaScript. A phone stopwatch works just as well.

What you’ll probably notice. Often fewer words get called out in the ranking round, because a word you share there helps the person you’re trying to beat. In the team round, the same word adds to a score you both own, so giving it away costs you nothing. Nobody’s character changed between the rounds, only what the rule made helping cost.

Step by step

Check the rule before the speech

Bring it to your next group task, like a team practice, a club event or a group project.

  1. Ask the cost question first. Before the task starts, ask, “If I help you succeed, does the way we’re judged make that a loss for me?” If the answer is yes, holding back makes sense, and the rule is the place to start.
  2. Give the group a shared result. If you set the rules, score the group as a whole, with one team score or one finished project that everyone owns. Competition between teams can stay.
  3. Count the useful handoffs. Make passes that lead to baskets, shared notes and clear explanations part of what the group counts and praises, so helping someone else also helps you.
  4. Give information room to move. Make space for everyone to say what they’ve noticed, and ask the quiet ones directly, so the talk isn’t only people defending their own spots.
  5. Watch for coasting. A shared score alone doesn’t stop someone from easing off. Give each person a part the others can see, and keep counting contributions.

How to tell it’s working. Count something you can see, like passes that lead to baskets or ideas shared out loud, in one session under the old rule and one under the new rule. If the count goes up and fewer people sit back, keep the new rule.

When it gets hard. You may not control the rules. Ask the cost question anyway and bring it to whoever sets them, since another pep talk about teamwork may miss the cause.
