How to Force Your Brain to Lock In (in 60 Seconds)

Visual Focus

When rereading does not work, the problem may be the visual path, not your character.

The Science

  • Rayner (Psychological Bulletin, 1998): in a review of 20 years of research, your eyes don't move smoothly across a page; they jump in quick movements called saccades and pause in fixations, and they take in the words mostly during the pauses.
  • Buschman & Miller (Science, 2007): recording from monkeys, they found that when a goal guided attention, prefrontal neurons responded first, and when an eye-catching target grabbed it, parietal neurons led; attention is steered both by your goals and by whatever stands out.
  • Sara (Nature Reviews Neuroscience, 2009): a review of how the locus coeruleus, a small brainstem nucleus, releases noradrenaline throughout the brain and helps set how alert and focused you are.

The Protocol

  • Put a blank card under the line you are reading. The card hides the rest of the page from the visual field.
  • If your eyes keep wandering, run a pen tip under the line to keep your place.
  • After each paragraph, stop and say the main idea in one plain sentence out loud. Forces comprehension before continuing.
  • Use it for one page first. Track whether rereading drops.

See it in real life

Example

Figure 2 by Monday morning

On Monday morning, at her first lab meeting, Amara has to explain Figure 2 to a room of people who do this for a living. She’s two weeks into a summer research program, and the assigned paper is about how zebrafish regrow their fins.

Sunday night, page three of the printout is yellow from top to bottom, and she has read the paragraph behind Figure 2 four times and still can’t say what it says. The program’s group chat is open on her laptop, her sister’s show is on in the next room, and her eyes keep sliding off the page.

Her eyes move along a line in small jumps and stops, and between jumps her attention can drift to anything else in view. Two kinds of attention are competing: the kind she aims, and the kind grabbed by anything bright or moving. A page that’s all highlighter, a chat and a flickering screen give the grabbing kind plenty of targets.

She closes the laptop and holds her eyes on the clock above the stove for sixty seconds. Then she slides an index card under the line she’s reading, runs her pen tip along under the words, and ends each paragraph by saying its point out loud: “They cut the fins, then counted new cells every day for a week.” Every time she goes back, a tally mark goes on the card. She tries it on page four first.

Page four gets one mark. She finishes the last six pages in half an hour, with three marks total. On Monday she explains Figure 2 in about a minute, answers the first question without checking her notes, and goes home with the next paper.

  2. What changes itSixty seconds on the stove clock, then a card and a pen
  3. What happens nextThree rereads in six pages, and Figure 2 explained in a minute

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

Catch your eyes jumping

You need your phone’s front camera, or a friend who can watch your eyes from the side, plus a pen and any page of text.

  1. Prop the phone up at eye level, about an arm’s length away, and start recording yourself. Keep your head still the whole time.
  2. Sweep your eyes slowly from the far left of the room to the far right, as smoothly as you can, like a slow camera pan.
  3. Hold the pen up and move its tip slowly along the same path, following it with your eyes.
  4. Read three lines of the page, then stop recording and play it back.

What you’ll probably notice. The slow sweep usually comes out as a string of little hops, while following the pen tip looks much smoother. Reading is all hops and stops, with a long jump back at the start of each line. Those are the saccades and fixations described in The Science above, and every stop is a place your attention can land. A card under the line and a pen tip leave it fewer places to go. A minute of looking at one still point can be a simple way to settle in first; the evidence for that part is thin, so judge it by your tally marks.

Step by step

Settle your eyes, then read

Try it on one page before you decide anything.

  1. Clear what you can see. Close the laptop, put the phone out of sight, and turn so no moving screen is in view. Leave only the page.
  2. Hold one point for sixty seconds. Pick something small and still, a light switch or a doorknob, and hold your eyes there for one minute before you start reading.
  3. Card under the line, pen under the words. An index card or a folded sheet hides what’s below the line you’re on, and the pen tip gives your eyes one place to land as you go.
  4. Say each paragraph’s point out loud. One plain sentence before you go on. If you can’t, reread that paragraph once, on purpose, with the pen.
  5. Tally every reread. Make a mark on the card each time you go back, and compare a page done this way with a page done the old way.

How to tell it’s working. Do one page the old way and one with the card, counting rereads both times. If the card page has fewer marks and you can say each paragraph’s point from memory, keep the setup.

When it gets hard. The pen can feel like a leash for the first page or two. Let it move at your normal reading speed instead of racing it, and drop it once your eyes stay on the line without help.

Good to know. If words blur, double or swim on the page, or reading often gives you headaches, ask a parent about getting your eyes checked.
