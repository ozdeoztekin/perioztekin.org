Calmer Isn't Always the Goal

Emotion Regulation Goals

Calming down is just one of five goals you can have for a feeling, so pick the goal before the strategy.

The Science

  • Managing an emotion can aim at five goals: prevent an unwanted feeling, reduce a hard one, start one, keep one going, or boost one.
  • In a body budget metaphor, your brain helps manage your heart, lungs and gut, and one account ties that to feeling pleasant or not, calm or worked up.
  • Hard effort, like a demanding run partway through, can feel unpleasant. Nothing has to be wrong: you might just be doing something hard.

The Protocol

  • Before you act on a feeling, choose your goal: prevent an unwanted one, reduce a hard one, start one, keep one going or boost one.
  • Fit the strategy to the emotion, you and the moment. To lower it where you are, try breathing work or rethinking the situation.
  • Sometimes let a hard feeling stay long enough to consider what it is telling you, rather than removing it automatically.

See it in real life

Example

The burn around the tenth width

Every day of varsity tryouts ends with seventeens, seventeen sideline-to-sideline widths of the court in under seventy seconds, while Coach Mayfield writes times on a clipboard. Two days running, Leona has pulled up partway through when her lungs started burning, sure something was wrong with her, and jogged in well over the time.

Before each run she sits on the baseline with her knees bouncing, trying to calm everything at once, the nerves now and the burn she knows is coming. A real athlete, she figures, wouldn’t feel any of it.

Managing a feeling sounds like calming it down, but there are other goals too, like preventing an unwanted feeling, starting a feeling, keeping a good feeling going or making it stronger. A body budget is a useful picture here. Your brain helps manage your heart, lungs and gut, and in one account, that work shows up as feeling pleasant or unpleasant, calm or worked up. Hard effort spends from the budget, so partway through a hard run it can feel awful, and the feeling isn’t a precise reading of the balance. Nothing has to be wrong, since she might be doing something hard.

On the third day she picks a goal for each feeling first. She wants the shaking on the baseline lower, and since she can’t leave the gym, she uses what’s there, a few slow breaths and one rethought sentence, that this run tests today’s legs and not her. The burn she leaves alone. When it arrives around the tenth width, she checks what it’s telling her. Her legs are heavy and her lungs are burning, but nothing is sharp and she isn’t dizzy, so she holds her pace.

She touches the last line at sixty-eight seconds and bends over with her hands on her knees while everything burns for about a minute. Then it starts to fade. Coach Mayfield writes 68 next to her name.

  1. Where it stallsTwo days running, she pulls up the moment her lungs start burning.
  2. What changes itShe calms her nerves with slow breaths and leaves the burn alone.
  3. What happens nextShe finishes all seventeen widths in sixty-eight seconds.

Try it yourself · 2 minutes

Step up, check in

You’ll need a sturdy bottom stair and the timer below. Go at a steady pace you could keep up while talking, not a race, and skip this if an injury or a health condition limits exercise.

  1. Start the timer and step up and down on the bottom stair at a steady pace, holding the rail if there is one.
  2. Notice when it starts to feel unpleasant, in your legs or your breathing, and keep the same pace.
  3. Ask yourself whether something is wrong or you’re doing something hard. Heavy legs and fast breathing are the hard kind, and pain or dizziness means stop right away.
  4. For the last thirty seconds, choose your goal on purpose, either keep going and let the feeling stay or slow down to bring it lower.
  5. When the timer ends, sit down and notice how quickly the unpleasant feeling fades.

The timer needs JavaScript. A phone stopwatch works just as well.

What you’ll probably notice. It usually feels fine at first and turns unpleasant partway through, even though nothing has to be wrong. In the budget picture, that’s effort spending resources, and the feeling isn’t a precise reading of how much is left. Choosing a goal for the last thirty seconds turns slowing down or keeping going into a decision rather than a reflex, and either one can be the right call.

Step by step

Pick the goal, then the move

Use it before practice, a game, a shift or anything else that stirs you up.

  1. Name the feeling and the moment. Say what you feel and where you are, like “shaky on the baseline” or “legs burning halfway through.”
  2. Choose one of five goals. Decide whether you want to prevent the feeling, reduce it, start a different one, keep it going or make it stronger. Calming down is only one of the choices.
  3. Fit the move to the moment. If you need it lower and can’t leave, use something right there, like a few slow breaths or rethinking what the moment means.
  4. Ask whether it’s hard or wrong. During effort, heavy legs and burning lungs often mean you’re doing something hard. Sharp pain, chest pain or dizziness mean stop and tell someone.
  5. Let some hard feelings stay. Sometimes leave a hard feeling alone long enough to hear what it’s telling you, rather than getting rid of it automatically.

How to tell it’s working. For a week, jot down the feeling, the goal you picked and what you did. Count how often you chose something other than calming down, and whether the move fit the moment.

When it gets hard. In the moment, “calm down” will still jump out first. Keep one question ready, “What do I want this feeling to do right now?”

Good to know. Discomfort during effort can be normal, but pain is different. Stop and tell a coach or a parent if you have chest pain, feel dizzy or faint, can’t catch your breath or feel a sharp pain, and see a doctor if it happens again.
