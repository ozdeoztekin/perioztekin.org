The Nap Decision

nap timing and duration

A nap can save your day or steal your next hour, depending on how you use it.

The Science

  • Short naps can restore alertness without dropping too deeply into sleep.
  • Longer naps may help some kinds of learning, but they can bring heavier wake-up fog.
  • Late naps can also push against your nighttime sleep timing.

The Protocol

  • Choose a short alertness nap when you need to wake up clean.
  • Choose a longer planned nap only when the schedule can absorb grogginess.
  • Keep naps earlier when possible, so bedtime still has room.

See it in real life

Example

Twenty minutes on the couch before driving

There was still a pillow crease on Xavier’s cheek when he pulled away from the curb for Thursday’s driving lesson. He had lain down at four “for a few minutes” and woken up two minutes before six to a text from his instructor saying, “I’m outside.”

The lesson went the way he felt. On Elm Street he drifted toward the center line, and at the four-way stop he rolled right through with his eyes open and his head still back on the pillow. Mr. Kowalski’s foot hit the brake on his side before Xavier’s did. That night Xavier was still awake at one.

A nap can rescue an afternoon or eat the next hour, depending on how it’s used. A short one can bring alertness back without sinking deep into sleep. A long one can leave heavier fog when you wake up, and a late one can push against the night’s sleep, and his two hours starting at four did both.

On Tuesday, before he lies down, he asks what the nap is for, and the answer is waking up clear for a six o’clock lesson. So it’s short and early. He lies on the couch instead of his bed a little before four, with a twenty-minute alarm on a phone across the room, so he has to get up to stop it. Then he gives himself a runway, with the lights on, cold water on his face and one lap around the block.

At six he’s behind the wheel with nothing left of the nap but a stiff neck. He stops fully at all four signs on Elm Street, and Mr. Kowalski checks off left turns at the light for the first time. He’s asleep before eleven.

  1. Where it stallsHe naps for two hours from four, rolls a stop sign and is awake at one.
  2. What changes itHe naps twenty minutes on the couch and then takes a short runway.
  3. What happens nextHe stops fully on Elm Street and falls asleep before eleven.

Try it yourself · 2 minutes

The nap job check

This is a quick way to practice the decision before you’re tired. Read the five afternoons in the box and decide each one.

  1. For each afternoon, decide whether it calls for a short nap, a longer planned nap or no nap, and say what time you’d lie down.
  2. Name the nap’s job in a few words, like waking up clear, catching up or leaving bedtime alone.
  3. Check your calls against the note under the box, and where you disagree, look at what comes right after the nap.
  4. Then write down your own most common afternoon and decide it the same way.
AHome at 3:30, driving lesson at 6:00.
BSaturday after a sleepover, nothing planned until dinner at 7:00.
CHome at 3:45, practice at 4:15.
DHome at 6:30 after a long day, homework still to do, bed at 10:30.
EEvery nap this month has left you feeling worse.

Here’s one way to call them. For A, take a short nap right away and then a runway. For B, a longer nap is fine early in the afternoon, since nothing needs you right after. For C, rest quietly instead, since there’s no time for the fog to clear. For D, probably skip the nap, so bedtime keeps its room. For E, rest quietly and protect night sleep first.

What you’ll probably notice. The same tired afternoon can call for different answers, depending on what comes next and how close bedtime is. A short nap can keep you light enough to wake up clear, a longer one can bring heavier fog that needs time to lift, and a late one can push into the night. Asking about the nap’s job first is most of the skill.

Step by step

Give every nap a job

Decide before you lie down, not when the alarm goes off.

  1. Ask what job the nap has. Decide whether it’s for waking up clear for something soon, like a lesson, a shift or practice, or for catching up on a free afternoon, since the job decides the nap.
  2. Keep it short for alertness. Set the alarm before you lie down, and put the phone where you have to get up to stop it. If you still wake up foggy, make it shorter next time.
  3. Go long only with time to spare. A longer nap may help some kinds of learning, but it can bring heavier fog, so save it for days when nothing needs you for a while after you wake.
  4. Nap early, so bedtime has room. The later the nap, the more it can push into the night, so right after school is usually easier on bedtime than right before dinner.
  5. Give yourself a runway. Turn the lights on, splash cold water on your face and walk for a few minutes, and leave time for it before anything that needs a clear head, especially driving.

How to tell it’s working. For two weeks, note when you napped, for how long, how you felt fifteen minutes after waking and when you fell asleep that night. Compare the short, early naps with the long or late ones.

When it gets hard. “A few minutes” on your bed easily turns into two hours. Nap somewhere a little less cozy, with the alarm out of reach, and if naps keep leaving you worse off, switch to quiet rest with your eyes closed and protect your night sleep first.

Good to know. If you need a nap every day to get through, fall asleep when you don’t mean to or feel too sleepy to drive, tell a parent and see a doctor. Never drive while you still feel foggy.
