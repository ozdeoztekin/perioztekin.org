A One-Night Trip Across Time Zones: When Should You Sleep?

Short Trips & Clock Timing

Your body clock arrives still running on home time, and a very short trip may end before adapting helps.

The Science

  • In clock cells, activating proteins switch on genes for proteins called PER and CRY, which build up and slow their own production.
  • Neurons in the suprachiasmatic nucleus, the brain's central clock, coordinate their rhythms and help time alertness, melatonin and body temperature.
  • Light information from the eyes adjusts the phase (when the cycle occurs relative to the outside day), shifting a rhythm that already exists.

The Protocol

  • Before leaving, compare the trip's commitments with your usual sleep window, together with the adult traveling with you.
  • Translate that window into destination time. For illustration, 10 p.m. to 7 a.m. is 8 p.m. to 5 a.m. somewhere two hours behind.
  • If that timing fits and keeps the sleep you need, staying near home time may work; if not, plan an alternative with the adult.

See it in real life

Example

Atlanta time in a Denver hotel

Last spring, on an overnight family trip from Atlanta to Denver, Benedikt switched to local time the way everyone said to. He stayed up at his cousin’s graduation party until ten and woke at five, wide awake in the dark.

Now he’s flying back with his mom for a single night, to play in a young pianists’ competition. His slot is Sunday at 9:20 in the morning, with a flight home that afternoon, and he has a practice room on Saturday at half past eight in the evening.

His body clock isn’t a remembered bedtime. In clock cells, proteins called PER and CRY build up and slow their own production, then get broken down until production restarts, a loop that helps keep a roughly daily rhythm. Neurons in the brain’s central clock, the suprachiasmatic nucleus, coordinate those rhythms and help time alertness, melatonin and body temperature. Light from the eyes shifts the timing of a rhythm that’s already running, so a trip shorter than about two days may end before adapting helps. Last spring his body was still keeping Atlanta time, and five in Denver is seven in Atlanta, when he usually wakes.

So on Wednesday he and his mom put his usual sleep window beside the schedule. Ten at night to seven in the morning at home becomes eight in the evening to five in the morning in Denver. The slot lands in his body’s late morning and the flight fits, but the practice room falls inside the window, so his mom trades it for an afternoon one. They’ll keep to his home hours.

On Saturday he falls asleep soon after eight, Denver time, and wakes a little after five on his own, about nine hours later. He runs his scales on the bedspread with his fingers while his mom sleeps and is in the warm-up room long before his slot. On Monday he’s up with his usual alarm, as on any school day.

  1. Where it stallsOn a past one-night trip, local bedtime left him wide awake at five.
  2. What changes itWith his mom, he writes his home sleep window in Denver time.
  3. What happens nextHe sleeps from about eight to five there and plays his morning slot.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

The paper-clip clock

This is a tabletop model of the loop inside clock cells, and it leaves out almost everything else. You need six paper clips, a square drawn on a sheet of paper and a slow, steady count.

  1. Count slowly out loud, about one number a second, and on each count put one clip in the square. Each clip stands for a newly made PER or CRY protein.
  2. When all six are in, the brake is on, because the built-up proteins slow their own production. From the next count, take one clip out per count instead, the way they’re broken down.
  3. When the square is empty, the brake has lifted, so start adding again. Run three full loops and notice how many counts each one takes.
  4. Partway through the fourth loop, announce “landed, two hours behind” and keep following the same rules. Watch whether anything in the square changes.

What you’ll probably notice. Each loop takes the same number of counts, because the rules inside it set the pace and nothing outside tells it when to start over. Clock cells run a loop with this shape, building up, braking, breaking down and starting again, and in the brain’s central clock, neurons running these loops coordinate their rhythms. A new time on the wall doesn’t reset them. Light from the eyes can shift when the cycle happens, but it shifts a rhythm that’s already running, so on a trip of a night or two you may be home before that shift helps much.

Step by step

Plan sleep for a one-night trip

For a trip of a night or two across time zones, worked out before you leave with the adult who’s traveling with you.

  1. Write down your usual sleep window. Use the hours at home that give you enough sleep, for example 10 at night to 7 in the morning. Those nine hours show the arithmetic, not what everyone needs.
  2. Translate it into destination time. Work it out with the adult on the trip. Somewhere two hours behind home, 10 at night to 7 in the morning becomes 8 in the evening to 5 in the morning.
  3. Set the trip’s commitments beside it. List the event, any practice, meals and travel in destination time, and mark anything that falls inside the window.
  4. If it fits, consider staying near home time. For a visit shorter than about two days, the trip may end before adapting does much good, so keeping close to home hours can work, as long as it keeps the sleep you need.
  5. If it clashes, plan another way together. If something important lands inside the window, or the plan would mean forcing yourself to sleep, work out a different plan with the adult, like moving a practice time or taking a later flight, instead of cutting sleep short.

How to tell it’s working. After the trip, compare when you slept with the plan, and whether you got about as much sleep as on a usual night at home. Note one thing you’d change next time.

When it gets hard. Someone will tell you to switch to local time the minute you land, and a late dinner or an outing can make it tempting. Agree with the adult beforehand which plans matter most, so you aren’t deciding on the spot while you’re tired.

Good to know. This is general travel guidance, not a schedule tested in young people, and sleep needs vary with age. Keep the sleep you need rather than trimming it to match a clock, and if sleep trouble keeps coming back, at home or after trips, let a parent know so a doctor can look into it.
