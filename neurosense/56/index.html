Why Shady Behavior Makes You Feel Sick

The Moral Brain & Disgust

When someone does something so shady it actually turns your stomach, that is not just a figure of speech. Moral disgust seems to borrow the same brain hardware that protects you from spoiled food.

The Science

  • Chapman, Kim, Susskind and Anderson (2009), Science: people make the same facial disgust response to a bad taste, something gross, and an unfair offer, suggesting moral disgust grows out of the body's food rejection system.
  • Wicker et al. (2003), Neuron: feeling disgust and seeing someone else's disgust both activate the anterior insula, a shared hub for the feeling.
  • Schaich Borg, Lieberman and Kiehl (2008), Journal of Cognitive Neuroscience: moral judgments involving violations recruit insula and related circuitry that overlaps with basic disgust.
  • Net effect: that gut level yuck can act as a fast moral signal, though it is a starting flag for reflection, not a verdict.

The Protocol

  • When a situation feels gross, pause and name it rather than dismissing it.
  • Notice where you feel it in your body, then ask: is this a values signal or just discomfort?
  • Do not act on the impulse alone; log the trigger and the feeling first.
  • Protect any insight with rest or a low input reset instead of more scrolling.

See it in real life

Example

Ellie’s message in the group chat

Shira’s stomach turns before she finishes reading. Jonah has posted a screenshot to the group chat: a private message from Ellie, a girl in their grade, asking whether her song is any good. Under it he typed “she really sent me this,” and two people have laugh-reacted.

Shira’s thumbs move before she decides anything. “you’re actually disgusting, jonah” sits in the text box, and part of her wants all seven people to see it.

Disgust began as protection against spoiled food, and people make the same disgust face for a foul taste, something gross and an unfair offer. Feeling disgust and seeing it on someone else’s face both activate the anterior insula, and judging moral violations draws on overlapping circuits, so a betrayal can hit the body before the words arrive. That speed helps, but it can misfire, which makes the feeling a reason to look closer.

She takes her thumbs off the screen and names it: disgust, in her throat and stomach. What line did it notice? Ellie sent that to one person, and now it’s a joke for seven. Is that a values signal, or is Jonah’s humor just different from hers? She checks behavior, not vibes, and scrolls up: it’s the second private screenshot he’s posted this month. Before anything else, she writes in her notes app, “9:41, Jonah posted Ellie’s message, throat tight.”

She deletes the draft and sends Jonah one private message: “Ellie sent that just to you. Take it down?” She mutes the chat, leaves her phone in the hall and takes a long shower instead of watching the reactions. By 7:10 the next morning the screenshot is gone, and the chat is back to arguing about who has the biology notes.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

The yuck face

You need a mirror or your phone’s front camera. Read the lines in the box slowly, one at a time.

  1. Hold the mirror or camera so you can see your nose and mouth. Read the first line and watch your face.
  2. Notice where the reaction lands in your body (throat, stomach, chest, face) and jot one word. Do the same for each line.
  3. Compare the first three. Did your face do the same thing for the sour milk as for the unfair split?
  4. For the last line, ask the question from the plan: is this a values signal, or just unfamiliar?
1You open a carton of milk that went sour last week.
2You and a friend win $20 for a project you did together, and she offers you $2 of it.
3Someone at a crowded bus stop cuts to the front just as the doors open.
4A sandwich you’ve never tried: peanut butter and pickles.

What you’ll probably notice. The milk and the unfair split often get a similar reaction: a wrinkled nose, a lifted lip, a tight throat. That overlap is The Science above, moral disgust borrowing the food-rejection reflex. The sandwich often gets a small yuck too, even though nobody did anything wrong, and that’s the misfire the plan is built to catch.

Step by step

Gut check before you act

For the moment something someone did turns your stomach.

  1. Pause and name it. Before you type or say anything, name the feeling (disgust) and notice where it sits: throat, stomach, face or chest.
  2. Ask what line it noticed. A lie, cruelty, something unfair? Or is it just unfamiliar, someone being different from you? Disgust can fire at both.
  3. Check what they actually did. Is it a pattern? Look at their choices and how they treat other people, more than at how they come across.
  4. Write one line before you act. The time, what happened and where you felt it. It takes ten seconds and puts a little space between the hit and your reply.
  5. Act on what you checked, then rest. If it’s real, step back with clarity: don’t share it, say one calm thing privately, or tell an adult you trust. If it was just unfamiliar, let it go. Either way, do something quiet afterward instead of scrolling the reactions.

How to tell it’s working. Keep your one-line notes for a week. Count how often the yuck matched something someone actually did and how often it was just unfamiliar, and how many of your replies you’d still stand behind the next day.

When it gets hard. The pull to reply is strongest while everyone else is reacting. If you can’t even write the line yet, put the phone face down for one minute first. The chat will still be there.

Good to know. If someone is being targeted, threatened or hurt, online or in person, tell a parent, a teacher or another adult you trust instead of handling it alone.
