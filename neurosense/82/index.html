Why Too Many Inputs Split Your Focus

The Claustrum

When your mind feels pulled in five directions, the problem may not be effort. It may be coordination.

The Science

  • Crick and Koch (2005), Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B: the claustrum connects to almost every cortical region and may help bind experience together.
  • It is not a magic consciousness switch; it is a reminder that experience has to be stitched into one moment.
  • Sight, sound, touch, and attention compete for that moment, so too many inputs make it feel scattered.
  • When your mind feels pulled five ways, the issue may be coordination, not effort.

The Protocol

  • One sense plus one anchor: pick one steady sound.
  • Pick one still visual point and hold both for thirty seconds.
  • If your mind wanders, return to the two anchors.
  • Notice the signal, name the mechanism, and change one input before autopilot.

See it in real life

Example

The vent hum in round five

Two boards over, a little kid is kicking the table leg, and Talia feels each thump through her own table. Round five is the last of the state scholastic chess tournament, and a win means the top-three trophy she’s chased for two years.

Her position is good if she can see far enough into it. Clocks slap all over the hotel ballroom, pairings crackle over the microphone, her opponent sniffs every few seconds, and her eyes keep sliding to the trophy table. Four times she has started the same calculation and lost it around the third move. Her clock is down to 6:40.

Every moment has to be stitched together from sight, sound, touch and attention, all competing for the same now. Scientists have wondered whether the claustrum, a thin sheet of neurons wired to almost every part of the cortex, helps with that stitching, and it’s still an open question. Either way, more inputs make a moment feel scattered. Talia’s problem may be coordination, not effort.

She stops calculating. Overhead, the air vents have hummed one steady note all day, and she picks that sound. For a still point she picks a chip in the table’s edge, just past her scoresheet. She holds both for thirty seconds and adds nothing. Twice her mind jumps to the thumping, and twice she comes back to the hum and the chip.

When she looks back at the board, the line goes all the way through: her rook takes on f7, and if his king takes back, her knight lands on e5 with check, attacking his queen too. She plays it with 5:52 left. Eight moves later her opponent stops both clocks and holds out his hand.

  1. Where it stallsThe same calculation started four times, under seven minutes left
  2. What changes itOne steady vent hum, one chip in the table, thirty seconds
  3. What happens nextThe knight check lands, and her opponent stops the clocks

Try it yourself · 2 minutes

Crowded round, anchored round

Do this somewhere with a few things going on, like a kitchen, a bus or a living room with the TV on low. You need a timer that beeps, a pencil and some paper.

  1. Set a thirty-second timer and keep your eyes on one still point, like a doorknob, while everything else keeps going: the TV, the talking, your phone face up. Make a tally mark each time your mind leaves the point.
  2. Now turn off what you can: the TV, the music, the phone screen. Pick one steady sound that’s left, like a fan, the fridge or traffic.
  3. Thirty seconds again, with the same still point plus the steady sound and no other task. Each time you drift, make a tally mark and come back to the two anchors.
  4. Compare the two tallies, and how crowded each round felt.

What you’ll probably notice. The crowded round often collects more tally marks, and the anchored one feels roomier even when the counts are close. Every tally mark is also a return, and returning without forcing it is the actual practice. Fewer streams means less competition for the same moment, which is the idea in The Science above.

Step by step

Two anchors when it gets crowded

Use it when a busy room is splitting your attention right before something that needs one clear moment.

  1. Notice the split. Rereading the same line or hearing everything at once. That’s the cue to stop pushing for a moment.
  2. Pick one steady sound. Something that doesn’t change, like a fan or rain. Skip music with words and the conversation next to you.
  3. Pick one still point. A spot that won’t move, like a scuff on the floor or a light switch. Not a screen.
  4. Hold both for thirty seconds. No planning, no checking, nothing added. You don’t need to count exactly; about half a minute is enough.
  5. Come back when you drift. You probably will, more than once. Return to the sound and the point without scolding yourself, then go back to the task.

How to tell it’s working. In the few minutes before the anchors and the few minutes after, count how many times you restart the same line, problem or sentence. If the after count tends to be lower, keep using it.

When it gets hard. When a clock is running, thirty seconds feels too expensive. It usually costs less than the minutes lost starting the same thought over.
