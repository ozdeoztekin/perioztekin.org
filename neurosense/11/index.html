Your Brain Is a Biological Battery

The Resting Potential

Move just 1 in 100,000 ions across the membrane and you generate -70 mV. The resting potential is built on the tiniest possible imbalance.

The Science

  • Hodgkin & Katz (J Physiol, 1949): the resting membrane potential of a neuron arises from differential ion permeability across the membrane. At rest, the membrane is most permeable to K+, which leaks out down its concentration gradient, leaving the interior slightly negative at approximately -70 mV.
  • The voltage difference is generated by a tiny charge imbalance: only about 1 in 100,000 ions need to move across the membrane to produce the resting voltage. The bulk concentrations on each side are essentially unchanged.
  • Goldman (J Gen Physiol, 1943): the resting potential is quantitatively predicted by the Goldman-Hodgkin-Katz equation, which integrates the permeabilities and concentration gradients of Na+, K+, and Cl-. Resting potential is a permeability-weighted average of the equilibrium potentials for each ion.
  • Different neuron types have different resting potentials (-40 to -90 mV) because they express different mixes of leak channels. The resting state is not a single number but a tuned parameter that shapes excitability.

The Protocol

  • Potassium-rich foods (bananas, leafy greens, beans, potatoes), K+ is the dominant ion at rest.
  • Magnesium (pumpkin seeds, spinach, almonds) supports the ATPase that maintains the gradient.
  • Hydration matters, every ion concentration is per liter of water.

See it in real life

Example

One grain through a paper door

Seventeen people have liked the comment under Naomi’s new video. She makes stop-motion science clips from paper cutouts, and part one of her neuron series shows a resting neuron as a little battery: the inside crowded with paper minus signs, the outside with plus signs.

The comment is polite, which makes it worse: “Love the paper ions, but almost nothing inside a resting neuron is unbalanced.” Naomi reads it on the bus, watches her own clip four times, and deletes three replies. Part two is due Friday.

The commenter is right. At rest, potassium crosses a neuron’s membrane more easily than any other ion does, through channels that stay open. Potassium leaks out, each ion carrying a plus charge, and that leaves the inside slightly negative, about minus 70 millivolts. Only about one ion in 100,000 has to cross, and the amounts inside and outside barely change. Minus 70 isn’t universal, either. Different neurons have different mixes of leak channels, so they rest anywhere from about minus 40 to minus 90, and where a neuron rests shapes how easily it fires.

Naomi starts over. She pours a whole bag of rice inside the paper ring that stands for the membrane, every grain an ion, and cuts a door in the ring marked K+. Over 40 frames, one grain slides out through the door while a caption fades in: “The real ratio is about one in 100,000. That’s the whole minus 70.” Her last shot puts three paper neurons side by side, labeled minus 40, minus 70 and minus 90.

The new version goes up Thursday night, 41 seconds long. By morning the commenter has replied, “That’s it exactly,” and Naomi pins the reply to the top.

Try it yourself · 2 minutes

The balloon test

A tiny imbalance of charge can do real work, and you can watch it happen. You need a balloon or a plastic comb, a tissue, and dry hair or a wool sweater.

  1. Tear the tissue into a small pile of bits about the size of rice grains, and spread them on a table.
  2. Hold the balloon a finger’s width above the bits. Nothing happens.
  3. Rub the balloon on your hair or the sweater for about ten seconds.
  4. Hold it above the bits again. Then look closely at the balloon: can you see anything that moved?

What you’ll probably notice. The bits jump up and cling, even though nothing visible changed on the balloon. Rubbing moved a tiny amount of charge from one surface to the other, and that small imbalance was enough for a real pull. A resting neuron runs on the same idea: about one ion in 100,000 crosses the membrane, everything else stays where it was, and that sliver is the whole minus 70 millivolts.

Step by step

Make minus 70 stick

About ten minutes today, two minutes tomorrow.

  1. Keep the rice picture. A full bag of rice with a single grain outside it. Sketch it in your notes and write “one in 100,000” and “minus 70” next to it.
  2. Name the open door. Write one sentence: at rest, potassium leaks out through channels that stay open, and each ion takes a plus charge with it, so the inside goes negative.
  3. Record a 20-second version. Say it in three sentences: what leaks out, how little it takes, and what that does to the inside. Record it as a voice memo and listen once.
  4. Add the range. Write minus 40 and minus 90 on either side of minus 70. Different neurons have different mixes of leak channels, and where a neuron rests changes how easily it fires.
  5. Say it again tomorrow. Before you open your notes or the memo, say the three sentences out loud. Whatever you leave out is what to review.

How to tell it’s working. Tomorrow, with nothing in front of you, explain in about 30 seconds why the inside is negative, how few ions it takes, and why minus 70 isn’t the same for every neuron. Then play yesterday’s memo and listen for anything you dropped.

When it gets hard. If one in 100,000 won’t stick, turn it into time: it’s one second out of about 28 hours.
