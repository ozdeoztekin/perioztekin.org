Change the Cost That Is Actually Stopping You

Effort Costs & Task Motivation

Motivation is not one setting: each action is weighed by whether it seems worth what it will demand.

The Science

  • The brain weighs possible outcomes against expected costs, and physical, thinking and social effort can carry very different prices.
  • Signals about outcomes and effort come from several brain systems and interact with circuits linking the frontal lobes and basal ganglia to action.
  • Past experience enters the comparison too: a previous struggle can make the next attempt seem costly before you have begun.

The Protocol

  • Name the exact activity you keep postponing and the outcome you actually value, instead of calling yourself unmotivated.
  • Identify the main expected cost, such as exertion, confusion or embarrassment, then change an avoidable part of it, not the goal.
  • Try the revised plan and check what actually happened, rather than judging your motivation as a whole.

See it in real life

Example

The gym bag he kept unpacking

Duc has packed his gym bag for open gym at the recreation center nine times since school started, and he has gone once. Junior varsity tryouts are in three weeks, and the coach wants everyone playing pickup games before then.

He doesn’t mind hard work. Most afternoons he runs sprints in the driveway and shoots until he can’t see the rim, but on open gym nights he unpacks the bag and calls himself lazy. The one time he went, in September, a dozen older guys were waiting along the wall for the next game. He waited forty minutes, got picked last, airballed his first shot and never saw the ball again.

The brain weighs what an action might bring against what it looks likely to cost, and physical, thinking and social effort can carry very different prices. Those signals come from several brain systems and feed into circuits linking the frontal lobes and basal ganglia to action. Sprinting costs Duc little, and standing on that wall in front of strangers costs a lot. September counts too, since one rough night can make the next try look costly before he leaves the house, though it doesn’t prove he can’t handle it now.

On Saturday he writes on an index card what he keeps putting off, open gym, and what he wants from it, pickup games before tryouts. The cost is the wall, waiting to be picked in front of a crowd. So he keeps the goal and changes the crowd. The front desk tells him Sunday mornings are usually quiet, with a few people and one half-court game.

On Sunday there are seven people. A man in a knee brace asks if he wants to run, and Duc plays three-on-three for most of the morning, until a kids’ class needs the court. He turns the card over, writes eight games, no waiting, and tucks it into his bag for next week.

  1. Where it stallsHe packs his bag for open gym and keeps unpacking it.
  2. What changes itHe names the crowd as the cost and goes on a quiet Sunday morning.
  3. What happens nextHe plays eight games of three-on-three with no waiting on the wall.

Try it yourself · 2 minutes

Same jumping jacks, new price

All this takes is a little floor space and the two sliders below. If jumping jacks aren’t comfortable for you, any easy movement works the same way.

  1. On the first slider, rate how willing you’d be to do twenty jumping jacks right now, alone where you are.
  2. On the second slider, rate the same twenty jumping jacks in a crowded school hallway between classes, with people walking past.
  3. Think about that hallway again with a friend doing them next to you, and notice where your number would land this time.
  4. Then do the twenty jumping jacks where you are, so you know what they cost your body.

The timer needs JavaScript. A phone stopwatch works just as well.

What you’ll probably notice. Your legs pay the same price in every version, yet the two numbers often come out far apart. The brain weighs what an action might bring against what it seems likely to cost, and a social cost like being watched can weigh very differently from the physical one. The version with a friend changes one avoidable part of that cost and leaves the exercise exactly as it was.

Step by step

Change the price, keep the goal

Pick one thing that keeps losing out, like a practice, a phone call or a club you meant to join.

  1. Name the exact thing you postpone. Write the specific activity, like “Tuesday open gym” or “calling about the summer job,” rather than a label for yourself like lazy or unmotivated.
  2. Write what you want out of it. Put down the outcome you care about, like pickup games before tryouts. That part stays fixed.
  3. Find the main cost. Ask which price is stopping you, whether it’s tiring your body, confusion about how to start, being watched or something else. A rough time before can raise the price you expect, even though it doesn’t prove you can’t do it now.
  4. Change the part you can change. Adjust when, where, how or with whom, like a quieter hour or a familiar place, while the goal stays the same.
  5. Try it and write down what happened. After the revised attempt, note whether you did it and what it cost you. If the barrier is still there, your first guess may have missed a cost, so look again before you judge your motivation as a whole.

How to tell it’s working. Keep a short note for each planned attempt, whether you did it and what got in the way. If the revised plan gets done more often than the old one, keep it, and if the same barrier keeps showing up, look for a cost you haven’t named yet.

When it gets hard. It’s tempting to change the goal instead of the cost, like deciding you don’t care about the team after all. Keep the goal written on the card and change only how you get there.
