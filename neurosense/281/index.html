Make the Movement Error Visible

Movement Feedback & Targeted Correction

A final score may not show which detail of a movement needs changing. Useful feedback can make it visible.

The Science

  • A movement gives you several kinds of information: what your body feels, what you can see and what happens at the end. They are not interchangeable.
  • Motor learning uses sensory feedback to refine coordination. The cerebellum contributes, helping movements arrive where and when you intend.
  • A beginner may not yet distinguish the key feature: two attempts can feel alike even though one changed arm position, acceleration or step timing.

The Protocol

  • Pick one feature, ideally from a coach or reliable instruction, such as where your hand enters the water relative to your head.
  • Record a short, safe attempt from an angle that shows that feature, with the equipment kept out of the movement.
  • Make one adjustment, record again and compare the same feature, rather than speed in one attempt and posture in the next.

See it in real life

Example

Where the right hand goes in

For weeks Anders’s 50 freestyle has been stuck within a tenth of the same time. His coach keeps telling him his right hand goes into the water right next to his head, and Anders doesn’t believe it, since every stroke feels like a full reach. So he answers the clock the only way he knows, by swimming harder, and the clock doesn’t move.

A swim gives him several kinds of information, and they aren’t interchangeable. There’s what his arm feels, what someone on the deck can see and the time at the end, which squeezes a whole swim into a single number and can’t show where his hand enters. Learning a movement relies on sensory feedback to refine it, with the cerebellum helping movements arrive where and when you intend. But he may not feel the detail that matters yet, and strokes can feel alike even when a hand goes in somewhere else.

Since his arm can’t tell him, he decides to look. After Thursday’s practice, once the others have left the pool, his coach says he can film himself, as long as he’s alone in the shot and the phone stays on the bleachers. They pick one thing to watch, where his right hand enters compared with his head, and he props the phone on the bottom bleacher, facing the middle of the lane, and swims a length at race pace.

Wrapped in a towel, he watches the clip. His left hand reaches well out in front, but seven of his eight right-hand strokes go in beside his goggles. He makes the change his coach asked for, reaching farther forward with his right arm, and films another length from the same spot.

This length feels exactly like the last one. On the screen, his right hand goes in ahead of his head on six of eight strokes. He doesn’t check the clock. His coach watches both clips side by side and says, “Same spot on Monday.”

  1. Where it stallsSwimming harder leaves his 50 freestyle stuck within the same tenth.
  2. What changes itHe films one length to see where his right hand goes in.
  3. What happens nextIn the second clip, six of eight strokes go in ahead of his head.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

Level arms, eyes closed

All it takes is your phone, something to prop it on at about chest height and enough room to stretch your arms out.

  1. Prop the phone three or four steps away, facing you, and start recording.
  2. Close your eyes and raise both arms out to the sides until they feel level with your shoulders, like a T. Hold them there for a slow count of three, then lower them.
  3. Stop the recording and look at one thing only, whether your two hands are at the same height.
  4. If one hand is lower, plan one change, like lifting that arm a little past where it feels level. Record again with your eyes closed and compare the same thing in both clips.

What you’ll probably notice. What feels level may not be, and the second try can feel exactly like the first even when the clip shows that it changed. What your arms feel and what a camera sees are different kinds of information, and only the camera shows your hands from the outside. Checking the same feature in both clips is how you find out whether the change you planned happened.

Step by step

One feature on camera

For a movement that has stopped improving, in the pool, on a court or in a dance class. It’s a simple way to use feedback, not a tested training program.

  1. Pick one feature with your coach. Ask your coach, or use reliable instructions, to choose one detail worth checking, such as where your hand enters the water compared with your head. Decide what you’re looking for before you film anything.
  2. Get permission and film only yourself. Ask the coach or the lifeguard first, keep other people out of the shot, and leave the phone on the deck or the sideline, away from the water and out of anyone’s way.
  3. Record a short attempt from the right angle. Set the phone where it can see that one feature, such as from the side for a hand entry, and record one short attempt.
  4. Watch only that feature. Count it, like how many of eight strokes go in beside your head, and leave everything else for another day.
  5. Change one thing and record again. Make the one adjustment your coach suggested, film from the same spot and compare the same feature, not your time.

How to tell it’s working. Write down the count from each clip. Compare clips of the same feature from the same angle, and don’t switch to judging speed one day and posture the next. It gives your practice something concrete to check, though it can’t promise faster progress.

When it gets hard. Once you watch yourself, you’ll probably spot five other things you dislike. Save them for your coach and stay with the one feature for this session.

Good to know. Only film where filming is allowed, and only yourself. At a pool, keep the phone well back from the edge, and never take it into the water.
