Why Brain Fog Is Actually Your Brain Being Dirty

The Glymphatic System

Pull an all-nighter and you're not just tired; your brain also misses its nightly cleanup.

The Science

  • Xie et al. (Science, 2013): in mice, the space between brain cells grew by about 60% during sleep, letting cerebrospinal fluid flow through and clear waste faster, including beta-amyloid, the protein linked to Alzheimer's disease.
  • Iliff et al. (Science Translational Medicine, 2012): in mice, cerebrospinal fluid flows into the brain along the outside of blood vessels and carries waste out, a route the team named the glymphatic system; Xie and colleagues then showed it runs much faster during sleep.
  • Lee et al. (Journal of Neuroscience, 2015): in anesthetized rats, brain waste clearance was more efficient when the animals lay on their side than on their back or belly; whether this holds for sleeping people is not known.

The Protocol

  • If you like, start the night on your side; in rats, lying on the side cleared brain waste best, though this has not been tested in sleeping people.
  • Keep the bedroom cool, around 65°F (18°C); body temperature naturally dips at night, and a cool room makes sleep easier.
  • Stop caffeine about eight hours before bed; it takes about five hours for the body to clear half of it.
  • Aim for 8 to 10 hours, the range sleep experts recommend for teenagers.

See it in real life

Example

Fair week in a hot camper

Camila’s club leader is playing judge in Thursday’s practice round, and when he asks what Pippa weighs, the number is gone. She weighed her goat on Monday and knew it by heart. While she searches for it, Pippa’s back legs drift out of position. Showmanship, the class she has been working toward all year, is on Saturday.

It’s fair week, and her family is staying in the camper behind the barns. She’s up at 5:30 every morning to feed and wash Pippa, out on the midway with her friends until after eleven, and asleep on her back on a bunk that holds the day’s heat. She has sweet tea from the lemonade stand with dinner, and she has slept about six hours a night for four nights in a row.

Sleep is when the brain does its cleaning. In mice, the spaces between brain cells widen during sleep, and fluid rinses through them, clearing out waste left over from the day. Sleep also helps the day’s learning settle in. A hot bunk and an evening of sweet tea both make deep sleep harder to get, and six hours is short to begin with, since teenagers need about eight to ten.

That night she changes what she can. She leaves the midway at nine, props the camper window open and clips a battery fan to the bunk. She lies on her side with a rolled towel behind her back to keep her there. In one study, rats cleared brain waste best lying on their side, and nobody knows yet whether that holds for people, but it’s easy to try. On Friday she stops the sweet tea after lunch.

On Saturday the judge walks down the line and asks Camila how much Pippa weighs. “A hundred and twelve pounds,” she says. She has answers for breed, age and feed too, and Pippa’s feet stay square the whole time. They take second in their class.

  1. Where it stallsAfter four hot, short nights, her goat’s weight goes blank.
  2. What changes itShe cuts the sweet tea after lunch, adds a fan and turns in early.
  3. What happens nextIn the ring she has every answer, and they take second.

Try it yourself · 2 minutes

The open-hand rinse

This one happens at the sink and models what changes in deep sleep. You’ll need a pinch of dried oregano or another dried herb and running water.

  1. Cup one hand under a slow stream from the tap, with your fingers pressed tightly together, so your palm holds a small pool.
  2. Sprinkle the herbs into the pool. They stand for the waste a busy day leaves behind.
  3. Keep the water running for ten seconds with your fingers still tight, and watch the flakes.
  4. Now spread your fingers a little to leave gaps between them, and keep the water running.

What you’ll probably notice. With your fingers tight, the flakes mostly swirl in place, and only a few escape over the edge. Once you open the gaps, the water runs through them and carries the flakes away in a few seconds. Your fingers are the brain cells, and the tap is the fluid that rinses between them. In mice, those spaces widen during sleep, which gives the rinse room to work.

Step by step

Set the room for deep sleep

Start with whichever one is easiest tonight.

  1. Count back eight to ten hours. Start from your wake-up time and pick a lights-out time that leaves at least eight hours before the alarm, and up to ten if you can.
  2. Stop caffeine eight hours before bed. For a 10:30 bedtime, that means nothing with caffeine after 2:30 in the afternoon. Soda, iced tea, coffee and energy drinks all count, because caffeine lasts for hours.
  3. Cool the room down. Aim for about 65°F (18°C), since a cool room makes it easier to sleep. A fan, an open window or a lighter blanket gets you most of the way there.
  4. Try sleeping on your side. In a study of rats, lying on the side cleared brain waste best. Nobody knows yet if that’s true for people, but it’s easy to try, and a pillow behind you helps you stay there.

How to tell it’s working. Each morning for a week, write down your lights-out time, your wake-up time and how clear your head feels by midmorning, from 0 to 10. Then compare the nights with eight hours or more in a cool room to the nights without.

When it gets hard. You’ll probably wake up on your back anyway, because everyone shifts around at night. Roll back over if you notice, and let the pillow do the rest.

Good to know. If lying awake for an hour or more happens a lot, or you still wake up worn out after full nights, tell a parent. A doctor can help figure out why.
