Ask Why the Number Is Not Lower

Self-Persuasion & Personal Reasons

Your best argument may not be their reason; one question can help someone voice a reason of their own.

The Science

  • When someone lacks motivation, your most convincing reason may not be theirs, so more explanation can leave that mismatch untouched.
  • Self-persuasion starts from reasons people generate themselves: they select what matters to them, rather than only hearing what matters to you.
  • Someone who rates raking leaves a three, asked why it is not a one, may name a reason of their own, such as enjoying moving outdoors.

The Protocol

  • If someone is willing, ask how motivated they feel from zero to ten, and treat the number as their honest starting point.
  • If the number is above zero, ask "Why is it not lower?" and listen to their reason before adding any of your own.
  • Reflect their reason accurately, then ask what they want to do next, leaving the choice, even no action, with them.

See it in real life

Example

A three, not a one

Katya and Alma have tutored third graders at the library’s after-school program since September. On Monday Alma texted that this week would be her last, and since then Katya has sent every reason she can think of, that the program is short of tutors, that the kids are used to them and that it counts toward the service award. Alma’s answers have shrunk to “I know” and then a thumbs-up.

Katya’s reasons are good ones, but they’re hers. When someone isn’t motivated, your most convincing argument may not be what matters to them, and more explaining can leave that gap untouched. Self-persuasion starts from the reasons a person comes up with on their own, because they pick what matters to them rather than only hearing what matters to you.

Stacking chairs after the kids leave on Thursday, Katya stops arguing and asks whether she can ask one question. Alma shrugs and says sure. “From zero to ten, how much do you want to keep coming?” “Like a three,” Alma says. If she had said zero, Katya had already decided to let it go. Instead she asks, “Why a three and not a one?”

The question points Alma at whatever already makes the job worth doing, and she takes longer to answer than Katya expects. Then she talks about the boy who couldn’t get his sevens for a month, and how last week he got the whole row and slapped the table. “So the part you like is the moment someone gets it,” Katya says. “Yeah,” says Alma. “Not chasing kids around the room.” Katya asks what she wants to do next and leaves it there.

Before they go, Alma asks the program leader whether she could work one-on-one at the corner table instead of roaming. The next Tuesday she’s there with the boy and a stack of flash cards, starting on the eights.

  1. Where it stallsKatya’s reasons to stay only get shorter replies from Alma.
  2. What changes itShe asks for a number from zero to ten, then why not lower.
  3. What happens nextAlma names her own reason and asks to tutor one-on-one.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

Your reasons and theirs

Choose one ordinary thing you’ve been putting off, like a chore, a practice or a message you owe someone. All you need is a page folded in half.

  1. On the left half, write the reasons other people have given you for doing it.
  2. Rate how much you want to do it, from zero to ten. Zero is an honest answer, and if that’s yours, you can stop here.
  3. If it’s above zero, ask yourself why it isn’t lower, and write that reason on the right half in your own words.
  4. Read both halves and circle the reason that pulls on you most.

What you’ll probably notice. The reason on the right is often smaller than the ones on the left, and for many people it’s the one they circle. Asking why the number isn’t lower points your attention at whatever already makes the task worth considering, and a reason you put into words yourself is one you picked as yours. The task hasn’t changed, but a reason of your own is now on the page.

Step by step

Their number, their reason

For a friend, teammate or family member who’s willing to talk about an ordinary task they don’t feel like doing.

  1. Ask first, and mean it. Check that they want to talk about it. If they say no, the conversation is over.
  2. Ask for a number from zero to ten. Ask how motivated they feel about the task, and treat the number as their honest starting point.
  3. Then ask why it isn’t lower. If the number is above zero, ask why it isn’t lower and listen. Keep your own reasons to yourself until they’ve finished.
  4. Say their reason back accurately. Use their words, like “So it’s the part with people you like,” without turning it into a sales pitch.
  5. Leave the next step with them. Ask what they want to do next. They may choose an action, a different approach or nothing, and a zero gets the same respect as a seven.

How to tell it’s working. Afterward, notice whose reasons filled the conversation. If they did most of the talking and named a reason you hadn’t thought of, the question did its part, whatever they decide.

When it gets hard. When you care about the outcome, the pull to add your own reasons or nudge a low number higher is strong. Ask once, listen, and let a zero stand.

Good to know. This is an ordinary conversation between two people who both want to have it, not counseling. A no or a zero ends it, and the choice stays with them.
