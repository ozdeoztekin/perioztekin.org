How Certain Is “Almost Certain”?

Overconfidence & Confidence Calibration

You can feel 98 percent sure and still miss several of the answers your ranges were meant to catch.

The Science

  • Overconfidence is certainty running ahead of accuracy, and it is a normal part of how most people decide.
  • If each range truly deserves 98 percent confidence, you would expect around nine or ten hits out of ten, though a set of ten can vary by chance.
  • One miss proves little, since it could be bad luck, but repeated misses across a set can signal that your ranges are too narrow.

The Protocol

  • Pick ten things you only half know, like the height of the Leaning Tower of Pisa or the year of the first Nobel Peace Prize.
  • For each, write a low and a high bound you are 98 percent sure surround the true value, then count the hits.
  • If fewer than nine catch the answer, take it as a warning to reconsider how narrow your next ranges should be.

See it in real life

Example

Four of ten in the estimation round

The last round of the library’s monthly teen trivia night is the estimation round, and it belongs to Clio. For each of ten questions nobody could know exactly, her team writes a low number and a high number, and a range scores a point if the true answer lands inside it, within the round’s limit on how wide a range can be.

Last month she filled in every range in a few minutes, sure of all of them. Four caught the answer, the team lost by two points, and on the walk home her teammate Femi asked how she could be that sure about the weight of a blue whale.

Feeling sure doesn’t make a range right, because certainty can run ahead of accuracy. That gap is called overconfidence, and it’s a normal part of how most people decide. If each range deserved the 98 percent certainty she felt, nine or ten of them should have caught the answer, though a set that small can vary by chance. A single miss proves little, but misses that keep repeating can mean the ranges are too narrow.

That Sunday she tests the feeling. At the kitchen table she writes ranges for ten things she only half knows, like the height of the Leaning Tower of Pisa and the year of the first Nobel Peace Prize, each wide enough that she feels 98 percent sure. When she looks them up, five catch the answer. That’s a warning, though not a verdict, so Femi writes her a new set, and she gets six. Twice in a row is enough, so on the next set she pushes every range wider until it feels a little silly, and nine catch.

At the next trivia night her ranges look wide on the answer sheet, and Femi raises an eyebrow but doesn’t argue. Eight catch the answer, and the team wins by a point.

  1. Where it stallsShe’s sure of all ten ranges, and only four catch the answer.
  2. What changes itShe counts hits on practice sets and widens her ranges after repeated misses.
  3. What happens nextEight of her ranges catch the answer, and the team wins.

Try it yourself · 10 minutes

Ten ranges, one count

Keep the answers covered until the end, and don’t look anything up. Something to write on is all it takes.

  1. For each question, write a low and a high number you’re 98 percent sure surround the true value, sure enough that you’d expect to miss about once in fifty.
  2. Answer all ten, even the ones you know nothing about, since a wide range is allowed.
  3. Uncover the answers and count how many of your ranges caught the true value.
  4. Write down the count, along with how sure you felt while you were writing.
1 to 5(1) The year of the first Nobel Peace Prize · (2) The height of the Leaning Tower of Pisa, in meters · (3) The distance from Earth to the Moon, in kilometers · (4) The number of floors in the Empire State Building · (5) The year of the first modern Olympic Games
6 to 10(6) The number of countries that border Germany · (7) The length of the Golden Gate Bridge’s main span, in meters · (8) The year the Titanic sank · (9) The number of time zones in Russia · (10) The year the Eiffel Tower was finished
Answers(1) 1901 · (2) about 57 · (3) about 384,400 · (4) 102 · (5) 1896 · (6) 9 · (7) 1,280 · (8) 1912 · (9) 11 · (10) 1889

Cover the answers until you’ve written all ten ranges.

What you’ll probably notice. If each range deserved 98 percent confidence, nine or ten should catch the answer, though ten questions can vary by chance. If several missed while you felt sure, that’s certainty running ahead of accuracy, which is overconfidence. A single miss proves little, but a handful is a warning that your ranges run too narrow, so try a new set with wider ones before you decide anything about yourself.

Step by step

Let the count set your ranges

Run it a few times over a week, with a fresh set each time.

  1. Pick ten things you half know. Choose quantities you can look up afterward, like a height, a distance, a year or a population, and don’t look until you’ve finished.
  2. Write 98 percent ranges. For each one, write a low and a high bound you’re 98 percent sure surround the true value.
  3. Look them up and count. Count how many ranges caught the answer. Ranges that deserve 98 percent should catch about nine or ten.
  4. Treat fewer than nine as a warning. One set of ten can be unlucky, so it isn’t a verdict about you. If several missed, plan to make your next ranges wider.
  5. Run a fresh set and compare. A few days later, try ten new quantities with wider ranges, and put the two counts side by side.

How to tell it’s working. Keep the count from every set of ten, with a word about how sure you felt. If the hits climb toward nine or ten as your ranges widen, your confidence and your accuracy are moving closer together.

When it gets hard. Wide ranges can feel like admitting you don’t know much. For questions like these you don’t, and a range that shows it is more useful than a narrow one that misses.
