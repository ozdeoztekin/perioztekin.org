Let Attraction Gather More Than a First Impression

First Impressions & Getting Acquainted

First impressions often lean on the same visible cues; getting acquainted adds details others never witnessed.

The Science

  • Early on, people often see many of the same cues, so with little shared history their judgments can lean on the same visible qualities.
  • Getting acquainted changes the information: you might see someone handle a mistake or listen closely in a way others never witnessed.
  • The direction is not guaranteed: more information can increase attraction, reduce it, or clarify that the connection is better as friendship.

The Protocol

  • Choose a recurring group activity you actually want to attend, such as a class, a club, or a regular shared project.
  • Show up on repeated occasions, have ordinary conversations, and notice how people respond across situations.
  • Let your view move in either direction, and do not keep pursuing someone who is not interested.

See it in real life

Example

Six Wednesdays at the yarn shop

Esteban is in his last year of high school and has gotten fast at sizing people up. At a party he knows within a minute who seems out of his league and who seems like nobody he’d click with, and his friends would sort the room the same way. Since September, none of those quick looks has turned into a second conversation.

In October he joined the Wednesday crochet circle at the yarn shop on Main Street, because he wanted help with his sweater. On his first night there, Lotte, who is a year older and at the community college, told him his stitches were too tight and went back to her own work. The whole table seemed to read her the same way, and so did he.

With little shared history, people notice the same visible things, like a loud laugh, clothes or a short answer, so early judgments often agree. Getting acquainted changes the information. You might see someone handle a mistake or listen closely in a way nobody else saw, and that becomes part of how you judge them. The direction isn’t guaranteed, since more information can make you more interested, less interested or sure you’d rather be friends.

So he holds that first read loosely and keeps going for the sweater, chatting with whoever sits nearby. In week three Lotte finds a mistake eleven rows back and pulls them all out, laughing at herself. In week four, when a nervous twelve-year-old’s yarn knots into a ball, she sits beside him and untangles it, asking about his dog. Isadora, whose big laugh everyone noticed first, becomes a friend he trades patterns with, and nothing more.

On the sixth Wednesday, while they pack up, he asks Lotte if she’d like hot chocolate after the next circle, and says it’s fine if not. She thinks about it and says she’d like that. A week later they leave together and take the window table at the café next door.

  1. Where it stallsHe sizes people up in a minute, and nothing gets past hello.
  2. What changes itHe keeps going to the crochet circle and notices more each week.
  3. What happens nextHe asks Lotte to hot chocolate, and she says she’d like that.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

First look, later look

Think of a friend you know well now but didn’t know at all a year or two ago. Something to write on is enough, and nobody else needs to see it.

  1. Write down what you noticed about them the first time you met, like how they dressed, how loud they were or how sure of themselves they seemed.
  2. Underneath, write three things you only learned by spending time with them, like how they handle a mistake, what they do when someone’s upset or what makes them laugh.
  3. Put a star next to anything on the second list that someone who met them once would never have seen.
  4. Compare the two lists, and ask which one tells you more about what they’re like to be around.

What you’ll probably notice. Usually the first list is short and made of things anyone could see across a room, and plenty of people would have noticed the same ones. The starred items come from your own history with that person, so your view of them can end up different from someone else’s. Time can move a view in either direction, too, and some of the people you got to know better became friends and nothing more.

Step by step

Give it more than one meeting

For anyone who decides about people in the first minute and stops there.

  1. Choose a group you’d go to anyway. Pick something that meets again and again, like a class, a club, a team or a shared project, and pick it for the activity itself.
  2. Keep showing up. Go on repeated occasions and have ordinary conversations, with no plan to impress anyone.
  3. Notice more than one situation. Pay attention to how people handle a mistake, a hard moment or someone new, rather than only how they came across on the first night.
  4. Let your view move either way. More time can make you more interested, less interested or sure you’d rather be friends, and each of those is worth knowing.
  5. Ask once, and let a no end it. If you’d like to know someone better, ask in a way that’s easy to turn down. If they’re not interested, stop asking and stay friendly in the group.

How to tell it’s working. After a few weeks, write down one thing you’ve learned about someone in the group that the first night couldn’t have shown you. If that list keeps growing, the repeated time is giving you information a first impression never had.

When it gets hard. It’s tempting to decide after one evening, either way. Give it several meetings before you put anyone in a box, and remember that the activity is worth going to on its own.

Good to know. This creates chances to get to know people, not a promise of a relationship on any schedule. Interest has to go both ways, so if someone says no or seems uncomfortable, that’s the answer, and you stop asking.
