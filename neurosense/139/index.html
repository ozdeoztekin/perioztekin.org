Future You Needs Evidence

Future Self & Identity

Future you can feel imaginary, so present comfort often wins the vote.

The Science

  • In a small brain-scan study, people whose brains treated their future self more like another person valued later rewards less.
  • In four experiments, people shown their own face aged by a computer were more willing to wait for later money.
  • Cognitive scientist Maya Shankar suggests identity may be built from what you keep doing and thinking.

The Protocol

  • Write one private note to your older self, not for applause.
  • Choose one action small enough to happen before the mood changes.
  • Anchor identity to your why, not only what you currently do.

See it in real life

Example

A note taped inside the shoebox lid

Grandpa Joe stopped driving in the spring, and his fishing rods have been leaning against the garage wall ever since. At dinner in April, Orion announced his plan to buy a used car by next spring, so their Sundays at the lake could start again with him driving.

He has mowed lawns all summer at $40 a yard, and the shoebox under his bed holds $85. Money comes in on Friday and is gone by Monday, spent on delivery fries, a game skin or a hoodie he wore twice. His sister calls it the leak, and the whole family laughs, Orion included.

Next spring is a long way from Friday night, and the brain marks down rewards that feel far away and blurry, so a someday car can’t compete with fries that arrive in twenty minutes. Identity runs on evidence too, because it’s partly built from what someone keeps doing. Every Monday told the same story about Orion, which made the old pattern easy for everyone to believe, including him.

One Tuesday night he writes a note to himself a year from now and shows it to no one. It’s three sentences about the lake, the rods in the trunk and the reason, which is that Grandpa taught him to cast off that dock and should get to watch him do it again. He tapes it inside the shoebox lid. Then he picks one move small enough to beat a bad mood. When he gets home from a lawn, $20 goes in the box before his shoes come off, and he writes the date under the note.

By October the lid has nine dates in a column and the box holds $265. Some weekends still leak. When his sister brings it up at dinner, Orion tells her to check the lid and keeps eating.

  1. Where it stallsAfter a whole summer of mowing, the shoebox holds $85.
  2. What changes itHe tapes a private note in the lid and puts in $20 before his shoes come off.
  3. What happens nextThe lid has nine dates, the box has $265, and he has a new answer at dinner.

Try it yourself · 10 minutes

The note and the next move

You’ll need paper, a pen and a private place to keep the note, like a drawer, a box or the back of a notebook.

  1. Write three sentences to yourself a year from now, about where you hope you are, what you’re working toward and the reason underneath it. Nobody else reads it.
  2. Under it, finish the sentence “I’m becoming someone who …”
  3. Pick one action so small you could do it in the next five minutes, like putting $5 aside, lacing your running shoes, sending the email or playing one scale, and do it now.
  4. On the back of the note, write today’s date and what you did. That’s your first line of evidence, and you add a line every time you repeat it.

What you’ll probably notice. The note tends to make the goal feel closer and more like yours, because now it has a place, a person and a reason in it. The action counts for more, though. A line of evidence is something future you can point to, and after a few lines, the “someone who” sentence starts to read less like a wish and more like a record.

Step by step

Leave evidence for future you

Pick one goal you keep putting off until later.

  1. Write one private note forward. Write three sentences to yourself a year from now about the place, the goal and the reason under it. Keep it where you’ll see it, and don’t post it.
  2. Choose a move smaller than a mood. Pick one action that takes a few minutes and comes right after something that already happens, like getting paid or walking in the door.
  3. Write the date every time. Add a date and a few words on the note, a calendar or a box lid, since that list is the evidence.
  4. Anchor it to the why. When plans change, like a lost job or a canceled program, look for the reason underneath and find a new move that serves it.
  5. Restart without a speech. If you miss a few days, do the small move at the next chance and add the line. People may know your old pattern, but it doesn’t lock you in.

How to tell it’s working. Count the lines at the end of each week. If the column keeps growing, even slowly, and the goal feels a little less like someday, you’re building the evidence.

When it gets hard. The move will feel too small to count, and that’s on purpose, because a move small enough for a bad day keeps the column growing on bad days too.
