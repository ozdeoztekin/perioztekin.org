Why Some People Notice What Others Miss

Sensory Processing Sensitivity

Sensory sensitivity can go with noticing faint signals in adults. A largely untested idea: noise gets in too.

The Science

  • People differ along a trait called sensory processing sensitivity. In adults, more of it can go with noticing faint images or sounds more easily.
  • One theory: the brain predicts what your senses will report and weights each mismatch. A largely untested idea: sensitive brains weight them all more.
  • Sensitivity can go with better memory during an undisturbed break, yet worse memory with interruptions; that link does not prove cause.

The Protocol

  • After studying, silence notification pings so the quiet stretch that follows is not broken by interruptions.
  • Give your brain a quiet stretch after learning, of whatever length your day allows, instead of jumping straight into messages.
  • Use this if busy, noisy days tend to drain you, and treat it as a change to your surroundings, not a diagnosis.

See it in real life

Example

The phone on the kitchen counter

The kitchen TV was fine at breakfast. When Mei gets home, her dad has it on at the same volume, and it’s so loud she takes her snack upstairs.

In September she switched to a school of 2,600, with bright hallways that roar between classes. The new chemistry class is two units ahead, so after dinner she studies the 24 polyatomic ions she missed, then answers sixty messages from her old friends and the new class chat until bed. Each morning on the bus she can write fewer than half the ions from memory.

People differ in how strongly a busy day stays with them, along a personality trait called sensory processing sensitivity. In one influential theory, the brain keeps predicting what the senses will report, and each mismatch, called a prediction error, gets a weight, so heavy ones travel deeper into processing and light ones fade. One largely untested idea is that a more sensitive brain weights every surprise heavily, so faint signals get through but so does noise, which keeps being processed until even the TV is too much. It may cut both ways, too, since sensitivity can go with better memory during an undisturbed break after learning and worse memory when the break gets interrupted, a link that doesn’t prove cause.

Quiet costs nothing, so on Wednesday she tries it. After studying, she puts her phone on Do Not Disturb, leaves it on the kitchen counter and lies on her bed with the lamp off for twenty minutes. Then she goes back down and answers the chat.

She does it again on Thursday, and on Friday morning, on the bus, she writes 20 of the 24 ions without looking. She can’t tell how much of that is the quiet and how much is a third night of studying, but she keeps the twenty minutes, and her old friends have learned she answers after nine.

  2. What changes itAfter studying she silences her phone and lies in the dark for twenty minutes.

Try it yourself · one day

Same volume, morning and night

You’ll need a TV, a speaker or a video you play often, plus somewhere to jot two numbers. Keep the volume at your usual level, never louder.

  1. In the morning, play something at your usual volume, write down the number and rate how loud it feels from 0 to 10.
  2. Go through your day, and jot down anything busy, bright or loud you run into.
  3. In the evening, after the busiest part of your day, play the same thing at the same number and rate it again.
  4. If you can, repeat it on a quiet weekend day and compare the two evenings.

What you’ll probably notice. Some people find the evening number jumps on a busy day, and others barely notice a change. Both are normal, since people sit at different points along this trait. In one largely untested idea, a brain that gives every surprise heavy weight keeps processing more of the day’s noise, so by evening the same volume can feel like a lot. A big jump is a reason to protect some quiet after studying, not a label for you.

Step by step

A quiet stretch after studying

Try it on the days that leave you drained.

  1. Notice which days drain you. Keep a note of the days that end with everything feeling too loud, and what they held, like crowds, bright lights or a long practice.
  2. Silence the pings when you finish. As soon as you stop studying, turn on Do Not Disturb and leave the phone in a different room.
  3. Give yourself a quiet stretch. Lie down with the light low, sit on the steps or walk around the block without earbuds, for whatever length your evening allows. Messages, feeds and new videos can wait.
  4. Then catch up all at once. Answer everything in one go afterward, so the quiet stretch stays unbroken and your friends still hear from you.
  5. Test yourself the next morning. Before you look at your notes, write down what you remember from the night before.

How to tell it’s working. After ten days or so, compare your next-morning recall after quiet evenings and after interrupted ones. If you see no difference, that’s worth knowing too, since the link between quiet and memory is a pattern, not a promise.

When it gets hard. The chat keeps going without you, and the first quiet evening can feel like missing out. Tell your friends ahead of time when you’ll be back on, so the silence doesn’t look like you’re ignoring them.

Good to know. Sensitivity to noise and light is a trait that varies from person to person, not a diagnosis. If overload often crowds out sleep, school or time with friends, bring it up with a parent, a doctor or a counselor at school.
