Why Bad News Has to Pass a Harder Test

Motivated Skepticism & Hot Cognition

The same test can feel less trustworthy when it gives the answer you did not want.

The Science

  • Motivated skepticism means doubting evidence that goes against what you want to believe, however strong that evidence is.
  • Hot cognition is thinking steered by desires and feelings: once you want a particular answer, you stop processing information coldly.
  • You hunt for flaws mostly in evidence you dislike and may still feel objective, which is how people often keep believing what they want.

The Protocol

  • When a result bothers you, ask whether you would trust the same test if it had given the answer you wanted.
  • Treat what you hope is true as a hypothesis, then look for evidence that could prove it wrong.
  • Hold welcome and unwelcome evidence to the same standard before you decide which answer to believe.

See it in real life

Example

The vest that scored both ways

With four seconds left in the final, the other girl throws a roundhouse kick, the scoreboard adds two points, and Rosalind loses by one. At this taekwondo tournament, sensors in the fighters’ socks register on electronic vests that do the scoring, and Rosalind is sure she blocked that kick with her arm.

She tells her mom on the drive home that the vests in that ring were too sensitive all day. She texts three teammates the same thing, and when her coach, Master Yoon, offers to go through his video of the match, she says there’s nothing to see.

That’s motivated skepticism, doubting evidence that goes against what you want to believe, however strong it is. It’s a form of hot cognition, thinking steered by wants and feelings, and once you want a particular answer, you stop weighing information coldly. You hunt for flaws mostly in the evidence you dislike, and you can still feel completely fair. That morning, in the semifinal, the same vests gave Rosalind two points for a kick she barely felt land, and she never doubted them.

By Monday night she’s calm enough to ask herself whether she would trust that vest if it had scored her kick, and it already had. So she treats what she hopes is true, that she blocked it, as a hypothesis, and goes looking for evidence that could prove it wrong. She asks Master Yoon for both clips and watches them the same way, at quarter speed, three times each.

In the final, her left arm drops as she pulls her own kick back, and the other girl’s foot lands under it. In the semifinal, her kick lands about as lightly as she remembered. On Tuesday she asks Master Yoon to count her arm drops while she kicks the pads, and it’s seven out of ten. By Friday it’s two.

  1. Where it stallsShe loses by one on a kick the vest scored, and blames the vest.
  2. What changes itShe asks if she’d trust the same vest if it had scored for her.
  3. What happens nextHer arm drops on seven of ten kicks, and by Friday on two.

Try it yourself · 5 minutes

The flaw hunt

Both results in the box are invented for this, and neither is a real finding. Keep something to write with and a one-minute timer handy.

  1. Decide which result would be bad news for you. If you usually study with music on, it’s A, and if you study in silence, it’s B.
  2. Set the timer for one minute and write down every reason the bad-news result might be wrong, like who the students were, which words they learned or what kind of music played.
  3. Set it again and do the same for the good-news result.
  4. Count the reasons on each list. Then check how many reasons from the first list would fit the second one too, since the study is identical.
Result AIn a study of 120 students, the ones who studied with music on remembered fewer words the next day than the ones who studied in silence.
Result BIn a study of 120 students, the ones who studied with music on remembered more words the next day than the ones who studied in silence.

The same made-up study with opposite answers.

What you’ll probably notice. The bad-news list often comes out longer and faster, even though both results describe the identical study. That’s motivated skepticism, with the hunt for flaws aimed mostly at the answer you don’t want, and from the inside it can feel like ordinary care. Putting both results through the same questions is what a fair trial looks like.

Step by step

Give the result a fair trial

Use it when a result bothers you, like a score, a ruling, a grade or a time on the board.

  1. Ask the fair-trial question. Would you trust this same test, judge or measurement if it had given the answer you wanted? If you would have, hold the answer you got to that same standard.
  2. Write down what you hope is true. Put it in one sentence, like “I blocked that kick” or “the timer started late,” and treat it as a hypothesis to test.
  3. Look for what could prove it wrong. Find the evidence that would show your hope is mistaken, like a video, a second measurement or a record, and look at that first.
  4. Check the result you liked the same way. Whatever you question about the bad result, ask about a good one from the same source too.
  5. Then decide what to work on. Once both sides have had the same standard, decide which answer to believe and what to do next.

How to tell it’s working. Each time, jot down the result that bothered you, your hope and what the evidence showed. After a few, look at whether you checked good and bad results the same way, and whether the checks ever changed your mind.

When it gets hard. It’s hard to look for proof that you’re wrong right after a loss. Waiting until the next day is fine, but set a time for it, so waiting doesn’t turn into never looking.
